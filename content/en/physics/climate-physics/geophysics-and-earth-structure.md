No instrument has ever been lowered into the mantle, let alone the core. Everything asserted about the interior below the crust — that there is a liquid layer, that a solid ball sits inside it, where each boundary lies — is an inference drawn from measurements made at or above the surface. Geophysics is the discipline of making those inferences defensible, and its methods deserve attention separately from its conclusions, because each conclusion is only as strong as what its method can distinguish.

## Two body waves, and the one that a liquid stops

An earthquake radiates elastic waves in two forms that travel through the body of the planet. The British Geological Survey's summary gives the mechanics of both. **P waves** are longitudinal: the material compresses and dilates along the direction of travel, exactly as a sound wave does. **S waves** are transverse, the motion perpendicular to the direction of propagation. The naming records the order they arrive in — P for primary, because they are faster.

The speeds are the useful part. P waves run at 5 to 7 km/s in typical crust and above 8 km/s in the mantle and core; S waves manage 3 to 4 km/s in crust and about 4.5 km/s in the mantle. That difference turns a single seismogram into a distance measurement: the gap between the P and S arrivals grows with path length, so three stations that each measure it locate the source where their three circles intersect. The underlying wave mechanics is treated generally in the article on [waves and oscillations](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/waves-and-oscillations-explained).

The decisive property is not a speed at all. A transverse wave needs a medium that resists shear, and a fluid does not, so S waves do not propagate through liquid — not through the ocean, not through magma, and not through a liquid layer inside the planet. A global network of seismometers therefore records, for a large enough earthquake, a region of the far side where the direct S arrival is simply absent. Direct P arrivals go missing over a band of distances too, then reappear beyond it, because the wave refracts sharply on crossing into a slower medium. The width and position of those absences are the measurement: they fix where the boundary lies and require that whatever sits below it will not carry a shear wave.

## The shells the arrivals imply

Assembling many such paths yields the layering that USGS summarises in *This Dynamic Earth*. The planet is about 12,750 km across. Crust beneath the oceans is thin and fairly uniform, generally about 5 km; continental crust averages roughly 30 km and reaches as much as 100 km under large mountain ranges. Beneath it lies the mantle, about 2,900 km thick, then a liquid outer core given as 2,200 km thick, and at the centre a solid inner core of about 1,250 km.

One further division cuts across that list, and it is mechanical rather than compositional; conflating the two kinds of boundary is the most common misreading of the diagram. The **lithosphere** — the rigid outer shell that is broken into the tectonic plates — is the crust plus the cooler, stiffer upper part of the mantle, averaging at least 80 km thick and thinnest under the oceans. It sits on the asthenosphere, mantle material of much the same composition that is hot enough to flow under sustained stress. Crust and mantle differ in what they are made of; lithosphere and asthenosphere differ in how they behave. The distinction matters whenever the [solid Earth is treated as one component](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-components-explained) of a larger system.

## The heat budget, and what it forces

A global compilation published in *Solid Earth*, drawing on 38,347 individual measurements — some 55 per cent more than earlier estimates had used — puts the total heat flux through Earth's surface at 47 ± 2 TW. Two sources supply it, according to the USGS account: the decay of naturally occurring uranium, thorium and potassium, and residual heat left from the gravitational energy of the planet's formation. Radiometric decay is the same process that underpins the dating discussed below, and its physics is set out in the article on [radioactivity and the units that measure it](/en/physics/matter-radiation/radioactivity-and-radiation-units).

Against the solar radiation that drives weather, that internal flux is negligible; it does essentially nothing to [the atmosphere's own energy budget](/en/physics/climate-physics/atmospheric-physics-explained). What it does drive is the interior. Heat has to escape, conduction through thousands of kilometres of rock is far too slow to carry it, and so the mantle convects — hot material rising, cooling, and sinking again over geological time, the same mechanism that operates in a heated fluid at any scale, as described in the treatment of [conduction, convection and radiation](/en/physics/thermodynamics/heat-transfer-conduction-convection-radiation).

That much is forced by the energy budget. The details are not. USGS states plainly that how many convection cells exist, where they originate and what their structure is remain unanswered, and the coupling to the plates is not settled either: explanations up to the 1990s emphasised seafloor spreading pushing plates apart, whereas most workers now favour the forces associated with subduction pulling them. Observed spreading rates span an order of magnitude — about 2.5 cm per year at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, more than 15 cm per year on the East Pacific Rise near Easter Island — and the interior flow producing that range is inferred rather than imaged.

## A field made by moving metal

The liquid outer core is electrically conducting and in motion, and a moving conductor in a magnetic field sustains a current that sustains the field: a self-exciting dynamo. Because the field is generated rather than fixed, it drifts, weakens, strengthens and occasionally reverses polarity entirely — every few hundred thousand years, on ESA's summary, so that a compass would point south. ESA's three Swarm satellites, launched on 22 November 2013, were built to separate the contributions to the measured field, which mixes signals originating in the core, mantle, crust and oceans with others from the ionosphere and magnetosphere. The strength of the field is changing continuously and currently shows signs of significant weakening.

The disentangling is harder than the measuring. A magnetometer in orbit records one total field; attributing a change to core flow rather than to crustal magnetisation or to a current system overhead needs the spatial and temporal separation a constellation provides and a single satellite cannot.

## Weighing mass from orbit

Gravity offers an independent handle. NASA's GRACE-FO mission, launched on 22 May 2018, flies two spacecraft about 220 km apart and measures the separation between them with a microwave ranging instrument. When the leading satellite passes over a region of slightly greater mass it accelerates fractionally, the separation changes, and the record of those changes builds a monthly map of the average gravity field. Month-to-month differences in that field are month-to-month redistributions of mass — chiefly water, in ice sheets, groundwater, lakes and rivers, and the ocean.

The limitation is intrinsic to the method, not the instrument. A gravity measurement made outside a body constrains the mass redistribution beneath it but cannot uniquely determine how that mass is arranged in depth, because many different subsurface distributions produce the same field at altitude. Gravimetry is strong evidence that something moved and roughly where, and weak on its own about depth or composition — which is why it is used alongside seismology rather than instead of it.

| Method | What it directly records | What it constrains well | What it cannot resolve alone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Body-wave seismology | Arrival times of P and S phases | Depth of internal boundaries; whether a layer transmits shear | Structure where no ray path samples it |
| Surface heat-flow compilation | Temperature gradient in boreholes and sediments | Total heat leaving the planet | Where in the interior the heat came from |
| Geomagnetic observation | Total field at the instrument | Existence and variation of a core dynamo | Which source region produced a given change, without separation |
| Satellite gravimetry | Change in inter-satellite range | Amount and horizontal location of moved mass | Depth and composition of that mass |

Seismic coverage is the constraint easiest to forget. Earthquakes occur where the stress is, at plate boundaries, and stations sit mostly on land, so the interior is sampled by a geometrically uneven set of ray paths. Parts of the mantle crossed by few of them are known less well than a smoothly layered diagram implies.

## Dating a planet that destroyed its own oldest rocks

The age of the Earth cannot be measured from Earth rocks, because plate tectonics has recycled the oldest ones out of existence. The oldest surviving rocks USGS lists are the Acasta Gneisses of northwestern Canada at 4.03 billion years and the Isua supracrustals of West Greenland at 3.7 to 3.8 billion years; single zircon crystals in younger Western Australian sediments reach 4.3 billion years, and grains dated by uranium–lead have been reported at 4.4 billion. Those set a floor, not the age.

The figure of 4.54 billion years, with an uncertainty of less than 1 per cent, comes instead from treating Earth and the meteorites as parts of one evolving system, and tracking how the ratio of lead-207 to lead-206 changed as uranium-235 and uranium-238 decayed at different rates. The primordial lead composition is read from uranium-free phases of iron meteorites, specifically troilite in the Canyon Diablo meteorite; more than seventy dated meteorites place the formation of the solar system between 4.53 and 4.58 billion years ago, and returned lunar samples give 4.4 to 4.5 billion.

USGS is careful about what that number denotes, and it is worth repeating: the age represents the last time lead isotopes were homogeneous throughout the inner solar system, and the time at which lead and uranium were incorporated into solid bodies. It is a date for a chemical event in the early solar system that Earth took part in, not a timestamp on the completion of a planet — an assembly that was a process rather than an instant, and so has no single moment available to date.
