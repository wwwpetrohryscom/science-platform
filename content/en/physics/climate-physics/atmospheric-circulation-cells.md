George Hadley proposed in 1735 that each hemisphere runs a single convection cell: air heated at the equator rises, travels aloft to the pole, sinks, and returns along the surface. The scheme is thermodynamically sound and it gets only half the answer. It predicts a tropical surface wind blowing from the north in the Northern Hemisphere and from the south in the Southern, which is the equatorward component sailors do observe. What it cannot produce is the far larger easterly component that makes the trades north-easterly and south-easterly rather than northerly and southerly. Accounting for that missing half is the organising problem of the whole circulation, because the poleward energy transport described in the overview of [atmospheric physics](/en/physics/climate-physics/atmospheric-physics-explained) is carried out by a structure that rotation forces into a shape Hadley's scheme cannot produce.

## The rotating surface underneath the air

The Earth's surface moves eastward at a speed that depends on latitude, because every point completes one rotation in the same 24 hours but travels a different distance doing it. NOAA puts that eastward surface speed at over 1,000 mph, or 1,600 km/h, at the equator, falling to zero at the poles. Air sitting over the equator shares that motion.

Send that air poleward and nothing removes its eastward momentum, while the ground beneath it slows down; the air drifts east relative to the surface, and the deflection grows with distance travelled. Air moving equatorward does the reverse, arriving over ground moving east faster than it is and lagging behind, which reads at the surface as a wind from the east. That is the trade wind — less a force acting on the air than a consequence of describing motion from a rotating frame. The same deflection is why the returning flow cannot reach the pole in one span, and why the single-cell picture breaks into three.

## Three cells, and only one of them is a straightforward convection loop

| Cell | Approximate span | Surface wind it produces | What sustains it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hadley | Equator to about 30° | Trade winds, from the east | Direct heating and ascent near the equator, descent in the subtropics |
| Ferrel | About 30° to 50–60° | Westerlies | Not the equator-to-pole temperature difference; NOAA attributes the westerlies it accounts for to friction |
| Polar | About 60° to the pole | Polar easterlies | Descent of cold air over the pole, forming the polar high |

The Hadley cell is the honest convection loop of the three: air rises where the heating is strongest, moves poleward aloft, and sinks in the subtropics. The Ferrel cell, proposed by William Ferrel in 1856, is the awkward one — its surface flow runs poleward and eastward while the air above it moves equatorward and westward, and NOAA is explicit that the westerly winds it accounts for between 35° and 60° are not produced by the equator-to-pole temperature difference. The polar cell is the smallest and weakest.

Between the cells sit bands of surface pressure that show up plainly on a climate map. NOAA places a high-pressure band near 30° N and S and over each pole, with low pressure along the equator and again at 50–60°. Descending air at 30° warms and dries, which is why the subtropical deserts of North Africa, Australia and the American Southwest sit in a rough line, and why the 50–60° belt — Canada, the British Isles, Alaska — is where storms track and rain falls instead.

## The jets sit at the seams

Where two cells meet, the horizontal temperature contrast is at its sharpest, and a sharp contrast plus the momentum argument above produces a narrow, fast band of upper-level wind. There are two of them per hemisphere: the subtropical jet near 30° and the polar jet between 50° and 60°. NOAA puts them typically around 30,000 ft, roughly 9,100 metres, blowing west to east, and gives a maximum of more than 275 mph — 239 knots, or 442 km/h.

Both are strongest in their own hemisphere's winter, when the contrast sustaining them is greatest, and both migrate poleward as the sun climbs in spring. Neither is a line: a jet is a region in which wind speed increases towards a core, so the drawn arrow on a weather map stands for a structure hundreds of miles wide.

## The ITCZ moves, and whole climates move with it

Where the northeast trades of one hemisphere meet the southeast trades of the other, the air has nowhere to go but up. The result is the **Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone**, visible from orbit as a band of convective cloud encircling the globe near the equator, solid for hundreds of miles at a stretch and elsewhere broken into shorter segments — the cloud-forming physics itself is the subject of the article on [how cloud droplets form and grow](/en/physics/climate-physics/convection-and-cloud-formation). NOAA estimates that 40 per cent of all tropical rainfall rates exceed one inch per hour, delivered by storms that are individually small and short-lived.

The band follows the sun. Near the equator, where it passes overhead twice a year, that produces two wet seasons; further away the two merge into one monsoonal wet season and a long dry one. NOAA illustrates the effect with two Nigerian cities 800 km apart: Lagos, in the south, averages 1,740 mm of rain a year, while Kano, in the north, gets 825 mm in a single rainy season from June to September because the convergence zone reaches it once and then retreats. Half the annual rainfall, from the same mechanism, decided by how far north the band travels.

That sensitivity is why monsoon projections are hard. AR6 finds high confidence that a slowdown of the tropical circulation under warming partly offsets the strengthening of monsoon rainfall a warmer, moister atmosphere would otherwise deliver — two effects of one warming, opposite in sign, on a regionally organised [global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained). The assessed projections are correspondingly mixed: increases in more regions than decreases, with medium confidence, but an increase for the South, South East and East Asian domains, a decrease for the North American monsoon, and within West Africa an increase over the central Sahel against a decrease over the far western Sahel.

## Midlatitude weather is waves, not cells

The three-cell diagram is a zonal average, and averaging away the longitude discards most of what makes weather. The midlatitude westerlies do not flow as a smooth ring. They meander in planetary-scale waves, named for Carl Rossby, who identified them in the 1930s, and generated by uneven heating across land and ocean and by air being forced over mountain ranges.

NOAA gives their wavelength as 6,000 to 8,000 km or more, with three to seven present around a hemisphere at any time and typically four or five. They move slowly eastward, sometimes stall, and occasionally retrograde. Smaller disturbances, under 6,000 km, ride within them and travel much faster — an average of about 37 km/h in summer and 55 km/h in winter — and it is these, rather than the long waves, that carry most precipitation bands.

The practical consequence matters for how weather is read. Because the long waves are few and slow, an unusually wet or cold spell in one place implies an unusually dry or warm one elsewhere at the same time; a hemisphere does not experience one anomaly everywhere. Inferring the state of the circulation from local weather is the commonest error made about it, and the way distant regions are linked is developed in the article on [climate variability and teleconnections](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-variability-and-teleconnections).

## The belt is widening, and the rate is contested

AR6 assesses that the Hadley circulation has likely widened since at least the 1980s and that extratropical storm tracks have likely shifted poleward in both hemispheres. The magnitude is much less settled. Studies since AR5, using several metrics across several reanalyses, put the annual-mean poleward shift of the Hadley cell edge at roughly 0.1° to 0.5° of latitude per decade over the last four decades — a fivefold spread that AR6 attributes explicitly to the choice of dataset and metric, compounded by interannual variability large enough to obscure a long-term trend. The widening is mostly a Northern Hemisphere phenomenon in the observations, and AR6 records only medium confidence in the extent of the change.

Longer records complicate rather than confirm the picture. Observational datasets and centennial reanalyses indicate the Northern Hemisphere edge moving equatorward by about 0.25° of latitude per decade between 1945 and 1980, before the recent poleward trend — and AR6 assigns low confidence to Hadley cell extent derived from centennial reanalyses at all, because those products disagree substantially with modern ones over the period they share.

Models sharpen a different question. The [CMIP6 analysis of Hadley cell expansion](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/20/5249/2020/) finds that the poleward shift driven by rising greenhouse gases is two to three times larger in the Southern Hemisphere than in the Northern, for most of the year — which is the opposite of where the observed trend is largest. That mismatch is informative rather than embarrassing: it implies that the recent Northern Hemisphere widening is not primarily a greenhouse-gas signal, and points instead to other forcings, aerosols among them, and to internal variability. The same paper notes that CMIP6 models contract the Northern Hemisphere summer cell substantially more than CMIP5 did, traceable to their higher average climate sensitivity rather than to any change in how the circulation itself is represented — an illustration of how a model's global response leaks into its regional dynamics, which is the theme of [what a climate model actually solves](/en/physics/climate-physics/climate-model-physics-explained).

The zonal circulation stands on less firm ground still. AR6 finds low confidence in long-term trends in the strength of the Walker circulation, which come out differently depending on the period and dataset chosen. Trends since 1980 are better characterised and consistent with a very likely strengthening and westward shift resembling a La Niña-like state, with only medium confidence in the magnitude because satellites and reanalyses disagree. Since that is the same circulation which reorganises during [El Niño and La Niña events](/en/ecology/earth-systems/el-nino-la-nina-enso-explained), separating a forced trend from a run of favourable phases is a genuinely open problem, not a matter of collecting more years.
