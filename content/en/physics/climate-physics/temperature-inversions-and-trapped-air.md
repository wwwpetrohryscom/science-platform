The atmosphere's ordinary condition is to cool with height, and nearly everything that moves air vertically depends on it. An [inversion](/en/glossary/temperature-inversion) is the local failure of that arrangement. The US National Weather Service glossary defines it as "generally, a departure from the usual increase or decrease in an atmospheric property with altitude", adding that the word "almost always refers to a temperature inversion, i.e., an increase in temperature with height, or to the layer within which such an increase occurs". The last clause carries more weight than it looks: an inversion is a layer with a thickness, not a level, and what it does depends on how deep it is and where its base sits as much as on the temperature difference across it. The stability framework it belongs to is set out in the companion article on [lapse rates](/en/physics/climate-physics/atmospheric-structure-and-lapse-rate); what follows is what happens when the sign of the profile flips. The wider frame is set out in the overview of [atmospheric physics](/en/physics/climate-physics/atmospheric-physics-explained).

## Three unrelated ways to build the same structure

Forecaster training material hosted by the National Weather Service, written by the meteorologist Jeff Haby, lists six routes to an inversion. The three taken here share nothing but the profile they leave behind.

The first is **radiational cooling**, which the same material calls "the most common inversion". Its account is compact: "The earth is cooled at night by longwave radiation emission to space. This is maximized on clear nights with light wind and dry air. Air in the lower PBL will cool much more rapidly than air at the top of the PBL at night." The ground loses heat to space and chills the air in contact with it; air a few hundred metres up has no cold surface to lose heat to. Cloud offsets the loss, wind stirs the cooled air away, and water vapour radiates back down, which is why the strongest cases need all three absent. This kind is surface-based and, ordinarily, gone within a few hours of sunrise.

The second is **subsidence**. Air descending in a high-pressure system is compressed and warms as it sinks. Because the sinking is strongest in the middle troposphere rather than at the ground, so is the warming, and the resulting inversion is elevated: for a deep high the training material places it "generally... between 850 and 600 millibars". Because its base sits well above the ground, it is not erased by the next morning's surface heating the way a radiational inversion is, which is what turns a stagnation episode from an overnight nuisance into a multi-day event.

The third is **frontal**. A shallow cold front slides a wedge of dense air underneath warmer air already in place — "the second most common type of inversion", the same source says, "especially evident when a shallow layer of polar air moves into lower latitudes". Here the inversion is an advective accident, two air masses of different origin stacked in the wrong order, and it travels with the front rather than belonging to a place.

Three more complete the six: warm-air advection into the middle troposphere, which the same material notes is "commonly called a cap or lid"; warm air moving over cold water, chilled from below by conduction; and the tropopause itself, created "by the absorption of shortwave radiation by ozone" near the 150 hPa level, which makes the stratosphere the permanent version of the same structure.

## Why a warmer layer above a colder one stops the mixing

The suppression follows from buoyancy, not from any barrier. A parcel displaced upward expands and cools at the adiabatic rate fixed by thermodynamics; in a normal profile the surroundings cool too, and the two rates compete. Inside an inversion the surroundings get *warmer* with height while the parcel keeps cooling, so the density contrast grows with every metre of displacement rather than shrinking: the restoring force strengthens as the parcel rises. Surface-generated turbulence can enter the layer only by working against that gradient, and it runs out of energy quickly.

What this leaves is a box. The volume beneath the inversion base is finite and no longer exchanging efficiently with the free troposphere, so everything emitted into it — exhaust, wood smoke, agricultural ammonia, water vapour — is diluted into that volume and no more. The concentration rises with no change whatever in the emission rate. The same reasoning inverted explains why an unstable column disperses so well, a process traced in [how convection builds clouds](/en/physics/climate-physics/convection-and-cloud-formation).

## What the lid does to fine particles

The clearest quantification comes from a 2026 *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics* study pairing high-resolution L-band radiosonde profiles with China's national air-quality monitoring network for 2016–2021. Inversions are routine rather than exceptional: they occur on 52 per cent of days on average, with a mean strength of 2.1 °C and a mean thickness of 214 m, and are more common in the 08:00 sounding than the 20:00 one, peaking at 84.0 per cent of 08:00 soundings in January. Surface-based inversions dominate northern China and elevated inversions eastern China, the surface-based kind being 1.3 °C stronger on average — 3.02 °C against 1.76 °C.

The same study finds 76 per cent of PM2.5 episodes coinciding with inversion events, and surface-based inversion strength correlating positively with [PM2.5](/en/glossary/pm25) concentrations nationwide. The elevated-inversion parameters do not behave the same way — they show *negative* associations in eastern and southern regions. A stable layer whose base sits above the polluted volume does something different from one sitting on top of it, and treating every inversion as equivalent obscures that.

Nor is the mechanism purely one of trapping. A case study of Truckee Meadows, Nevada, over the cold, snowy winter of 2009/2010 found that all nine days with PM2.5 above 35 µg/m³ — the US 24-hour primary standard, in the form of the 98th percentile averaged over three years, against an annual standard of 9.0 µg/m³ — showed a 24-hour average temperature inversion of 1.5–4.5 °C together with snow cover of 8–18 cm. The snow was an active ingredient, its albedo suppressing the daytime heating that would otherwise break the layer. Receptor modelling attributed the mass to secondary ammonium nitrate at 27–37 per cent, residential wood combustion at 11–51 per cent and diesel exhaust at 7–22 per cent: a substantial fraction of those particles were not trapped but *made* in the stagnant layer. The study attributes the majority of that secondary ammonium nitrate to the same wood stoves and diesel engines, by way of their oxides-of-nitrogen emissions, and names the nitrate and the water associated with it as the main reasons for the exceedances. The size class at work here is examined in [the evidence on particulate matter and health](/en/ecology/pollution/particulate-matter-and-health-evidence), and why a standard's statistical form does as much work as its number in [what the monitors measure](/en/ecology/pollution/air-quality-measurement-and-standards).

London, December 1952, is the canonical subsidence case. The Met Office attributes the event to an anticyclone: "This pushes air downwards, warming it as it descends. This creates an inversion, where air close to the ground is cooler than the air higher above it." The smog-laden fog lay over the city from Friday 5 December to Tuesday 9 December in a layer 100–200 m deep, visibility down to a few metres, while London went on emitting 1,000 tonnes of smoke particles a day, along with the 370 tonnes of sulphur dioxide a day that were converted into 800 tonnes of sulphuric acid. The Met Office puts the toll at about 4,000 known deaths with the caveat that "it could be many more" — a hedge worth keeping, since reconstructed excess-mortality figures depend on the counting method and the attribution window.

## Fog is the same lid with the water left in

An inversion that cools the surface layer to its dew point gives fog rather than haze; the two are expressions of one structure. A field campaign in southwestern France over autumn and winter 2019–2020 combined cloud radar, microwave radiometer, wind lidar and surface stations to follow individual fog layers. Its central structural finding is that "radiation and advection–radiation fogs form under deep and thin temperature inversions, respectively" — the same appearance at ground level beneath two different stability profiles. Pre-onset, the paper describes a double stratification of the [boundary layer](/en/glossary/boundary-layer): a thin inversion from the surface to about 100 m, with a deeper and stronger one of around 8 °C per kilometre above it.

Dissipation shows the inversion's grip most clearly: advection–radiation fog cleared at night, driven by mechanical turbulence from advection, while radiation fog cleared only after sunrise, once solar heating supplied a near-surface sensible heat flux above 10 W/m². Nothing removes a radiative inversion but an energy source that reverses the sign of the surface flux.

## Valleys keep them longest

Terrain converts an episodic inversion into a semi-permanent one. Cold, dense air drains downslope and pools in the basin, and the ridges shelter it from the wind that would scour it out. A study of the three interconnected valleys around Grenoble — the Voreppe, Drac and Grésivaudan, 2 to 6 km wide and 20 to 40 km long, enclosed by ranges reaching 3,000 m — defines a persistent cold-air pool by requiring the valley lapse rate to exceed a threshold of −3 K/km for at least five consecutive days. Over 1985–2014 that criterion identified 119 episodes in the regional model and 91 in the observations, mean duration between seven and eight days. During them, the authors write, "the atmosphere inside the valley is stable and vertical mixing is prevented, promoting the accumulation of pollutants close to the valley bottom and worsening air quality".

The projection is weaker than the diagnosis. The study finds a statistically significant decreasing trend in cold-pool intensity only under SSP5–8.5, the valley atmosphere being slightly less stable under that pathway and statistically unchanged under SSP2–4.5. Anyone expecting basin winter air quality to improve because the climate is warming is leaning on a signal this analysis found only at the high end of forcing.

## Strength is a definition before it is a measurement

The conventional metrics for comparing inversions across places and datasets are proxies rather than the thing itself. Lower-tropospheric stability is the potential-temperature difference between 700 hPa and the surface; estimated inversion strength refines it by removing the moist-adiabatic increase above the lifting condensation level. Neither locates the inversion. A 2023 profile-based method that does — finding the layer of greatest vertical potential-temperature gradient between the lifting condensation level and 5 km above ground in ERA5, then measuring the jump across it — correlates with the inversion strength measured at the ARM Southern Great Plains site at R = 0.74, against R = 0.53 for lower-tropospheric stability and R = 0.45 for estimated inversion strength. The real strength, the same work notes, is typically less than 10 K.

Two things follow. A published trend in "inversion strength" is partly a property of the metric. And the layers are frequently thinner than the vertical spacing of the instruments and models meant to resolve them — the 214 m mean thickness found over China sits well inside the range a coarse profile smears out. An inversion can be real, consequential and invisible to the sounding meant to detect it: a limit on the evidence base, not a detail of it.
