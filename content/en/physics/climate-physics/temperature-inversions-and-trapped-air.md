The atmosphere's ordinary condition is to cool with height, and nearly everything that moves air vertically depends on it. An [inversion](/en/glossary/temperature-inversion) is the local failure of that arrangement. The US National Weather Service glossary defines it as "generally, a departure from the usual increase or decrease in an atmospheric property with altitude", adding that the word "almost always refers to a temperature inversion, i.e., an increase in temperature with height, or to the layer within which such an increase occurs". That last clause carries more weight than it looks. An inversion is a layer with a thickness, not a level, and what it does depends on how deep it is and where its base sits as much as on the temperature difference across it. The stability framework it belongs to is set out in the companion article on [lapse rates](/en/physics/climate-physics/atmospheric-structure-and-lapse-rate); what follows is what happens when the sign of the environmental profile flips.

## Three unrelated ways to build the same structure

Forecaster training material published by the National Weather Service lists six routes to an inversion, and the three that account for most of them have almost nothing in common except the profile they leave behind.

The first is **radiational cooling**, which the same material calls "the most common inversion". Its account of the mechanism is compact: "The earth is cooled at night by longwave radiation emission to space. This is maximized on clear nights with light wind and dry air. Air in the lower PBL will cool much more rapidly than air at the top of the PBL at night." The ground loses heat to space and chills the air in contact with it; air a few hundred metres up has no cold surface to lose heat to. Cloud offsets the loss with downwelling radiation, wind stirs the cooled air away from the surface, and water vapour radiates back down, which is why the strongest cases need all three absent. This kind is surface-based and, in the ordinary case, gone within a few hours of sunrise.

The second is **subsidence**. Air descending in a high-pressure system is compressed and warms as it sinks. Because the sinking is strongest in the middle troposphere rather than at the ground, so is the warming, and the resulting inversion is elevated: for a deep high the training material places it "generally... between 850 and 600 millibars". This one has no diurnal cycle. It is not undone by the following morning's sunshine, and that is what turns a stagnation episode from an overnight nuisance into a multi-day event.

The third is **frontal**. A shallow cold front slides a wedge of dense air underneath warmer air already in place — the same source calls this "the second most common type of inversion" and notes it is "especially evident when a shallow layer of polar air moves into lower latitudes". Here the inversion is an advective accident, two air masses of different origin stacked in the wrong order, and it travels with the front rather than belonging to a place.

Two further routes are often mistaken for those three. Warm air moving over cold water is cooled from below by conduction, producing a marine surface inversion with no radiative or dynamical cause at all. And the tropopause itself is an inversion, created, in the training material's words, "by the absorption of shortwave radiation by ozone", near the 150 hPa level. The stratosphere is not a special case; it is the permanent version of the same structure.

## Why a warmer layer above a colder one stops the mixing

The suppression follows from buoyancy, not from any barrier. A parcel displaced upward out of the surface layer expands and cools at the adiabatic rate fixed by thermodynamics. In a normal profile the surroundings cool too, and the two rates compete. Inside an inversion the surroundings get *warmer* with height while the parcel keeps cooling, so the density contrast between them grows with every metre of displacement rather than shrinking. The restoring force strengthens as the parcel rises. Turbulence generated at the surface can only enter the layer by doing work against that gradient, and it runs out of energy quickly.

What this leaves is a box. The volume beneath the inversion base is finite, it is no longer exchanging efficiently with the free troposphere above, and everything emitted into it — exhaust, wood smoke, agricultural ammonia, water vapour — is diluted into that volume and no more. The point to hold on to when reading an air-quality episode is that the concentration rises without the emission rate changing at all. The same reasoning inverted explains why an unstable column disperses so well, a process traced in [how convection builds clouds](/en/physics/climate-physics/convection-and-cloud-formation).

## What the lid does to fine particles

The clearest quantification of the link comes from a 2026 study in *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics* that paired high-resolution L-band radiosonde profiles with the national air-quality monitoring network across mainland China for 2016–2021. Inversions turn out to be a routine rather than exceptional feature of the profile: they occur on 52 per cent of days on average, with a mean strength of 2.1 °C and a mean thickness of 214 m, and they are more common in the 08:00 sounding than the 20:00 one. Surface-based inversions dominate northern China and elevated inversions eastern China, and the surface-based kind is 1.3 °C stronger on average — 3.02 °C against 1.76 °C. The seasonal concentration is severe: monthly occurrence peaks at 84.0 per cent of 08:00 soundings in January.

The pollution association in the same study is that 76 per cent of PM2.5 episodes coincide with inversion events, and that surface-based inversion strength correlates positively with [PM2.5](/en/glossary/pm25) concentrations nationwide. The elevated-inversion parameters do not behave the same way — they show *negative* associations in eastern and southern regions — which is a useful corrective to the assumption that an inversion is an inversion. A stable layer whose base sits above the polluted volume does something different to a stable layer sitting on top of it.

The mechanism is not purely one of trapping. A case study of Truckee Meadows, Nevada, over the cold, snowy winter of 2009/2010 found that all nine days with PM2.5 above 35 µg/m³ showed a 24-hour average temperature inversion of 1.5–4.5 °C together with snow cover of 8–18 cm — the snow being an active ingredient, since its high albedo suppresses the daytime surface heating that would otherwise break the layer. Receptor modelling attributed the mass to secondary ammonium nitrate at 27–37 per cent, residential wood combustion at 11–51 per cent and diesel exhaust at 7–22 per cent. A substantial fraction of the particles were therefore not trapped but *made* in the stagnant layer, from precursors given days of residence time and high humidity to react in. That distinction matters for what abatement is supposed to target; the size-class definition doing the work here is discussed in [the evidence on particulate matter and health](/en/ecology/pollution/particulate-matter-and-health-evidence).

The 35 µg/m³ figure is the US 24-hour primary standard for PM2.5, whose regulatory form is the 98th percentile averaged over three years; the annual primary standard is 9.0 µg/m³ as an annual mean averaged over three years. Why the statistical form does as much work as the number is set out in [what the monitors measure](/en/ecology/pollution/air-quality-measurement-and-standards).

London, December 1952, remains the canonical demonstration of the subsidence case. The Met Office attributes the event to an anticyclone: "This pushes air downwards, warming it as it descends. This creates an inversion, where air close to the ground is cooler than the air higher above it." The smog-laden fog lay over the city from Friday 5 December to Tuesday 9 December in a layer 100–200 m deep, with visibility down to a few metres. Each day, London emitted roughly 1,000 tonnes of smoke particles and 370 tonnes of sulphur dioxide, which converted to about 800 tonnes of sulphuric acid. The Met Office puts the toll at about 4,000 known deaths with the caveat that "it could be many more" — a hedge worth preserving, since reconstructed excess-mortality figures depend heavily on the counting method and the window over which deaths are attributed.

## Fog is the same lid with the water left in

An inversion that cools the surface layer to its dew point produces fog rather than haze, and the two are expressions of one structure. A field campaign in southwestern France over autumn and winter 2019–2020 combined cloud radar, microwave radiometer, wind lidar and surface stations to follow individual fog layers through their life cycle. Its central structural finding is that "radiation and advection–radiation fogs form under deep and thin temperature inversions, respectively" — the same appearance at ground level produced beneath two quite different stability profiles. The pre-onset state is described as a double stratification of the boundary layer: a thin inversion from the surface to about 100 m, with a deeper and stronger one of around 8 °C per kilometre above it.

Dissipation is where the inversion's grip shows most clearly. The campaign observed advection–radiation fog clearing at night, driven by mechanical turbulence from advection, and radiation fog clearing only after sunrise, when solar heating supplies a near-surface sensible heat flux above 10 W/m² and thermal turbulence combines with wind shear. Nothing removes a radiative inversion except an energy source that reverses the sign of the surface flux.

## Valleys keep them longest

Terrain converts an episodic inversion into a semi-permanent one. Cold, dense air drains downslope and pools in the basin, and the surrounding ridges shelter it from the wind that would otherwise scour it out. A study of the three interconnected valleys around Grenoble — 2 to 6 km wide, about 20 km long for the Voreppe and Drac valleys and 40 km for the Grésivaudan, enclosed by ranges reaching 3,000 m — defines a persistent cold-air pool by requiring the valley lapse rate to exceed a threshold of −3 K/km for at least five consecutive days. Over 1985–2014 that criterion identified 119 episodes in the regional model and 91 in the observations, with a mean duration between seven and eight days. The authors state the consequence plainly: during these episodes "the atmosphere inside the valley is stable and vertical mixing is prevented, promoting the accumulation of pollutants close to the valley bottom and worsening air quality".

The projection is weaker than the diagnosis. The study finds a statistically significant decreasing trend in cold-pool intensity only under the SSP5–8.5 scenario, with the valley atmosphere slightly less stable in the future under that pathway and statistically unchanged under SSP2–4.5. A warming climate does not obviously deliver fewer valley inversions, and anyone expecting winter air quality in basins to improve on that account is relying on a signal this analysis found only at the high end of forcing.

## Strength is a definition before it is a measurement

Comparing inversions across places and datasets requires agreeing what is being compared, and the conventional metrics are proxies rather than the thing itself. Lower-tropospheric stability is the potential-temperature difference between 700 hPa and the surface; estimated inversion strength refines it by removing the moist-adiabatic increase above the lifting condensation level. Neither locates the inversion. A 2023 profile-based method that does — identifying the layer of greatest vertical potential-temperature gradient between the lifting condensation level and 5 km above ground in ERA5, then measuring the jump across it — correlates with the inversion strength measured at the ARM Southern Great Plains site at R = 0.74, against R = 0.53 for lower-tropospheric stability and R = 0.45 for estimated inversion strength. The same work notes that the real inversion strength is typically less than 10 K.

Two things follow. Fixed-level proxies are systematically imperfect, so a published trend in "inversion strength" is partly a property of the metric. And the layers themselves are frequently thinner than the vertical spacing of the instruments and models meant to resolve them — the mean 214 m thickness found over China is well within the range that a coarse profile smears out. An inversion can be real, consequential and invisible to the sounding intended to detect it, which is a limitation on the evidence base, not a detail of it.
