Temperature is the quantity that [the laws of thermodynamics](/en/physics/thermodynamics/laws-of-thermodynamics-explained) are written in, and until 2019 its unit was defined by a property of one substance. That is an unusual foundation for a quantity that appears in every branch of physics, and the reason it lasted is that the substance is very well behaved.

## The old definition

NIST states it: "In 1954, the kelvin was defined as equal to the fraction 1/273.16 of the thermodynamic temperature of the triple point of water."

The triple point is where solid, liquid and vapour coexist, and it is a fixed point rather than a chosen one: "For a precise formulation of water at a specific pressure, the triple point always occurs at exactly the same temperature: 273.16 K."

That is a genuinely good standard. It is reproducible, it needs no calibration against anything else, and a well-made triple-point cell realises it to better than a tenth of a millikelvin. The words "a precise formulation" carry weight, though — the isotopic composition of the water has to be specified, because the triple point of water made from different isotopic mixtures is not quite the same temperature.

## Why it was replaced

The weakness was not at the fixed point but away from it. Defining a unit by one reference temperature means every other temperature is reached by extrapolation, and NIST identifies extrapolation to extremes as the problem the redefinition addressed.

The practical mitigation had been to specify many additional defining points along an agreed temperature scale, so that a thermometer could be calibrated against a nearby reference rather than extrapolated from 273.16 K. That works, and it is machinery layered on top of a definition rather than a consequence of it.

## The new definition

Since the revised SI was approved in November 2018, the unit is defined through a constant instead: "The kelvin, symbol K, is the SI unit of thermodynamic temperature; its magnitude is set by fixing the numerical value of the Boltzmann constant to be equal to exactly 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹."

The Boltzmann constant is the conversion between energy and temperature. Fixing it means a kelvin is defined as the temperature change corresponding to a specific change in thermal energy — a definition with no privileged temperature and no privileged substance.

That is the substantive gain. Any method that measures an energy can now realise the kelvin directly: acoustic gas thermometry, Johnson noise thermometry, dielectric-constant gas thermometry. Each is best in a different range, and each is a primary realisation rather than a calibration against a distant fixed point.

## What did not change

The numerical value was chosen so that the new kelvin matches the old one as closely as measurement allowed. No temperature reading changed on 20 May 2019, and the triple point of water remains 273.16 K — but now as a measured quantity with an uncertainty rather than as an exact definition.

That inversion is the same one described in [the SI and its defining constants](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/the-si-and-its-defining-constants). Uncertainty was moved from the constant to the artefact, and in both the kelvin and the kilogram the artefact was the thing that could not be improved without breaking the definition.

## The connection to entropy

Fixing k also fixes the bridge between the two definitions of [entropy](/en/physics/thermodynamics/entropy-explained) — Clausius's ratio of heat to temperature, and Boltzmann's count of microstates. The constant that converts between a thermodynamic quantity and a statistical one is now exact, which means the relationship between them carries no unit-related uncertainty at all.
