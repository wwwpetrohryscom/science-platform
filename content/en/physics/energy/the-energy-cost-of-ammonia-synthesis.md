The triple bond in molecular nitrogen is among the strongest in chemistry, and breaking it is the reason a large share of the world's [energy system](/en/physics/energy/energy-systems-explained) goes into one industrial process. Both routes that exist — the industrial one and the biological one — are expensive, and comparing them is more informative than either in isolation.

## The industrial number

The IEA's ammonia technology roadmap states the scale plainly: "Ammonia production accounts for around 2% of total final energy consumption and 1.3% of CO2 emissions from the energy system."

The destination matters as much as the total: "Around 70% of ammonia is used to make fertilisers, with the remainder used for a wide range of industrial applications."

So roughly 1.4% of world final energy is spent converting atmospheric nitrogen into a form crops can use. That is an agricultural figure wearing industrial clothes, and it is the reason nitrogen fertiliser prices track natural gas prices rather than agricultural ones.

## Where the energy goes

Two distinct demands sit inside that total, and they decarbonise differently.

**Making the hydrogen.** Most ammonia hydrogen comes from steam methane reforming, which reacts methane with steam and releases CO₂ as a direct product of the chemistry rather than of combustion. That CO₂ is intrinsic to the route: it appears even if the process heat is supplied from a zero-carbon source.

**Running the synthesis.** The Haber–Bosch reaction is run at high pressure and elevated temperature over an iron catalyst, and compression is a substantial part of the energy demand. This half is straightforwardly electrifiable.

The split explains why the decarbonisation discussion is dominated by hydrogen supply rather than by the synthesis loop. Replacing reformed hydrogen with electrolytic hydrogen removes the intrinsic emissions; it also imports the [efficiency penalties of hydrogen as a carrier](/en/physics/energy/hydrogen-as-an-energy-carrier), because every conversion step costs.

## The biological comparison

Life solved the same problem, and the cost is quantified. The molybdenum nitrogenase reaction is written in a 2022 *FEBS Letters* perspective as "N2+8H++8e−+16MgATP→2NH3+H2+16MgADP+16Pi".

Sixteen ATP per nitrogen molecule fixed, plus eight electrons, plus an obligatory hydrogen molecule released as a by-product. The authors draw the comparison directly, noting that this "highlights that both the biological and industrial processes are energy consumptive."

Biology is not a cheap alternative that industry failed to copy. It runs at ambient temperature and pressure, which industry cannot, and it pays for that in a different currency: a large ATP budget, and an enzyme with a disabling constraint — "the nitrogenase proteins exhibit considerable oxygen sensitivity and thus must be manipulated within anaerobic environments."

An oxygen-sensitive enzyme in an oxygen-rich world requires an infrastructure of protection, and that infrastructure is much of what a root nodule is. The full biological accounting is in [nitrogen fixation and its cost](/en/biology/microbiology/nitrogen-fixation-and-its-cost).

## What this means for the transition

Three points follow that are easy to miss when ammonia is discussed only as a fuel candidate.

**The existing demand is the priority.** Ammonia is already made at industrial scale, overwhelmingly for fertiliser, and decarbonising that existing output is a defined problem. Proposals to use ammonia additionally as a fuel or a hydrogen carrier add demand on top of a supply chain that is not yet clean.

**The emissions are chemical, not just combustive.** A process whose CO₂ comes from the feedstock rather than from heating cannot be fixed by decarbonising the heat.

**The alternative is not doing without.** Fertiliser nitrogen supports a large share of global food production. Reducing ammonia demand means improving how efficiently applied nitrogen is used, which is an agronomic problem rather than an industrial one, and one where the losses are large enough to matter.
