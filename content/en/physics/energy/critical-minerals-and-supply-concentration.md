Every technology in a decarbonised [energy system](/en/physics/energy/energy-systems-explained) is made of something, and a few of those somethings have become the subject of a policy category. "Critical" in critical minerals is not a statement about geological scarcity. It is a statement about the consequences of a supply interruption, which depend on how concentrated the supply chain is and how hard the material is to replace. Both are properties of the industry rather than of the element, and both can change without the geology changing at all.

## The number that matters

The IEA reports the direction of travel plainly: "Critical mineral markets have become more concentrated, not less, particularly when it comes to refining and processing", with "the average market share of the top three refining nations rose to 86% in 2024 from around 82% in 2020."

Two features of that statement deserve emphasis. The concentration is in refining, not extraction — ore is mined in many places and processed in few. And it is rising rather than falling, over a period in which diversification has been an explicit policy objective across several major economies.

Investment is not obviously closing the gap. The IEA records that "Investment momentum in critical minerals has weakened: spending grew by just 5% in 2024, down from an increase of 14% in 2023."

## Which materials, and what for

The IEA sets out the mapping between materials and technologies: "Lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese and graphite are crucial to battery performance"; "Rare earth elements are essential indispensable to the permanent magnets used in wind turbines and electric vehicle motors"; and "Electricity networks need a huge amount of aluminium and copper."

Those three groups have different risk profiles. Battery materials have active substitution research and chemistries that trade one constraint for another. Rare earths for magnets are harder to substitute, because the properties depend on specific electronic structure. Copper and aluminium are abundant and well supplied, and the exposure is to volume rather than to availability — grid buildout needs a great deal of both, and mine development takes a long time.

## Why refining concentrates

The step from ore to a battery-grade or magnet-grade material is chemically demanding, capital-intensive, environmentally regulated, and subject to strong economies of scale. Those four properties push toward few large facilities rather than many small ones, and they favour locations where capital, permitting and expertise coincide.

That is a structural explanation rather than a political one, and it means the concentration will not be reversed by opening mines. A new mine in a diversified location that ships its concentrate to the same refineries changes the extraction map and leaves the bottleneck where it was.

## What the data behind these judgements looks like

The underlying statistics are ordinary government reporting rather than proprietary analysis. The USGS *Mineral Commodity Summaries* is "the earliest Government publication to furnish estimates covering nonfuel mineral industry data", published annually, with "data sheets contain[ing] information on the domestic industry structure, Government programs, tariffs, and 5-year salient statistics for over 90 individual minerals and materials."

Over ninety commodities, updated every year, with a five-year window on each — that is the empirical base most criticality assessments rest on, and knowing its structure is useful, because a criticality judgement is a weighting applied to that data rather than an independent measurement.

## What reduces exposure

**Recycling changes the volume required.** The IEA estimates that "The growth in new mining supply for critical minerals could be brought down by between 25% and 40% by mid-century by scaling up recycling" — a substantial reduction in the growth rate, examined in [recycling and material circularity](/en/physics/energy/recycling-and-material-circularity).

**Substitution changes what is required.** It works well where the function depends on bulk properties and badly where it depends on specific electronic structure, which is why battery chemistry has more room than [permanent magnets](/en/physics/matter-radiation/materials-for-magnets-and-why-substitution-is-hard) do.

**Efficiency changes the intensity.** Less material per unit of service is the cheapest reduction available and the least discussed, because it does not produce a project.

None of these removes the refining bottleneck. They reduce how much passes through it.
