Electricity systems are sized for their peak rather than their average, so a load that is large in total but flat is easier to serve than one that is smaller and concentrated. Space cooling is the second kind, and its concentration is what makes it a structural problem for [an energy system](/en/physics/energy/energy-systems-explained) rather than a demand-growth problem.

## The elasticity

The EPA gives a figure for the temperature sensitivity of US urban electricity demand: consumption for air conditioning rises "1–9% for each 2°F increase in temperature".

Even the low end of that range compounds sharply over a heatwave. A 10 °F excursion above normal, on a 1% per 2 °F sensitivity, is a 5% demand increase; at the high end it is 45%. The range is wide because it depends on climate, building stock and how much cooling is already installed — a city where most buildings are already air-conditioned has less headroom to add than one where they are not.

## Why it lands at the worst time

Three things go wrong simultaneously in hot weather, and their coincidence is the substance of the problem.

**Demand peaks in the late afternoon and early evening.** Buildings have been absorbing heat all day and occupants have returned home. This is after peak solar output and often before wind picks up.

**Thermal generation derates.** Gas turbine output falls with rising ambient temperature, and thermal plants dependent on cooling water lose capacity when river and lake temperatures rise or flows fall. The plants available in a heatwave are less capable than their nameplate ratings.

**Transmission derates.** Line ratings depend on ambient temperature and wind; hot still air is the worst case for both conductor cooling and sag limits. The network carries less at exactly the moment more must move.

So the supply side loses capacity while the demand side gains load, and the two effects are driven by the same weather. This is why [capacity factor and nameplate capacity](/en/physics/energy/capacity-factor-and-energy-metrics) are poor guides to whether a system will hold: what matters is availability at the coincident peak.

## The feedback with the city

Air conditioning moves heat from indoors to outdoors. The EPA lists anthropogenic heat from vehicles and air conditioning among the causes of [urban heat islands](/en/ecology/ecosystems/urban-heat-islands-and-surface-energy-balance), which raise the temperature that drives the demand.

The loop is real and bounded — the rejected heat is a small fraction of the solar input to a city — but it runs in the wrong direction, and it concentrates in exactly the districts where cooling is most used.

## What changes the picture

**Efficiency changes the level, not the shape.** More efficient equipment reduces energy per unit of cooling and leaves the coincidence with the peak intact. It helps, and it does not solve the timing.

**Thermal mass and pre-cooling change the shape.** Cooling a building earlier, when supply is abundant and cheap, and letting it drift through the peak, moves load without reducing service. This is one of the few demand-side measures with a large addressable volume.

**Solar output partially matches the load.** Cooling demand correlates with sunshine, so photovoltaic generation covers part of the daytime rise — but the peak is late, and the mismatch at the end of the day is precisely the problem [grid integration of variable renewables](/en/physics/energy/grid-integration-of-variable-renewables) has to solve.

**The elasticity is not symmetric.** Heating demand falls with warming and cooling demand rises, but they are met by different infrastructure — heating substantially by fuels delivered through pipes, cooling almost entirely by electricity. A shift from one to the other moves load onto the electricity system even if total energy demand falls.
