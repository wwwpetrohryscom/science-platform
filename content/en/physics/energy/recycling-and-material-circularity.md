Recycling is often presented as the answer to the material constraints on an [energy system](/en/physics/energy/energy-systems-explained) built largely of metal, and the useful question is how much of the answer it can be. The IEA gives a figure: "The growth in new mining supply for critical minerals could be brought down by between 25% and 40% by mid-century by scaling up recycling."

That sentence repays careful reading, because it is a claim about growth rather than about total supply, and the distinction decides what recycling can and cannot do.

## Why a growing stock cannot be closed

Recycling supplies material from products reaching end of life. In a system whose installed stock is growing, the material reaching end of life today corresponds to what was installed one product lifetime ago, when the stock was much smaller.

For a technology deployed at an accelerating rate with a fifteen-year life, this year's retirements reflect fifteen-year-old deployment volumes. Recovery from that flow, even at perfect efficiency, cannot meet current demand — not because the recycling is inefficient but because the arithmetic of a growing stock does not permit it.

The corollary is that circularity becomes achievable as growth slows. A stable installed base with a fixed lifetime can in principle be maintained largely from its own retirements, minus losses. The transition period is the hard part, and the transition period is now.

## What "scaling up" has to overcome

Three obstacles are distinct and are often conflated.

**Collection.** Material that is not collected cannot be recycled, and collection rates for dispersed consumer products are low. This is a logistics problem, and it is the largest single loss in most material flows.

**Separation.** Modern devices are deliberately hard to take apart, and alloys and composites are designed for performance rather than for separability. Recovering a pure stream from a mixed one is the technical core of the problem.

**Economics against a commodity price.** Recycled material competes with mined material at market price. When prices fall, recycling capacity idles, which is the opposite of what a growing stock of retired products requires.

## What it does and does not change

**It does not relieve the refining bottleneck.** Recovered material still requires refining to battery or magnet grade, and refining is where [supply concentration](/en/physics/energy/critical-minerals-and-supply-concentration) sits. Recycling reduces the volume entering that step; it does not diversify it.

**It changes the emissions accounting.** Recovered material generally carries lower embodied energy than primary material, which moves the upstream stage of a [life-cycle comparison](/en/physics/energy/life-cycle-emissions-and-how-they-are-compared). How much depends on the process and on the grid supplying it.

**It interacts with design.** A cell chemistry chosen for energy density may be harder to separate than one chosen for recyclability, and the trade is real. This is one of the places where a materials decision made for [battery performance](/en/physics/energy/battery-energy-density-and-its-limits) propagates decades forward into a waste stream.

## The honest summary

A 25 to 40 per cent reduction in the growth of mining supply is a large and worthwhile outcome. It is also a statement that mining supply still grows, and that recycling is a substantial contributor to a system that remains substantially extractive through mid-century.

Presenting circularity as an alternative to extraction, rather than as a reduction in its growth rate, misstates both the size of the achievement and the scale of what remains.
