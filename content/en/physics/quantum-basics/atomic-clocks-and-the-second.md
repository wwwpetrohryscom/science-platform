Time is the best-measured quantity in science by a wide margin, and the reason is that [quantum mechanics](/en/physics/quantum-basics/quantum-mechanics-fundamentals) supplies something no macroscopic object can: an energy difference that is identical in every atom of a given species, everywhere, forever.

## How the definition works

NIST describes the mechanism plainly: "The second is currently defined using cesium atoms, which absorb and emit microwave radiation with a specific frequency. Atomic clocks count 9,192,631,770 of those microwave oscillations, and we call the elapsed time interval a second."

The number is not measured. It is fixed by definition, which means the second is whatever duration contains that many oscillations of that particular transition. Any question about how accurately the second is realised is a question about a clock, never about the number.

The transition itself is between two hyperfine levels of the caesium-133 ground state — an energy splitting produced by the interaction between the nuclear spin and the electron. Caesium was chosen partly because that splitting falls in a microwave band that mid-twentieth-century electronics could handle, and partly because the atom has a single valence electron and a convenient vapour pressure.

## How good the clocks are

For commercial instruments, NIST gives the figure: "commercially available cesium clocks keep time to within 1/3,000,000 of a second per year."

A third of a microsecond a year is about one part in 10¹⁴. That is already far beyond any requirement of ordinary life, and it is the floor rather than the ceiling of what exists.

The ceiling is elsewhere: NIST notes that "new experimental clocks based on other atoms are ten thousand times more precise still".

Four orders of magnitude beyond one part in 10¹⁴ is one part in 10¹⁸ — a precision at which a clock resolves the gravitational redshift over a height difference of a few centimetres. At that point a clock is no longer only a timekeeper; it is a gravimeter, and the same instrument measures elevation and geopotential.

## Why the definition may change again

The reason optical clocks matter for the definition rather than only for applications is that a definition should not be less precise than the instruments realising it. When the best clocks outperform the defining transition by four orders of magnitude, the definition constrains what can be expressed.

The obstacle is agreement rather than performance. Redefining the second requires several independent optical standards, on different atoms in different laboratories, to agree at the level of their claimed uncertainties — which is the same [interlaboratory comparison](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/interlaboratory-comparison-and-consensus-values) requirement that governs any quantity with no external reference, applied to the most precisely measured quantity there is.

## Why timekeeping propagates

The second appears inside other definitions. As set out in [the SI and its defining constants](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/the-si-and-its-defining-constants), the caesium frequency helps define "the second, meter, kilogram, and ampere" — so an improvement in timekeeping is an improvement in three other units at once.

It is also the reason satellite navigation works. Position is inferred from signal travel times, so a clock error of a nanosecond is a position error of about 30 centimetres, and the system's performance is a timekeeping performance wearing a geometric disguise.
