The modern SI is often described as being based on constants of nature. That is true and slightly abstract; the operational content is that the constants are realised through quantum effects, and quantum effects have a property that manufactured artefacts do not.

## The property that matters

Two caesium atoms are not similar. They are identical, in a sense that has no classical analogue: there is no measurement that distinguishes them, and the energy difference between their hyperfine levels is the same number in every laboratory and every epoch.

That is why [quantum mechanics](/en/physics/quantum-basics/quantum-mechanics-fundamentals) is the natural foundation for measurement standards. A standard built on an atomic property needs no calibration against a master copy, because there is no master copy — every instance is the standard.

Compare the alternative. A platinum-iridium cylinder is a particular object with a particular history, and copies of it [drifted relative to one another](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/what-changed-when-the-kilogram-changed) over a century. Nothing analogous can happen to a hyperfine splitting.

## Where the quantum effects enter

**Time** through the caesium hyperfine transition, counted at "9,192,631,770 hertz" by definition, with commercial clocks reaching "1/3,000,000 of a second per year" and experimental clocks "ten thousand times more precise still".

**Electrical units** through the Josephson effect and the quantum Hall effect, which relate voltage and resistance to h and e in ways that depend only on those constants — which is why fixing e and h fixes the electrical units without any reference to a physical resistor or cell.

**Mass** through the Kibble balance, which compares mechanical power against electrical power realised by those same quantum effects, so that a mass is weighed against the Planck constant.

**Temperature** through methods that measure a thermal energy directly, now that "the numerical value of the Boltzmann constant" is fixed "to be equal to exactly 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹".

**Amount of substance** through a fixed count, "exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities", realised by counting atoms in a silicon crystal.

Five of the seven base units are realised through quantum physics. The remaining two — the metre and the candela — depend on the second and on defined constants, so the dependency is universal even where the realisation is optical or photometric.

## What this buys, beyond precision

**Independence.** Any competent laboratory can realise the unit from first principles rather than borrowing it. That changes the shape of the [traceability chain](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/calibration-and-traceability) from a tree rooted in one object to a set of independent realisations that can be compared with each other.

**Scalability.** An artefact defines one value and everything else is obtained by subdivision or multiplication, each step adding uncertainty. A constant applies at any magnitude.

**Durability.** A definition that references no object survives the destruction of every apparatus that realises it.

## Where it is heading

The pattern of the last two redefinitions was the same: measure a constant well enough by two independent routes, confirm they agree, then fix it and invert the arrow. The second is the candidate now, because optical clocks outperform the defining caesium transition by four orders of magnitude and the obstacle is agreement between independent standards rather than performance — the position set out in [atomic clocks and the second](/en/physics/quantum-basics/atomic-clocks-and-the-second).

The same physics is moving out of national laboratories into field instruments, which is the subject of [quantum sensors leaving the lab](/en/physics/quantum-basics/quantum-sensors-leaving-the-lab). A gravimeter based on atom interferometry and a clock precise enough to measure elevation are the same argument applied outside metrology: an instrument whose calibration is a constant of nature does not need recalibrating.
