The [electromagnetic spectrum](/en/physics/quantum-basics/electromagnetic-spectrum-applications) is one phenomenon at different wavelengths, and the [quantum description of light](/en/physics/quantum-basics/quantum-mechanics-fundamentals) supplies a single relation — a photon's energy is inversely proportional to its wavelength — determines what each part of it can do to matter. That relation is why the spectrum has the sharp functional divisions it does, and why those divisions do not sit where intensity would put them.

## Three regimes, set by what a photon can break

**Below the vibrational range**, a photon can set a molecule rotating or vibrating and nothing more. The energy is deposited as heat. This is the microwave and infrared regime, and it is why these bands warm things without changing their chemistry.

**In the visible and near-ultraviolet**, a photon can promote an electron to an excited state. That is enough to start photochemistry, and it is the entire basis of vision and photosynthesis. It is not enough to remove an electron from a molecule.

**Above roughly the far-ultraviolet**, a photon can eject an electron entirely, leaving a reactive ion behind. This is ionising radiation, and the threshold is a genuine one rather than a convention: the chemical consequences of ionisation are categorically different from those of excitation.

The boundary between the second and third regimes is the most consequential in biology, and it falls inside the ultraviolet.

## The ultraviolet case, in detail

Ultraviolet spans a large enough energy range to sit on both sides of biologically important thresholds. A review in *Photochemistry and Photobiology* gives the divisions: "UVA (315–400 nm), UVB (280–315 nm) and UVC (100–280 nm)."

The most striking observation is that biological effect does not follow intensity. "UVB forms only about 5% of all UV radiation reaching the earth's surface, which effectively causes DNA damage", while "UVA forms about 95% of all UV radiation entering the earth, but is weaker than UVB."

Ninety-five per cent of the ultraviolet arriving at the ground does comparatively little direct damage to DNA, because it falls outside the absorption band of the bases. Five per cent does most of it, because it falls inside. The relevant quantity is overlap between the source spectrum and the absorber's spectrum, not total energy — which is the general lesson of this article stated in its sharpest instance, and the mechanism behind [ultraviolet damage and its repair](/en/biology/cells/ultraviolet-damage-and-photoprotection).

## The same logic on the useful side

Photosynthesis works in the visible because that is where photon energies match the electronic transitions of chlorophyll — energetic enough to drive a charge separation, not energetic enough to break the pigment. The machinery is correspondingly narrow: it absorbs strongly in the blue and red and weakly in the green, so a substantial part of the incident solar energy passes through a leaf unused.

That mismatch is not a failure of optimisation but a consequence of using discrete electronic transitions to capture a continuous spectrum. A photon above the transition energy delivers its excess as heat within picoseconds; a photon below it does nothing at all. The same constraint bounds a solar cell, and it is why [the single-junction limit](/en/physics/thermodynamics/thermodynamic-limits-of-photovoltaics) sits where it does.

The efficiency achieved within that constraint is remarkable: Photosystem I's "internal quantum efficiency is close to 100 %", making it, in the words of a 2013 review, "the most efficient energy converter in nature". The system is nearly perfect at using the photons it can use, and structurally unable to use most of the ones that arrive.

## Why this framing is worth keeping

Three habits follow from thinking in photon energy rather than in named bands.

**Ask what the absorber absorbs.** Exposure to a band means nothing biologically until it is weighted by an action spectrum. Total ultraviolet is not a dose in any useful sense.

**Expect thresholds, not gradients.** Because the effect depends on whether a photon can cause a specific transition, the response to wavelength is often a step rather than a slope. Ionisation is the clearest case; photochemical damage is nearly as sharp.

**Do not infer penetration from energy.** More energetic is not more penetrating in any simple way — alpha particles are stopped by paper and radio waves pass through walls. Penetration depends on what the medium absorbs, which is again a question about the absorber.
