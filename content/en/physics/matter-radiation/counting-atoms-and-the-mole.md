Chemistry needs a counting unit. Reactions proceed in ratios of particles, and [atomic and nuclear physics](/en/physics/matter-radiation/atomic-and-nuclear-physics-explained) supplies the particles, but no instrument counts them directly at laboratory scale. The mole is the bridge, and how it is defined determines what a chemical measurement is ultimately compared against.

## The definition now

NIST states it as a bare count: "One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities", that number being the fixed value of the Avogadro constant — "6.02214076 x 10²³ particles per mole".

Notice what is absent. There is no substance, no mass, no temperature and no pressure in that sentence. A mole is a number of things, in the way that a dozen is, and the things are whatever the context specifies — atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, formula units.

## Why that is a change

The previous definition tied the mole to mass: a mole was the number of atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12. That made Avogadro's constant a measured quantity, with an uncertainty, and it coupled the counting unit to the kilogram — which was itself [a metal cylinder in a vault](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/what-changed-when-the-kilogram-changed).

Two units defined through one object is a fragile arrangement. Any drift in the prototype propagated into the mole, and any improvement in measuring Avogadro's constant was an improvement in a comparison rather than in knowledge of nature.

Fixing the number severs the link. The mole no longer depends on the kilogram, on carbon, or on any material at all, and the molar mass of carbon-12 becomes a measured quantity with a small uncertainty instead of an exact defined value.

## The consequence for molar mass

This is the part that surprises chemists, and it is worth stating carefully. Molar masses are no longer exact by definition. The molar mass constant — the factor relating relative atomic mass to grams per mole — is now determined experimentally rather than being exactly 1 g mol⁻¹.

The uncertainty involved is around one part in 10⁹, which is far below what any ordinary chemical measurement resolves. So nothing in practice changed, and the conceptual position did: molar mass is now a measured quantity, as the mass of the old prototype is, and as the triple point of water is under [the redefined kelvin](/en/physics/thermodynamics/the-kelvin-and-the-boltzmann-constant).

## The route that made it possible

Fixing Avogadro's constant to a specific value required knowing it well enough that the fixed value would not visibly shift the unit. The measurement that achieved this counted atoms in a sphere of isotopically enriched silicon-28, using the crystal lattice spacing, the sphere's volume and its mass.

That experiment is also one of the two independent routes to realising the [redefined kilogram](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/what-changed-when-the-kilogram-changed), which is why the two redefinitions were done together. Determining h and N_A precisely enough are the same problem approached from two directions, and the agreement between the silicon route and the Kibble balance is what justified fixing both.

## What it does not fix

The mole says nothing about what is being counted, and a measurement in moles is only as good as the identification of the entity. That is a chemical question rather than a metrological one, and it is where most real-world errors in molar quantities originate — not in the unit, which is now exact, but in the assumption about what the sample contains.
