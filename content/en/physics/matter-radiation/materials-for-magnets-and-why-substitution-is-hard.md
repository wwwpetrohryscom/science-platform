Some material constraints yield to engineering and some do not, and the difference usually comes down to whether the required property is a bulk one or an atomic one. Permanent magnets are the clearest case in the energy transition of a property that is atomic, and the consequences run through every discussion of supply risk.

## Where they are used

The IEA states the dependency directly: "Rare earth elements are essential indispensable to the permanent magnets used in wind turbines and electric vehicle motors."

Both applications want the same thing: the strongest possible field from the smallest possible mass, sustained without a supply current. In a direct-drive wind generator that reduces nacelle mass at the top of a tower, which propagates into tower and foundation cost. In a traction motor it reduces mass and improves efficiency at part load, which is where a vehicle spends most of its life.

## Why the elements are hard to replace

A permanent magnet needs two things at once: a large magnetic moment per atom, and a strong resistance to being demagnetised by heat or by an opposing field.

The first comes from unpaired electrons. The second — magnetocrystalline anisotropy — comes from coupling between the electrons' spin and their orbital motion within a particular crystal structure, which locks the magnetisation to a crystal axis.

Rare earth elements supply both because of where their electrons sit — a fact about [atomic structure](/en/physics/matter-radiation/atomic-and-nuclear-physics-explained) rather than about materials processing. Their partially filled inner shells provide large moments and strong spin-orbit coupling, and that combination is not a property that another element can imitate by being processed differently. It is a consequence of atomic structure.

This is why substitution here differs from substitution in a battery. A cell chemistry can trade energy density against cost, cycle life or safety, and several combinations are workable — the range visible in [what limits battery energy density](/en/physics/energy/battery-energy-density-and-its-limits). A magnet asked to deliver a given field strength at a given operating temperature has far fewer options, and each of them gives up performance rather than trading it.

## The routes that exist

**Use less.** Grain-boundary diffusion places the scarcest elements only where they are needed for thermal stability rather than throughout the magnet, reducing the quantity for the same performance.

**Design around the magnet.** Induction and wound-rotor machines avoid permanent magnets entirely, at a cost in mass and efficiency. Some [wind turbine](/en/physics/energy/wind-energy-physics) designs use geared drivetrains with smaller generators for the same reason.

**Recover from end of life.** Magnets are dense, concentrated and located in identifiable components, which makes them a better recycling target than dispersed materials — one of the more tractable cases within the [25 to 40 per cent reduction in mining supply growth](/en/physics/energy/recycling-and-material-circularity) that recycling could deliver.

**Accept the performance penalty.** In stationary applications where mass is not costly, a heavier machine with no rare earths may be the right answer. In an aircraft or a vehicle it is not.

## Why the supply risk is about processing

Rare earths are not geologically rare. The difficulty is that they occur together, in chemically similar forms, and separating them requires many stages of solvent extraction — capital-intensive, chemically demanding, and heavily regulated for its waste streams.

That profile is exactly the one that concentrates: the IEA reports the average market share of the top three refining nations rising "to 86% in 2024 from around 82% in 2020". Opening a mine does not change it, because the mine's output goes to the same separation facilities.

So the magnet case combines the two hardest features of a material constraint — a property that resists substitution, and a supply chain whose bottleneck is in processing rather than in the ground. It is the reason rare earths appear in every [critical minerals](/en/physics/energy/critical-minerals-and-supply-concentration) list despite not being scarce.
