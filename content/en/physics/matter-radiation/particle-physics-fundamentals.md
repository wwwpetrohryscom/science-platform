The Standard Model is the most precisely tested theory in science and one of the least complete. Both statements are true at once, and holding them together is the whole difficulty of reading particle physics news.

It is built from a small inventory. CERN's description of it counts six quarks arranged in three generations, six leptons arranged the same way, and a set of force carriers: the gluon for the strong force, the photon for electromagnetism, and the W and Z bosons for the weak force. Adding the Higgs boson completes the list. Every particle detected in every accelerator experiment is either one of these or a composite built from them, and the theory's predictions for their behaviour have survived measurement at a precision that in some cases reaches parts per billion.

## What "fundamental" is doing in that sentence

The distinction that matters is between particles that are composite and particles for which no substructure has been found. A proton is not fundamental — it is three valence quarks bound by gluons, and most of its mass comes from the binding energy of that field rather than from the quarks themselves, which is the same accounting that governs [nuclear binding energy at the next level up](/en/physics/matter-radiation/atomic-and-nuclear-physics-explained). A quark, so far as any experiment has resolved, is.

"So far as any experiment has resolved" is load-bearing. Resolving structure requires wavelength short enough to see it, which requires energy, which is why the search for substructure is a search at ever higher collision energies. The statement "the electron is fundamental" is really the statement "no experiment to date has resolved anything inside an electron", and it carries an implicit bound on scale rather than a claim about all scales.

## The forces, and why their strengths cannot be compared in one number

Three of the four known interactions appear in the Standard Model. Gravity does not: CERN's own summary says fitting gravity into the framework has proved a difficult challenge, which is the field's understated way of describing a problem unsolved since the 1930s.

The three that are included differ in more than magnitude.

| Interaction | Carrier | Range | What it dominates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong | Gluon | Confined to about a nuclear diameter | Binding quarks into hadrons and hadrons into nuclei |
| Electromagnetic | Photon | Unlimited | Atoms, chemistry, light, all of materials science |
| Weak | W and Z bosons | Far below a nuclear diameter | Flavour change: [beta decay](/en/physics/matter-radiation/radioactivity-and-radiation-units), the first step of stellar fusion |

Relative strengths are frequently quoted as a ratio — the strong force is often given as roughly a hundred times electromagnetism — but the number depends on the distance and the energy at which it is evaluated, because the effective coupling of each interaction runs with energy. A single comparison figure is a snapshot at one scale presented as a constant, and it is one of the most common ways popular accounts mislead.

The weak force's short range and low apparent strength are consequences of its carriers being massive, unlike the massless photon and gluon. That is the point where the Higgs enters: it is the mechanism — a field with a non-zero value everywhere, in the [quantum-field description of matter](/en/physics/quantum-basics/quantum-mechanics-fundamentals) — that gives the W and Z their mass, and the reason the weak interaction looks weak at everyday energies is that its carriers are heavy enough to be hard to produce.

## Five sigma, and what a discovery claim actually asserts

On 4 July 2012 the ATLAS and CMS experiments at the Large Hadron Collider each announced observation of a new particle in the mass region around 126 GeV. Two independent detectors, two independent collaborations, one signal.

Particle physics claims a discovery at five standard deviations — a result whose probability under the background-only hypothesis is about one in 3.5 million. The threshold is unusually strict compared with most sciences, and the reason is structural rather than cultural: an experiment testing an enormous number of mass bins and decay channels will produce apparently significant fluctuations somewhere by chance, so the threshold has to absorb that multiplicity. It is a correction for how many questions were asked, not a statement that the result is 99.99997% certain to be true.

It is also worth being clear about what five sigma does not cover. It quantifies statistical fluctuation. It does not quantify a systematic error in the detector calibration, a mismodelled background, or a wrong theoretical prediction of what the background should be — and those are handled separately, by argument and cross-check rather than by a p-value. This is the same distinction between the two classes of uncertainty that runs through [any careful measurement](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/measurement-uncertainty-explained).

## Neutrino mass: physics beyond the Standard Model, already confirmed

The Standard Model as originally written contains massless neutrinos. They are not massless.

Neutrinos change flavour as they travel — an electron neutrino produced by [the proton-fusion chain in the Sun](/en/physics/matter-radiation/nuclear-fusion-fundamentals) can arrive as a muon or tau neutrino — and that oscillation is only possible if the flavour states are mixtures of states with different masses, which requires at least two of the three masses to be non-zero. The 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Takaaki Kajita and Arthur B. McDonald for the discovery of neutrino oscillations, showing that neutrinos have mass.

This is worth stating plainly because "physics beyond the Standard Model" is usually discussed as a prospect. In this instance it is a settled experimental fact, confirmed for a decade, and the theory has been patched rather than replaced. What the masses actually are, whether they are ordered normally or inversely, and whether the neutrino is its own antiparticle all remain open.

## The 95% problem

CERN's summary of the observational situation is blunt: ordinary matter — everything made of the particles above, every star and galaxy — accounts for about 5% of the content of the universe. Dark matter makes up about 27%, outweighing visible matter roughly six to one, and dark energy approximately 68%.

The evidence for dark matter is gravitational and indirect. Galaxies rotate fast enough that the gravity of their observable matter could not hold them together, and the inference has been reinforced by gravitational lensing and by the structure of the cosmic microwave background. What it is remains unknown: it does not absorb, reflect or emit light, and decades of increasingly sensitive direct-detection experiments have returned null results, steadily excluding parameter space rather than finding a signal.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions. The Standard Model is not wrong — nothing in the laboratory contradicts it, and the null results are constraints on what the answer can be, not failures of the theory. But it is manifestly incomplete, in the specific sense that the great majority of the universe's energy content lies outside its inventory, alongside the unexplained matter–antimatter asymmetry and the unexplained pattern of three generations with wildly different masses. A theory can be exactly right about everything it describes and still describe a small fraction of what exists.
