Mass is the quantity [classical mechanics](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/classical-mechanics-explained) is built on, and until 2019 its unit was an object in a vault. NIST puts the arrangement plainly: "For more than a century, the kilogram (kg) — the fundamental unit of mass in the International System of Units (SI) — was defined as exactly equal to the mass of a small polished cylinder, cast in 1879 of platinum and iridium."

That definition had a property no other unit's had. The prototype was, by definition, exactly one kilogram; if it gained or lost material, the kilogram changed with it, and there was nothing to compare it against that was not itself calibrated from it.

## The drift

The problem was observed rather than hypothesised. National copies were compared with the international prototype at long intervals, and NIST notes that "the measured mass of each individual standard changed, or 'drifted,' a bit over time."

What can be said is that they drifted relative to each other, by tens of micrograms over a century. What could not be said is which of them changed, because the international prototype defined the unit and therefore could not, by construction, be wrong.

That is the deepest objection to an artefact definition, and it is logical rather than practical. A standard that cannot be measured against anything cannot be checked at all.

The consequence propagated downward. Calibrating a small mass from a kilogram means subdividing, and each step adds uncertainty, so the smallest masses were the worst served — the opposite of what a measurement system should do.

## The replacement

NIST records the decision: "In November 2018, the international scientific community voted to redefine the kilogram, freeing it from its embodiment in one golf-ball-sized artifact, and basing it instead on a constant of nature."

The kilogram is now defined "by taking the fixed numerical value of the Planck constant h to be 6.62607015 ×10−34 when expressed in the unit J s". Since a joule second is kg m² s⁻¹, fixing h with the metre and second already defined fixes the kilogram.

The realisation is where the physics is. A Kibble balance equates mechanical and electrical power, measuring a mass against electrical quantities that are themselves traceable to h and e through quantum effects; the alternative route counts atoms in an almost perfect silicon sphere and uses the [Avogadro constant](/en/physics/matter-radiation/counting-atoms-and-the-mole). Two independent methods, agreeing within their uncertainties, are what made the redefinition possible — the same [interlaboratory logic](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/interlaboratory-comparison-and-consensus-values) that governs any quantity without an external reference.

## What a user notices

Nothing. The value of h was chosen so that the new kilogram matched the old prototype as closely as the comparison allowed, so no scale needed adjusting and no product specification changed.

What changed is the direction of the arrow. Before, the prototype defined the kilogram and h was measured. Now h defines the kilogram and the prototype's mass is a measured quantity with an uncertainty that grows as it drifts.

The practical gain is at the extremes. A definition realised through electrical quantities can be applied at any scale rather than subdivided down from one, which improves small-mass metrology directly — and it removes the single point of failure that a physical object represents, which is the point of [traceability](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/calibration-and-traceability) in the first place.
