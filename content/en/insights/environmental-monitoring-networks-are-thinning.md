Slow change is detectable only against a long, consistent record. That is not a preference of the discipline; it is a property of the statistics. A trend in flood frequency, a shift in low-flow behaviour, a change in the timing of a biological event — each requires enough years of comparable observation that the signal separates from interannual noise. And a record has a property no other scientific instrument has: once it is interrupted, the missing years cannot be recovered by any later investment. You can rebuild a station. You cannot rebuild its past.

That asymmetry is why the arithmetic of monitoring budgets is so misleading. A network can hold its headcount, satisfy every reporting metric, and still lose most of its scientific value.

## The count stays flat while the records vanish

The clearest documented case is the United States streamgauge network. A WMO Bulletin review of hydrological observation reported that more than 2,200 long-record gauging stations — those with at least thirty years of data — were lost between 1980 and 2005, while the total number of stations in operation stayed roughly constant at about 7,000. Nothing in the headline count moved. What disappeared were precisely the stations able to support flood and low-flow frequency analysis, and to place any recent year in the context of multi-decadal variability.

The same review documents sharper regional losses: 67 per cent of river gauges in Ontario closed between 1986 and 1999, 73 per cent in the far east of Siberia over the same period, and a pan-Arctic network reduced to the density it had in the early 1960s. Its own framing is worth keeping: the problem is that even if stations are later reopened, the break in the series degrades any statistic that depends on consistent multi-decadal observation. That article dates from 2008, which is itself informative — this is a long-standing structural issue rather than a recent one.

## The gauges are not where the variability is

Loss of records is compounded by where the surviving instruments sit. A 2022 analysis in *Nature Sustainability* mapped [32,091 stream gauges against a global river network](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-022-00873-0) of 2,896,897 segments draining more than 25 km². Gauges are concentrated disproportionately in large, perennial rivers draining human-occupied watersheds, and are sparse in protected areas and in rivers with non-perennial flow — the two categories most relevant to freshwater conservation and to water security in dry regions.

That is a bias, not merely a gap. Non-perennial rivers are where hydrological variability is greatest and where climate change is expected to alter flow regimes most sharply. A network optimised for water supply management in settled basins is not the network you would design to detect environmental change, and the two purposes have been quietly conflated for decades. Related consequences for aquifers, where the in-situ well record is even patchier, are covered in the explainer on [groundwater and aquifer depletion](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion).

## Separating what is documented from what is asserted

This subject attracts advocacy, and some widely repeated claims are weaker than they sound. The apparent collapse in the number of stations reporting to the Global Runoff Data Centre after the 1980s is a case in point: much of it reflects the lag between data collection by national agencies and entry into an international archive, not station closure. A 2023 paper in *Earth System Science Data* working with that archive describes [10,701 stations and about 470,000 station-years](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/15/5617/2023/) of daily and monthly discharge data, with an average record length of 45 years. Reading the archive's recent-year tail as a measure of network health would be a straightforward error.

The literature on terrestrial ecological monitoring has a similar mix. A 2025 essay in *PLOS Biology* on [the end of long-term ecological data](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3003102) reports that 191 of 411 long-term mammal studies had been terminated, and describes a 63-year continuous study whose renewal was declined. That is a serious observation, but it is an argued perspective drawing on a prior compilation rather than a systematic audit of monitoring capacity, and it should be read as such. The distinction matters for the same reason the rest of this piece does: a claim about a shrinking evidence base ought to meet the evidential standard it is defending.

## Some networks have grown, which is the strongest objection

The one-directional story is wrong, and the counter-example is substantial. Argo, the array of profiling floats measuring ocean temperature and salinity, did not exist in 1999. Its [implementation status](https://argo.ucsd.edu/about/status/) records the millionth profile collected by November 2012, the two millionth in November 2018 and the three millionth in July 2024, with close to 4,000 active floats and a design target raised from 3,000 to 3,800 to cover seasonal sea-ice zones and marginal seas. Ocean interior heat content, one of the best-constrained quantities in the Earth system, is well constrained precisely because a new in-situ network was built and sustained.

Satellite records have likewise lengthened, and coordinated frameworks such as the essential-variable approach described in [biodiversity monitoring under essential biodiversity variables](/en/ecology/biodiversity/essential-biodiversity-variables-monitoring) have improved comparability where they have been adopted. Anyone claiming a uniform decline has to explain these. The defensible claim is narrower and, for that reason, harder to dismiss: investment has shifted towards instruments that produce data quickly and away from the specific asset — an unbroken multi-decadal series at a fixed place — that cannot be bought later at any price.

## Expertise is part of the instrument

An observing system is not only hardware. Identifying what was caught in a trap or growing in a plot requires people who can do it, and that capacity has its own geography. A 2026 study in *PLOS One* identified [31,521 European taxonomists](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0347332) from a decade of publications, with 59.3 per cent working on animals and 38.0 per cent on plants, and found effort concentrated on vertebrates, insects and vascular plants. Classes including annelids, brachiopods, cnidarians and sponges receive minimal coverage, microalgae remain understudied, and there is a clear east–west divide in numbers across the continent.

That is a capacity map rather than a decline curve, and it should not be quoted as one. But it locates the shortfall in the same place as the gauge shortfall: away from the groups and regions where the biological signal is largest and the baseline weakest.

## Why orbit supplements rather than substitutes

The standard rebuttal is that satellites have made ground networks redundant. They have not, for three reasons that follow from the physics rather than from budgets.

Sensors measure what they can reach. NASA's SMAP mission maps soil moisture at [36 km resolution every two to three days](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/observatory/overview/), and its radiometer responds only to the uppermost few centimetres of the profile — not the root zone, and not the deep store that governs drought persistence. Optical instruments cannot see through cloud or beneath a canopy; gravimetry integrates all water in a column without separating groundwater from soil moisture, snow and surface water.

Orbital records are also short. Satellite gravimetry began with [the GRACE mission launched in March 2002](https://grace.jpl.nasa.gov/mission/grace-fo/), continued by GRACE-FO from May 2018 — roughly two decades against hydrological and cryospheric changes that operate over many more. And a satellite product is a retrieval: a modelled quantity inferred from measured radiances via an algorithm that must be calibrated and validated against ground truth, a dependency examined in [the limits of remote sensing](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty) and, from the sensor side, in the companion insight on [what satellites cannot see](/en/insight/what-satellites-cannot-see). Thinning the in-situ network degrades the satellite record too, because it removes the reference against which the retrieval is checked.

The practical implication for anyone reading a trend claim is a single question: how long is the series it rests on, and was it continuous? A twenty-year record can establish that something changed; it usually cannot establish that the change lies outside natural variability. The indicator products described in [Earth-system climate indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-indicators-earth-system-monitoring) are transparent about which of their inputs are long and which are not, and that distinction is doing more work than the headline value.
