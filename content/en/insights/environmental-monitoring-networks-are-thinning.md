Slow change is detectable only against a long, consistent record. That is not a preference of the discipline; it is a property of the statistics. A trend in flood frequency, a shift in low-flow behaviour, a change in the timing of a biological event — each requires enough years of comparable observation that the signal separates from interannual noise. And a record has a property no other scientific instrument has: once it is interrupted, the missing years cannot be recovered by any later investment. You can rebuild a station. You cannot rebuild its past.

That asymmetry is why the arithmetic of monitoring budgets is so misleading. A network can hold its headcount, satisfy every reporting metric, and still lose most of its scientific value.

## The count stays flat while the records vanish

The clearest documented case is the United States streamgauge network. A WMO Bulletin review of hydrological observation reported that more than 2,200 long-record gauging stations — those with at least thirty years of data — were lost between 1980 and 2005, while the total number of stations in operation stayed roughly constant at about 7,000. Nothing in the headline count moved. What disappeared were precisely the stations able to support flood and low-flow frequency analysis, and to place any recent year in the context of multi-decadal variability.

The same review documents sharper regional losses: 67 per cent of river gauges in Ontario closed between 1986 and 1999, 73 per cent in the far east of Siberia over the same period, and a pan-Arctic network whose station count had fallen back to its early-1960s level. Its own framing is worth keeping: the problem is that even if stations are later reopened, the break in the series degrades any statistic that depends on consistent multi-decadal observation. That article dates from 2008, which is itself informative — this is a long-standing structural issue rather than a recent one.

## The gauges are not where the variability is

Loss of records is compounded by where the surviving instruments sit. A 2022 analysis in *Nature Sustainability* mapped [32,091 stream gauges against a global river network](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-022-00873-0) of 2,896,897 segments draining more than 25 km². Gauges are concentrated disproportionately in large, perennial rivers draining human-occupied watersheds, and are sparse in protected areas and in rivers with non-perennial flow — the two categories most relevant to freshwater conservation and to water security in dry regions.

That is a bias, not merely a gap. Non-perennial rivers are where hydrological variability is greatest and where climate change is expected to alter flow regimes most sharply. A network optimised for water supply management in settled basins is not the network you would design to detect environmental change, and the two purposes have been quietly conflated for decades. Related consequences for aquifers, where the in-situ well record is even patchier, are covered in the explainer on [groundwater and aquifer depletion](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion).

## Separating what is documented from what is asserted

This subject attracts advocacy, and some widely repeated claims are weaker than they sound. The apparent collapse in the number of stations reporting to the Global Runoff Data Centre after the 1980s is a case in point. That holding is a secondary compilation — national agencies collect discharge data and submit it afterwards — so its most recent years are the least complete by construction, and a falling recent-year count is not by itself evidence that gauges closed. A 2023 paper in *Earth System Science Data* working from the March 2022 release describes [10,701 stations and about 470,000 station-years](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/15/5617/2023/) of daily and monthly discharge data, with an average record length of 45 years — against the 7,532 stations available to a comparable exercise a decade earlier. Reading the archive's recent-year tail as a measure of network health would be a straightforward error.

The literature on terrestrial ecological monitoring has a similar mix. A 2025 essay in *PLOS Biology* on [the end of long-term ecological data](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3003102) reports that 191 of 411 long-term mammal studies had been terminated, and describes a 63rd year of continuous work on a single mammal population conducted against a funding panel's view that the project already had too much data. That is a serious observation, but the 191-of-411 count comes from a 2017 synopsis the essay cites rather than from a fresh audit, and the essay is an argued perspective rather than a systematic survey of monitoring capacity. The distinction matters for the same reason the rest of this piece does: a claim about a shrinking evidence base ought to meet the evidential standard it is defending. The same test applies downstream, where the case for land-based interventions collected under [the nature-based-solutions label](/en/insight/nature-based-solutions-and-the-evidence-behind-them) rests on outcome records far shorter than the commitments they are asked to support.

## Argo went the other way

The one-directional story is wrong, and the counter-example is substantial. Argo, the array of profiling floats measuring ocean temperature and salinity, has been built up continuously since deployments began in 2000. Its [implementation status](https://argo.ucsd.edu/about/status/) records the millionth profile collected by November 2012, the two millionth in November 2018 and the three millionth in July 2024, with close to 4,000 active floats and a design target raised from 3,000 to 3,800 to cover seasonal sea-ice zones and marginal seas. The modern record of ocean interior temperature rests on that array. It exists because a network was built and sustained, not because one was inherited.

Satellite records have likewise lengthened, and coordinated frameworks such as the essential-variable approach described in [biodiversity monitoring under essential biodiversity variables](/en/ecology/biodiversity/essential-biodiversity-variables-monitoring) have improved comparability where they have been adopted. Anyone claiming a uniform decline has to explain these. The defensible claim is narrower and, for that reason, harder to dismiss: investment has shifted towards instruments that produce data quickly and away from the specific asset — an unbroken multi-decadal series at a fixed place — that cannot be bought later at any price.

## Expertise is part of the instrument

An observing system is not only hardware. Identifying what was caught in a trap or growing in a plot requires people who can do it, and that capacity has its own geography. A 2026 study in *PLOS One* identified [31,521 European taxonomists](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0347332) from a decade of publications and found effort concentrated on vertebrates, insects and vascular plants. Among the 57 per cent whose work could be tied to a kingdom, 59.3 per cent published on animals and 38.0 per cent on plants — shares that overlap, because 17 per cent published in more than one. Phyla including annelids, brachiopods, cnidarians and sponges have very few specialists at all, microalgae remain understudied, and there is a clear east–west divide in raw numbers across the continent, although the study is careful to note that the divide is much weaker per head of population.

That is a capacity map rather than a decline curve, and it should not be quoted as one. But it locates the shortfall in the same place as the gauge shortfall: away from the groups and regions where the biological signal is largest and the baseline weakest.

## Why orbit supplements rather than substitutes

The standard rebuttal is that satellites have made ground networks redundant. They have not, for three reasons that follow from the physics rather than from budgets.

Sensors measure what they can reach. NASA's SMAP mission maps soil moisture at [36 km resolution every two to three days](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/observatory/overview/), and what its radiometer retrieves is surface soil moisture — the top few centimetres, not the root zone, and not the deep store that governs drought persistence. Optical instruments cannot see through cloud or beneath a canopy; gravimetry integrates all water in a column without separating groundwater from soil moisture, snow and surface water.

Orbital records are also short. Satellite gravimetry began with [the GRACE mission launched in March 2002](https://grace.jpl.nasa.gov/mission/grace-fo/), continued by GRACE-FO from May 2018 — roughly two decades against hydrological and cryospheric changes that operate over many more. And a satellite product is a retrieval: a modelled quantity inferred from measured radiances via an algorithm that must be calibrated and validated against ground truth, a dependency examined in [the limits of remote sensing](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty) and, from the sensor side, in the companion insight on [what satellites cannot see](/en/insight/what-satellites-cannot-see). Thinning the in-situ network degrades the satellite record too, because it removes the reference against which the retrieval is checked.

The consequence for trend detection is arithmetic rather than rhetorical. A twenty-year record can establish that something changed; it usually cannot establish that the change lies outside natural variability, and the shorter the series, the more of any published trend is supplied by the statistical model rather than by the observations. The indicator products described in [Earth-system climate indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-indicators-earth-system-monitoring) are transparent about which of their inputs are long and which are not, and that distinction is doing more work than the headline value they report.
