Ask whether species can adapt to the pace of current environmental change and the answer depends entirely on which of three quite different processes the question means. An individual adjusting its physiology or its timing within its own lifetime, a population changing its allele frequencies across generations, and a species occupying different ground are all called adaptation in ordinary usage. They run on different clocks, they leave different evidence, and only one of them is easy to observe.

## Three responses, three clocks

**Phenotypic plasticity** is a change in the trait an individual expresses, with no change in its genes. It is fast, reversible in many cases, and bounded by whatever range of responses the genotype already encodes. **Adaptive evolution** is a change in the genetic composition of a population, which requires heritable variation in the relevant trait, enough generations, and selection strong enough to act on it — the mechanism described in the article on [how natural selection produces adaptation](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation). **Range shift** moves the population rather than changing it, and depends on dispersal ability and on whether suitable habitat exists to disperse into.

| Response | Timescale | What it requires | How it is detected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plasticity | Within a lifetime | Existing reaction norm; a reliable environmental cue | Repeated measurements on the same individuals; common-garden rearing |
| Adaptive evolution | Multiple generations | Heritable variance in the trait; population size; sustained selection | Quantitative-genetic models on pedigrees; resurrection of dormant propagules |
| Range shift | Years to decades | Dispersal capacity; connected, climatically suitable habitat | Repeated surveys against a historical baseline |

The distinction is not academic. A trait that has shifted because every individual responded flexibly to a warmer spring tells you nothing about whether the population has any evolutionary capacity in reserve, and the two are routinely conflated in reporting of the same result.

## What the observational record is strong on

The volume of documented response is genuinely large. The IPCC's Working Group II assessment of terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems drew on new evidence covering more than 12,000 species and, for the subset of more than 4,000 species where attribution was assessed, reported that approximately half had shifted their ranges to higher latitudes or elevations and that two-thirds of spring phenological events had advanced — a conclusion carried at very high confidence.

The direction of that movement is less uniform than the summary suggests. A 2023 systematic review in *Environmental Evidence* screened the range-shift literature and found that fewer than half of individual observations — 46.6 per cent — went towards higher latitudes, higher elevations or greater depths. Averaged across the subset reporting rates, shifts towards higher latitudes ran at about 11.8 km per decade and upslope shifts at about 9 m per decade, while no significant average shift to greater marine depths emerged. The review also found that methodological choices within individual studies significantly affected both the reported direction and the reported magnitude, which is a warning about pooling.

Rates also have to be judged against the speed of the target. A 2024 analysis in *Nature* mapped lapse rates across the world's mountains and converted surface warming from 1971 to 2020 into vertical isotherm velocities, identifying 17 regions where isotherms are climbing faster than about 11.67 m per year on the satellite-derived lapse rate. In mountains with slower velocities the authors found instances of species tracking closely; in many others, species lag. Where habitat is discontinuous, tracking fails for reasons covered in the article on [habitat fragmentation and connectivity](/en/ecology/conservation/habitat-fragmentation-and-connectivity).

## What the record is weak on

The hard question is whether observed change is genetic. A 2014 review in *Evolutionary Applications* laid out three levels of inference where the literature typically falls short: distinguishing genetic change from plasticity, testing rather than assuming that a change is adaptive, and excluding drivers other than climate. Its verdict was that evidence for [genetic adaptation](/en/glossary/evolutionary-adaptation) to climate change "has been found in some systems, but is still relatively scarce", and that few studies use the designs capable of separating the possibilities — animal-model analyses on pedigreed populations, common-garden rearing, resurrection of dormant eggs or seeds from dated sediment, or direct experimental manipulation.

A review of avian breeding and migration timing in the same journal put the point more sharply for the taxon with the best data: plasticity in the timing of breeding and migration appeared common and often adaptive, but at that time no study had provided direct evidence of an evolutionary response of avian phenology to contemporary warming. The assessed position has since moved, though not as far as the popular framing implies. The IPCC records that a growing number of studies have documented genetic evolution within populations in response to recent climate change (very high confidence), while adding that those genetic changes remain within the limits of variation already known for the species concerned (high confidence).

Evolutionary rescue — the idea that adaptation might arrest a decline before extinction — is well developed as theory and much thinner as field evidence. Its determinants are set out in a 2023 review in *WIREs Climate Change*: population demography, generation time, standing genetic variation, and the rate of environmental change relative to the rate at which adaptation can proceed. A 2013 paper in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* framed the gap precisely: laboratory systems and mathematical models have characterised the process well, but inferring the inheritance and the absolute fitness that drive it in wild populations remains the unsolved part. One of those determinants deserves emphasis, because it is the parameter that decides whether selection has anything to work with at all: the share of a trait's variation that is actually heritable, treated in the article on [what heritability does and does not measure](/en/biology/genetics/heritability-explained).

## The counting problem

There is a structural bias in how this evidence accumulates, and it is worth stating precisely rather than as a general suspicion. A 2019 synthesis in *Nature Communications* screened 10,090 abstracts to find 71 studies, reported in 58 publications, that carried enough information to test whether trait changes associated with warming were actually adaptive. That subset is dominated by birds — 17 species across 13 countries, with a few intensively monitored temperate passerine populations supplying much of the data — and skewed towards the Northern Hemisphere, towards spring events, and towards common and abundant species. The paper's own conclusion is that observed phenological advances are adaptive for some species but incomplete, with selection for still-earlier timing persisting, and that the resulting evolutionary load may already threaten persistence.

The bias runs in a particular direction. Long-term individual-level datasets exist because someone chose, decades ago, to monitor an accessible and abundant population. Populations that collapsed before anyone set up a study contribute nothing. Rare and range-restricted species — the ones whose adaptive capacity the question is really about — are the least represented. This is the same asymmetry that affects historical reference points generally, discussed in the article on [shifting baselines in biodiversity assessment](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-baselines-and-shifting-baselines).

## The strongest case for the optimistic reading

The case that adaptive capacity is systematically underestimated is real, and it comes in part from the same reviews that document the gaps. Three strands support it. First, the designs able to detect genetic change are expensive and rarely deployed, so scarcity of evidence is partly scarcity of the right studies rather than absence of the phenomenon. Second, the climate an organism experiences is not the climate a gridded product reports: buffering under canopy, in soil and on shaded slopes decouples local exposure from the regional average, and the mountain analysis did find species tracking isotherms closely where those isotherms move more slowly. Third, plasticity has a dual role in this literature — the 2023 review treats it as capable of facilitating adaptive evolution as well as impeding it — and the IPCC assesses with high confidence that plasticity and genetic adaptation together may let populations persist locally for longer than local climate change alone would suggest. Work on heritable thermal tolerance in reef symbioses, taken up in the article on [coral microbiomes and bleaching resistance](/en/biology/cells/coral-microbiome-bleaching-resistance), is a live test of how far that reaches.

## Where the science is not divided

One part of this is not contested, and inventing a dispute there would misrepresent the field. Evolution works on variation that exists; it does not manufacture a climate space a lineage has never occupied. The IPCC states that none of the evolutionary changes documented or theorised would enable a species to survive and reproduce in climate spaces it does not already inhabit, and that it is very improbable that evolutionary responses would prevent extinction where a species loses its climate space entirely at regional or global scale. The genuinely open questions sit below that ceiling: how much local persistence plasticity and adaptation buy, for which taxa, and over what period — questions that feed directly into [how extinction risk is assessed](/en/ecology/conservation/species-extinction-risk-assessment).

## What would move the answer

Three things would narrow the range more than another decade of correlational studies. Quantitative-genetic estimates from populations other than a small set of temperate birds, so that generation time and genetic variance can be compared across taxa rather than assumed. Resurrection experiments using dated dormant propagules, which compare ancestors and descendants under identical conditions and sidestep the plasticity problem entirely. And publication of populations that failed to respond, without which the denominator stays unknown.

Until those exist, the practical reading rule is to check the design before the claim. A documented phenological shift establishes that a trait moved. It does not establish that the movement was genetic, that it was adaptive, or that it was enough.
