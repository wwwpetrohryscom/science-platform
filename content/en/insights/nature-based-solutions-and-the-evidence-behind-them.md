IUCN defines nature-based solutions as "actions to address societal challenges through the protection, sustainable management and restoration of ecosystems, benefiting both biodiversity and human communities". That is a statement about intent and about co-benefits. It is not a statement about mechanism, magnitude or measurability, and it groups together interventions whose evidence bases have almost nothing in common.

The IPCC's own phrasing in its Sixth Assessment is revealing. Working Group III describes land-sector mitigation measures that also deliver biodiversity and wellbeing benefits as ones "sometimes described as 'Nature-Based Solutions'". The label is applied to measures assessed individually; it is not itself a unit the assessment evaluates. That distinction is routinely lost downstream, and losing it has a specific consequence: the members with the strongest measurement basis end up lending their credibility to the members with the weakest.

## Four members, four different evidence problems

| Intervention | What is actually measured | Evidence assessment in AR6 | The accounting problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coastal habitat protection and restoration | Wave-height attenuation across habitat width; avoided flood damage; carbon stock in sediment | Robust evidence, high agreement for coastal storm and flood protection | Permanence under sea-level rise; restored blue-carbon flux estimates rest on limited evidence |
| Peatland conservation and rewetting | Water-table depth; CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O flux from chambers and towers; peat depth and bulk density | Medium confidence on a technical potential of 0.86 (0.43–2.02) GtCO₂-eq per year | Peat extent and existing stocks are poorly constrained in both northern and tropical latitudes |
| Afforestation and reforestation | Tree survival and growth increment; above-ground biomass via allometry; soil carbon change | Sectoral and model estimates of restoration potential differ by a factor of three at the central estimate | Additionality, leakage, fire and drought reversal, and albedo warming at high latitudes |
| Urban and peri-urban green space | Air and surface temperature differentials; runoff attenuation; canopy cover | Robust evidence, high agreement for local climate regulation and flood regulation | Benefits are highly local and distributionally uneven; carbon contribution is negligible at global scale |

Two rows in that table carry the IPCC's strongest evidence language and two do not. Nothing about the shared label predicts which is which.

## How wide the uncertainty actually is

The most-cited global quantification of the mitigation side is a 2017 paper in *PNAS* that identified twenty land stewardship pathways and put their [combined maximum potential at 23.8 PgCO₂e per year](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5676916/) once constrained by food security, fibre security and biodiversity safeguards, of which 11.3 PgCO₂e per year was judged cost-effective at a social cost of CO₂ of at least USD 100 per tonne. Those are the numbers that circulate. The internal structure of the estimate circulates much less.

Within it, avoided forest conversion carries an uncertainty of about ±17 per cent, reflecting decades of forest monitoring. Reforestation — the pathway that dominates public discussion — carries a 95 per cent confidence interval of 2.7 to 17.9 PgCO₂e per year, a spread of roughly sevenfold, driven by disagreement about how much land is available. Eight of the twenty pathways were quantified by expert elicitation rather than empirical uncertainty estimation. Biochar is included with the explicit caveat that it has not been demonstrated beyond research settings. And the authors excluded activities whose carbon gains would be offset by biophysical warming, naming boreal forest expansion — an exclusion that only works if you already know which projects fall into that class.

The IPCC's independent assessment reaches a similar shape. AR6 Working Group III puts the economic AFOLU mitigation potential at [8 to 14 GtCO₂-eq per year between 2020 and 2050](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-7/) at up to USD 100 per tonne, with the low end being the mean of integrated assessment models and the high end the mean of sectoral studies. Within that, protection of forests and other ecosystems is assessed at 4.0 (2.5–7.4) GtCO₂-eq per year, while restoration — afforestation, reforestation, peatland and coastal wetland restoration — comes out at 2.1 (0.8–3.8) in sectoral studies against 0.7 (0.07–6.8) in the models. Two credible method families disagree by a factor of three on the central estimate of the same activity. That is the honest state of the field, and it is not visible in a headline gigatonne figure.

## Protection is better evidenced than restoration, and the reason is physical

The pattern across both assessments is consistent: avoiding loss is better constrained than creating gain. Carbon already in a peat profile or a mangrove sediment is measurable now; carbon that a project promises to accumulate over forty years is a projection. AR6 makes the stronger version of this point, noting that most carbon lost from primary peatlands, coastal wetlands and forests is irrecoverable through restoration on the timescale of a mid-century net-zero target.

Restoration also inherits the reversal problem. Wildfire, drought-driven mortality and land-use change can return sequestered carbon to the atmosphere, and AR6 states that the feasibility of land-based mitigation is hampered by uncertainty over long-term additionality, weak governance and uncertain permanence. It adds that robust measurement, reporting and verification is essential to prevent misleading claims about net carbon-stock change per unit of land — the same structural issue examined in the insight on [carbon offset markets and measurement](/en/insight/carbon-offset-outsourcing-science) and, on the ground, in the article on [what tree-planting evidence supports](/en/ecology/forests/reforestation-and-tree-planting-evidence).

Albedo compounds this asymmetry. AR6 finds that afforestation and reforestation change surface reflectivity and evapotranspiration in ways that produce net cooling in tropical and subtropical latitudes but net warming at high latitudes. Confidence in prescribing specific land-cover changes to achieve a desired climate outcome remains low, partly because the relationship between a change in albedo and a change in surface temperature is itself poor.

## The strongest case for the category

The counter-argument is not weak and deserves stating properly. The term was coined precisely to stop climate policy from evaluating land through a single carbon metric. A 2020 paper in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* set out both halves of this: nature-based approaches can address adaptation, mitigation and biodiversity simultaneously, and yet [their potential has not been rigorously assessed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7017763/), with a specific risk that mitigation policy will favour interventions of low biodiversity value such as non-native monoculture afforestation and produce maladaptation. The category exists to make that trade-off visible rather than to hide it.

The adaptation evidence supports the same reading. AR6 Working Group II assesses ecosystem-based adaptation measure by measure, and several receive its strongest grading: coastal habitat re-establishment, urban and peri-urban green space, and invasive aquatic plant control for water security are all rated [robust evidence, high agreement](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-2/), while natural flood management in rivers, agroforestry and rangeland rehabilitation sit at medium evidence, medium agreement. The chapter cites a systematic map of 386 published studies finding that interventions in natural or semi-natural ecosystems reduced adverse climate impacts in 66 per cent of cases, with fewer trade-offs than more artificial systems such as plantation forest — while noting that most of that evidence comes from the Global North and that site-specific comparisons against alternatives are scarce.

The best-quantified member of the whole family may be coastal defence. A 2016 study in *PLOS ONE* synthesised [69 field measurements of wave attenuation and 52 nature-based defence projects](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0154735), finding that coral reefs and salt marshes have the highest overall potential for reducing wave height, that effectiveness depends on measurable ratios of wave height to water depth and habitat width to wavelength, and that salt marshes and mangroves can be two to five times cheaper than a submerged breakwater for waves up to half a metre. That is a defensible engineering claim with stated limits — and it says nothing whatever about whether a tree-planting scheme in a different biome will deliver its projected tonnes. IUCN's own [Global Standard for nature-based solutions](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/49070), published in 2020 as a verification framework, is best read as an admission of the same problem: a label needs criteria before it can be checked.

## What to ask instead

The useful question is never whether nature-based solutions work. It is which intervention, at which site, delivering which measurable quantity, verified how, against what counterfactual, and for how long. Those are the questions the evidence is organised to answer, and the ones the category name obscures. A wetland restoration scheme and a monoculture plantation can both be presented under the same heading; the [functions a wetland performs](/en/ecology/freshwater/wetlands-and-their-functions) and the evidence for them are entirely distinct from the carbon arithmetic of the plantation, and the general findings on [what restoration meta-analyses show](/en/ecology/conservation/ecological-restoration-evidence) do not transfer between them. Where the intervention is urban, as covered in [urban ecology](/en/ecology/ecosystems/urban-ecology-explained), the measurable benefit is local cooling and stormwater attenuation rather than global mitigation, and claiming the latter for it weakens the well-evidenced case for the former.
