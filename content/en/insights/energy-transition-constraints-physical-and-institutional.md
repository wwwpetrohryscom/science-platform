Two statements that sound alike do quite different work. *No rotor can extract more than 59.3 per cent of the kinetic energy in the air passing through it* is a theorem. *A generation project in the United States waits a median of 61 months between asking to connect to the grid and delivering power* is a description of an administrative process that a federal regulator has recently rewritten. Both get quoted as limits on the energy transition. Only one of them has a derivation.

The diagnostic is that plain. A physical constraint can be derived from conservation laws and stated without reference to any country, decade or institution. An institutional constraint has a history: someone can name when it started, what it replaced, and which body would have to act to change it. The classification is not a ranking of importance — institutional limits are frequently the binding ones — but the two classes respond to entirely different interventions, and the categories are routinely mixed in public argument.

## Limits with a derivation

The **Betz limit** is the cleanest example. Extract energy from a moving fluid and you slow it; slow it too much and the flow diverts around the rotor rather than through it. Balancing mass and momentum across an idealised disc gives a maximum power coefficient of 16/27, about 59.3 per cent. The chapter *Wind Turbine Power: The Betz Limit and Beyond* sets out the assumptions behind that number — one-dimensional flow through an ideal loaded disc, no friction or turbulence losses, an infinite number of blades — and notes that operating machines sit nearer 40 per cent. The mechanism is developed further in the article on [the physics of wind energy capture](/en/physics/energy/wind-energy-physics).

Photovoltaics have their own version. Detailed balance says that a semiconductor warm enough to absorb photons must also emit them, and that photons below the band gap pass through while photons above it dump their excess as heat. The result is the [Shockley–Queisser limit](/en/glossary/shockley-queisser-limit). A 2020 report of a six-junction concentrator device in *Nature Energy* put the ceiling for the conventional case bluntly: single-junction flat-plate terrestrial cells are "fundamentally limited to about 30% solar-to-electricity conversion efficiency". The same device measured 47.1 per cent under 143 suns, and a variant reached 39.2 per cent at one sun.

That pairing is the important lesson about derived limits, and it is the one most often missed. A theorem binds the thing it was derived for. Shockley–Queisser constrains a single junction under unconcentrated light, not photovoltaic conversion in general; Betz constrains a single idealised disc in unconstrained flow, not the aggregate output of an array whose machines interact through the same airflow. Quoting either as a ceiling on the technology rather than on the architecture is a category error, and it is why the [thermodynamic ceiling on solar conversion](/en/physics/thermodynamics/thermodynamic-limits-of-photovoltaics) sits far above the number usually cited.

Some derived limits are simply awkward. Hydrogen carries about 120 MJ per kilogram against roughly 44 MJ per kilogram for gasoline, but as a liquid it holds only about 8 MJ per litre where gasoline holds 32, and reaching even that density requires 350–700 bar of pressure or cryogenic storage below its −252.8 °C boiling point. Those figures come from the Department of Energy's hydrogen storage programme, and they follow from the molecule rather than from any deficiency of engineering — a constraint that shapes every argument about [hydrogen as an energy carrier](/en/physics/energy/hydrogen-as-an-energy-carrier).

## Limits with a history

Now the other class. Berkeley Lab's *Queued Up* series compiles interconnection queue data from more than fifty U.S. transmission providers covering roughly 98 per cent of national generating capacity. The 2026 edition, current to the end of 2025, counts about 2,061 GW actively seeking interconnection across 8,244 projects — 1,312 GW of generation and 749 GW of storage — alongside 24,221 withdrawn requests totalling about 4,532 GW. The median project reaching commercial operation in 2025 spent 61 months in the process, against 36 months for the 2015 cohort and 22 months for 2008. Of the capacity that requested interconnection between 2000 and 2020, about 13 per cent had reached operation by the end of 2025; on a project count the figure is about 19 per cent. The duration statistics rest on the 73 per cent of operational projects with a usable in-service date, which is worth carrying alongside the number.

Nothing in that paragraph is derivable from physics. The report attributes the shifting queue picture to interconnection process reforms alongside market conditions, transmission constraints, permitting barriers and other factors — a list of institutions rather than of conservation laws. FERC Order 2023 exists because the arrangement is amendable, and the data in this edition pre-date its implementation.

The same is true of hardware supply. The IEA's assessment of transmission supply chains reports that procuring cables now takes two to three years and large power transformers up to four, with direct-current cables extending beyond five — lead times that have almost doubled since 2021, while cable prices have nearly doubled since 2019 and transformer prices have risen by around 75 per cent. Factory capacity is a decision about capital, not a conservation law, and these delays feed directly into what limits [the integration of variable renewables](/en/physics/energy/grid-integration-of-variable-renewables).

Minerals supply carries the same ambiguity in its public framing. The IEA's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025* finds the average market share of the top three refining nations for key energy minerals rising from around 82 per cent in 2020 to 86 per cent in 2024, with China the dominant refiner for 19 of the 20 minerals assessed at an average share of about 70 per cent. That is a statement about where processing plants were built, not about what the crust contains.

## Where the diagnostic gets hard

Not every claim sorts cleanly. Some fuse a derived quantity to a modelling choice so tightly that the two come apart only under inspection — the storage requirement of a very-high-renewable grid is one such case, worked through in the companion piece on [why seasonal and daily storage are different problems](/en/insight/seasonal-storage-is-a-different-problem).

Declining ore grades sit awkwardly between the classes in a different way, and the honest treatment is to say so. A study of 25 copper mines published in *Resources* found the combined weighted average grade falling by roughly 25 per cent between 2003 and 2013 — at Chile's El Soldado the average oxide grade fell from 1.7 per cent in 2003 to 0.46 per cent by 2012 — while total energy use across the Chilean operations analysed rose 46 per cent against a 30 per cent rise in copper produced. The authors read the pattern through the second law: as grade falls, more rock must be moved and ground to recover the same metal, and the energy requirement climbs steeply as concentration approaches crustal abundance.

The competing reading is set out in the same paper's opening pages, and it deserves its full weight rather than a summary. On that account, declining grades are neither a sign of depletion nor an indicator of resource availability. Grades fall partly because innovation in extractive technology made lower-grade ore economic to process at all, and partly because extending the life of an older mine is cheaper than finding a new one — an expansion of the accessible resource base showing up in the statistics as a decline. The strongest form of the argument is quantitative rather than rhetorical: for several metals, uranium, nickel and zinc among them, deposit size has grown faster than grade has fallen, so the metal contained in the known resource has risen even as the headline percentage dropped. Both readings fit the same observations. The physical relationship between grade and separation energy is not in dispute; what the grade *trend* signifies is.

## Why keeping the classes apart is worth the trouble

The practical payoff is that the two respond to different things. A derived limit yields only to a change of architecture — more junctions, concentration, a different carrier — and no amount of institutional reform moves it. A queue, a lead time or a refining share yields to procurement policy, capital allocation and rule changes, and no amount of materials research moves it.

There is a normative trap on the institutional side that deserves stating plainly. Many of these arrangements exist to protect something: environmental review, cost allocation between incumbents and entrants, land tenure, grid reliability standards. Calling such a rule a "barrier" and calling it a "protection" describe the same object, and the choice between those words is a judgement about competing values rather than a technical finding. An analysis that classifies a constraint as institutional has established that it could be changed, not that it should be.

Two things this taxonomy does not do. It does not tell you which constraint binds first — that depends on the system, though where a project spends five years waiting on a study process, the limit it meets first is plainly not a thermodynamic one. And it does not settle where costs belong, because a price is neither a theorem nor a rule: it is an outcome, and it moves for reasons drawn from both classes at once. What the taxonomy does is fix where an argument has to be won: against a derivation, or against a body that could decide otherwise.
