A synthesis of 400 studies and 5,142 response variables examined recovery from oil spills, agriculture, logging and other large disturbances. Two of its findings sit uneasily together. Recovery was positive in every category examined, at a median rate of about 2.9% per year. And half the dataset had no more than ten years in which to recover.

That is the shape of the problem. Restoration is a decades-to-centuries process being evaluated on a decade of evidence, and the evaluation is not neutral about where in the trajectory it lands.

## What the syntheses report, and against what

Three large syntheses give the clearest picture, and reading them together requires care, because they do not use the same comparator.

A [meta-analysis of 221 forest-restoration study landscapes](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms11666) found that restoration enhanced biodiversity by 15–84% and vegetation structure by 36–77% *compared with degraded ecosystems*, while stating that restoration "does not result in full recovery of biodiversity and vegetation structure" relative to old-growth reference forest. Time elapsed since restoration began was one of the three main ecological drivers of success.

A [global meta-analysis of 83 terrestrial restoration studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9320827/) put numbers on both comparisons at once: restored sites carried 20% higher mean biodiversity than unrestored sites, and 13% lower mean biodiversity than reference sites. Restored sites were also more variable than reference sites — 20% more — while being less variable than unrestored ones. Biodiversity at restored sites rose by roughly 0.6% per year relative to unrestored sites.

A [meta-analysis of 621 restored or created wetlands against 556 reference wetlands](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.1001247) supplies the long-horizon result. Even a century after restoration, biological structure remained on average 26% lower and biogeochemical functioning 23% lower than in reference sites. Plant assemblages took an average of 30 years to converge statistically with reference states. Wetlands in cold climates, and wetlands in inland depressions fed by rainfall or groundwater, had not recovered to reference conditions after 50 years. Wetlands with riverine or tidal exchange did better: as a group they recovered biogeochemical functions after about 20 years and biological structure after about 30. The functions those systems perform are described in the article on [what wetlands do](/en/ecology/freshwater/wetlands-and-their-functions).

Read across, these do not support a single ordering of structure and function. The wetland analysis found the sequence reversed between climates — in temperate settings structural variables looked recovered within five years while biogeochemical functioning was still at 79% of reference after thirty, and in tropical settings biogeochemistry recovered first — and at the century horizon the structural shortfall was marginally the larger of the two, 26% against 23%. Nor is it true that nothing converges: vertebrate assemblages reached reference-like structural values within five years, and macroinvertebrates within five to ten. What the three syntheses do support is narrower and more awkward: recovery times differ by an order of magnitude between attributes, the slowest of them are unfinished at every horizon anyone has observed, and which attribute a study chose to measure moves the verdict about as much as which ecosystem it studied.

## The monitoring horizon

The wetland analysis reports the distribution that makes this a measurement problem rather than a research finding: 49% of its data came from sites restored or created for less than five years. The 400-study synthesis reports that half its dataset had ten years or less. Against a plant assemblage that takes 30 years to converge and biogeochemical function that is still 23% short after a century, most of the published evidence is drawn from the first few years of the curve.

That matters in a specific direction. If recovery rates fall as a site approaches its reference state, then estimating an annual rate from a short record and extrapolating it forward flatters the outcome. The authors of the 400-study synthesis raise exactly this caveat against their own estimate, noting that a median study duration of about a decade may yield optimistic recovery rates, particularly for systems in later recovery stages.

The taxonomic composition of the evidence compounds this. In the 83-study meta-analysis, 61.4% of biodiversity values came from plants and 28.3% from invertebrates, with vertebrates contributing 4.2% and soil microbes 0.6%. The organisms that respond fastest and are cheapest to survey are the ones the literature is mostly made of.

## The reference state decides the verdict

Notice that the three syntheses above disagree less about ecology than about what they are comparing to. Measured against degraded land, forest restoration raises biodiversity by up to 84%. Measured against old-growth, the same restoration is incomplete. Both statements come from the same dataset.

This is not a technical detail that better methods will resolve. A study in *Conservation Biology* on [baseline specification in conservation evaluation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4241037/) worked through the arithmetic for biodiversity offsets and found that whether an intervention registers as net loss, no net loss or net gain turns on the interaction between the reference frame chosen and the background biodiversity trend of the region. Under a fixed historical baseline, offsets were less likely to reach no net loss where biodiversity was already declining; under a no-development counterfactual, no net loss was reachable *only* where it was declining. Its conclusion is worth stating exactly: "The actual success or failure of an intervention depends on the frame of reference chosen to assess it against," and "the choice of reference frame is ultimately a value judgment, yet there are criteria that should guide reference frame specification."

A reference state is therefore a decision about what a landscape is supposed to be, presented in the grammar of a technical parameter. Choosing a nearby remnant patch, a pre-industrial condition, or the projected trajectory of the site if nothing had been done are three different value positions, and they produce three different verdicts on the same hectares. The broader problem of drifting comparison points is treated in the article on [shifting baselines in biodiversity assessment](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-baselines-and-shifting-baselines).

## Why the counterfactual comparison may be the right one

The case for judging restoration against what would otherwise have happened is serious, and it is not answered by pointing at the reference gap.

If the realistic alternative to restoring a degraded site is continued degradation, then the decision-relevant comparison is restored versus unrestored, not restored versus pristine. On that comparison the evidence is consistent and positive across every synthesis cited here. Judging an intervention against a state that no longer exists anywhere in the region is arguably the wrong test — it evaluates the world rather than the decision. The baseline-specification study does not adjudicate between the two frames; it treats the choice as partly subjective and sets one requirement, that "some kind of frame of reference should be transparently specified and the implications of the choice of frame of reference should be appreciated in advance of the intervention." A declared counterfactual meets that requirement as fully as a declared historical baseline does.

The second argument is about what the recovery curve looks like early on. In the 400-study synthesis, the median response variable started recovery at 10% of its reference value, and the authors read that as recovery being achievable for most damaged ecosystems within decades if the rate held. If the largest share of the gap closes in the years a typical study can afford to watch, then a short record is capturing the informative part of the trajectory, not a misleading fragment of it. The measured rate of slowdown was small — recovery rate fell by 0.026% per year since recovery began — which is a quantified bias rather than an unknown one.

A third argument cuts at the active-restoration result specifically. The 400-study synthesis found that active restoration was not associated with more complete or faster recovery than passive recovery. That reads as a negative result about restoration, but it is a result about *intervention choice*, and a plausible confound is that active restoration is preferentially deployed on the most degraded sites. It does not show that recovery fails; it shows that spending more does not reliably buy a faster trajectory. The distinction matters when the same evidence is used to argue about where a fixed budget should go, which is the subject of the article on [conservation prioritisation under a finite budget](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-trade-offs-and-prioritisation).

## What the evidence cannot settle

The central open question is the shape of the far end of the curve. The wetland analysis shows a gap persisting for a century, but it cannot distinguish between a trajectory that asymptotes permanently below reference and one that continues to close very slowly. Almost no restoration site has been monitored long enough to tell the difference, and the sites that have been are unlikely to be representative — long monitoring records exist where institutions were stable and funding continuous, which is not a random sample of restored land.

Settling it would require what the field has almost none of: continuous monitoring of a cohort of restored sites and their reference sites over 50 to 100 years, with functional measurements rather than species lists, and with the reference sites tracked too, since they move. Nothing about that is technically hard. It is a funding-duration problem.

## A target written in hectares is not a claim about outcomes

Target 2 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits parties to ensure that "by 2030 at least 30 per cent of areas of degraded terrestrial, inland water, and coastal and marine ecosystems are under effective restoration." Its headline indicator is area under restoration. The framing is careful: the target does not require recovery to be complete by 2030, only that effective restoration activities have been initiated, appropriately resourced and monitored.

Given everything above, that is the honest formulation. Nothing in the evidence base would support a target expressed as ecosystems recovered by 2030, because the recovery times are longer than the target period. The failure happens one step downstream, when area under restoration is quoted as area restored — which converts a defensible statement about effort into an undeliverable statement about outcome. The UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration, which frames restoration as "assisting in the recovery of ecosystems that have been degraded or destroyed," attaches its headline benefit estimates to 350 million hectares by 2030, and those estimates inherit the same horizon problem. Area is the tractable thing to count, which is why it becomes the number that travels — the same substitution that afflicts the aggregate indices used to score biodiversity itself, examined in the piece on [what a biodiversity index can carry](/en/insight/biodiversity-numbers-and-what-they-can-support).

Two restoration claims can look like a dispute and contain none. "Forest restoration raised biodiversity by 84%" and "restored forest remains below reference" are the same 221 landscapes, differently framed; "wetland structure recovered in five years" and "wetland structure is 26% short after a century" are the same 621 wetlands, one read in a temperate subset and one at the century horizon. Each pair separates once the comparator, the elapsed time and the measured attribute are on the page — and stays incomprehensible until they are. That is why the strongest reporting convention available to this field is not a better statistic but a fuller sentence. The broader evidence on what restoration achieves is set out in the article on [ecological restoration evidence](/en/ecology/conservation/ecological-restoration-evidence), and the specific case of planted trees — where the gap between area and outcome is widest — in the article on [tree planting](/en/ecology/forests/reforestation-and-tree-planting-evidence).
