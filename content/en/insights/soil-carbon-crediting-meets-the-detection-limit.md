Improved management adds organic carbon to farmland at a rate the FAO's monitoring protocol puts at 0.2 to 0.8 tonnes per hectare per year. In the temperate croplands and grasslands where most of the verification work has been done, the stock it is added to sits somewhere around 60 to 90 tonnes per hectare in the top 30 centimetres. And when a study at eight German cropland and eight grassland sites resampled soil pits after shifting them by the smallest distance physically possible — 20 to 60 centimetres — the stock estimate moved by a mean absolute 5.1 tonnes per hectare on the arable sites and 7.6 on the grasslands, or 7.5 and 8.5 per cent.

Those three numbers are the argument. A five-year accrual at the middle of the FAO range is about 2.5 tonnes per hectare. The error from standing in a slightly different spot is two to three times larger. Anything that resolves the first quantity has to get past the second, and at the scale a credit is issued — one field, one interval between two sampling campaigns — nothing currently does.

## The stock is large, the change is small, and the ground is not uniform

The reason soil is hard is not that laboratories measure carbon badly. Dry combustion on a prepared sample is precise. The difficulty is that a stock estimate is a product of concentration, bulk density, depth and stone content, each measured at a point, and then extrapolated across a field that is heterogeneous at every scale.

That same study measured plot-scale coefficients of variation in carbon stock of about 9 per cent on arable land and 10 per cent on grassland for 0–10 cm, rising to 10 and 15 per cent for 10–30 cm and to 26 and 33 per cent at 30–50 cm. Variability grows with depth exactly where the carbon signal shrinks, which is why deep sampling is both more necessary and less rewarding than it looks. The mechanics of that measurement chain are set out in the companion article on [measuring soil carbon and its uncertainty](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty); what matters here is the ratio of signal to that spread.

Sample handling adds another layer that is easy to overlook because it happens indoors. A 2025 study in *SOIL* compared preparation procedures and external laboratories on identical material and found that not oven-drying before elemental analysis lowered organic carbon by about 5 per cent on average, and that one grinding procedure produced a standard deviation of ±15.0 tonnes per hectare against a mean stock of 27.9 — half the quantity being estimated. Between laboratories, one produced a spread of ±15.4 against a mean of 50.2. None of that is fraud or incompetence. It is what happens when a protocol leaves a step unspecified.

## Two ways to get a different answer from the same soil

The most consequential unspecified step is how a stock is defined. Sampling to a fixed depth multiplies concentration by bulk density over 0–30 cm. If bulk density changes — and it changes with tillage, traffic, wetting and the carbon itself — the mass of soil inside that fixed volume changes too. A compacted field contains more soil in its top 30 centimetres than a loosened one, so a fixed-depth measurement can register a carbon gain that is really a change in how much material was scooped up.

The correction is to compare **equivalent soil mass**: express the stock over a fixed mass of mineral soil rather than a fixed depth. The FAO protocol works a numerical example in which the same before-and-after soils yield a gain of 2.4 tonnes of carbon per hectare on an equivalent-volume basis and about 5.2 tonnes when referred to an equivalent mass of 4,400 tonnes per hectare. Same soil, same laboratory, one convention, more than double the answer. A 2024 analysis in *Carbon Balance and Management* using two long-term cropping trials found that fixed-depth and equivalent-soil-mass estimates of stock change can differ by over 100 per cent, with the difference tracking bulk-density change in the surface layer, and that this fed straight through into how many credits a treatment would have earned.

## What the crediting protocols require, and what they leave open

The same 2024 analysis reports a synthesis of twelve publicly available measurement, reporting and verification protocols. Of those that specify a sampling requirement, three call for or suggest equivalent soil mass; one requires sampling below 30 centimetres. The rest leave the accounting convention to the project developer and stop at 30 centimetres — which is also where the FAO protocol sets its reference depth, and where a change in tillage regime can redistribute carbon rather than add it.

The market has largely responded to the cost of measurement by measuring less. As the *Environmental Research Letters* analysis discussed below observes, measure-and-model now dominates commercial crediting for the straightforward reason that modelling is cheaper than sampling. Verra's VM0042, the improved-agricultural-land-management methodology of the Verified Carbon Standard, is built around biogeochemical modelling, with sampling used to parameterise and check the model rather than to detect the change directly. That is a defensible engineering choice, and the case for it is stronger than it is usually given credit for. A process-based model is calibrated against decades of long-term experiments rather than against two visits to one field; its bias can be tested against withheld sites; and once its uncertainty is quantified, credits can be discounted by that uncertainty, which makes the error conservative by design rather than merely unknown. Sampling then serves to parameterise and audit the model instead of carrying the whole inference.

What modelling does not do is remove the verification problem. It relocates it. The question stops being "can this change be detected in this field?" and becomes "is this model unbiased for this crop, this practice and this soil, and how large is the deduction its residual error warrants?" Both are answerable, but answering them consumes the same field measurements that were too expensive to make in the first place — gathered somewhere, by someone, and usually not by the project being certified.

## The strongest counter-argument is statistical, and it is a good one

The detection limit is a property of a single field, not of an inference. Standard errors fall as the square root of the number of independent units, so a portfolio of many fields can resolve a mean effect that no member of it could resolve alone.

The effect appears even at farm scale. Work in *Frontiers in Environmental Science* on Swiss arable land found minimum detectable changes in carbon content of 0.21 to 0.12 per cent by mass when five fields were pooled, falling to 0.09 to 0.05 per cent across thirty; on a fifteen-field holding, a detection threshold of 0.07 per cent translates to an annual change of 0.014 per cent becoming visible after five years. Aggregation is not a rhetorical escape from the noise. It is the arithmetic of the noise.

This is not a debating point; it has been costed. A 2025 paper in *Environmental Research Letters* modelled a "measure-and-remeasure" design in which a small fraction of enrolled fields are sampled as treated and control units and the treatment effect is estimated across the portfolio. Assuming an average effect of 0.3 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year and a conservative carbon price of $40 per tonne of CO₂, projects above roughly 5,000 fields met the authors' economic-feasibility criterion, with measurement costing about $3 per hectare per year against farmer payments of about $25. Their argument is that direct measurement at portfolio scale is affordable now, and that it makes fewer and more transparent assumptions than modelling does.

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions. Aggregation genuinely solves the statistical problem. But it also changes the object being certified: what is measured is a mean effect across thousands of fields, and the credit attached to any one farm is an allocation of that mean, not a measurement of that farm. Whether that is acceptable is a question about what a credit is supposed to represent — the same structural question raised in the insight on [how offset markets fund the measurement they depend on](/en/insight/carbon-offset-outsourcing-science) — and it is not settled by better soil science.

## What the evidence does not settle

Three things remain genuinely open. The first is depth: almost nothing in the crediting literature measures below 30 centimetres, so whether practice-driven gains in the topsoil are additions to the profile or redistributions within it is largely unmeasured rather than resolved. The second is duration. The FAO protocol requires a minimum of eight consecutive years precisely because shorter windows cannot separate accrual from weather; most commercial crediting periods are shorter. The third is generalisation: the detection arithmetic above comes from temperate croplands and grasslands with good archives, and tropical smallholder systems have neither the baseline data nor the plot sizes that make portfolio-scale sampling work.

None of this is an argument against building soil carbon. The agronomic case for organic matter — water holding, aggregate stability, nutrient supply, the [soil biology it supports](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web) — is one of the older results in [the study of agricultural ecosystems](/en/ecology/ecosystems/agricultural-ecosystems-and-agroecology) and does not depend on resolving a tonne per hectare, and the same practices sit inside the broader [carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained) whether or not anyone issues a certificate for them. The claim here is narrower and harder to escape: a number that cannot be measured at the scale it is sold at is being sold at that scale anyway. For anyone reading a soil-carbon claim, three questions do most of the work — was the stock computed on an equivalent-mass basis, to what depth, and was the reported change measured on this land or allocated to it from a model or a portfolio mean.
