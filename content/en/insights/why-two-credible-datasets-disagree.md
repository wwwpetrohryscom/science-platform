Two reputable organisations publish different numbers for the same thing, and the reader is left to decide which one is lying. Usually neither is. The gap is generated inside the products — by what each counts, where each looks, what each measures against, how each fills the parts it cannot see, and which edition of each is being quoted — and once the responsible difference is identified, the two figures normally turn out to be consistent statements about different questions.

That is not a reassurance. It is a work instruction, because the responsible difference is nearly always documented and nearly always findable.

## Definitions: the forest case

The Food and Agriculture Organization's Global Forest Resources Assessment 2025 reports a world forest area of 4.14 billion hectares, deforestation of 10.9 million hectares a year over 2015–2025, and a net loss of forest area of 4.12 million hectares a year — the residual after 6.78 million hectares a year of forest expansion.

Satellite tree-cover products report much larger annual losses. The foundational global map of forest change, published in *Science* in 2013, found 2.3 million square kilometres of forest loss and 0.8 million square kilometres of gain between 2000 and 2012 at 30-metre resolution: an average of roughly 19 million hectares a year of loss, against the FAO's 13.6 million hectares a year of deforestation for the overlapping period, with the FAO's net figure smaller again.

Both are right. FAO's forest is a **land-use** category — land over 0.5 hectares with trees above 5 metres and canopy cover above 10 per cent, "or trees able to reach these thresholds in situ", explicitly excluding land predominantly under agricultural or urban use. Its explanatory notes include areas "temporarily unstocked due to clear-cutting as part of a forest management practice or natural disasters, and which are expected to be regenerated within 5 years". Its definition of deforestation "specifically excludes areas where the trees have been removed as a result of harvesting or logging, and where the forest is expected to regenerate".

A satellite that measures canopy has no access to intent. A clear-cut coupe destined for replanting is full-height forest to FAO and total cover loss to the optical product. The *Science* paper says as much in its own abstract — intensive forestry in subtropical forests produced the highest rates of forest change globally in its map.

The published argument about that dataset landed on precisely this point. A 2014 Comment in *Science* held that the product "does not distinguish tropical forests from plantations and even herbaceous crops", compromising its value for local policy. The authors' Response was that both criticisms "suffer from a miscomprehension of the definition of forest employed as well as the requirements of product validation". Whatever one makes of that exchange, its subject was not the imagery. It was what the word meant. The same fault line runs through the reporting of [why credible sources publish different deforestation figures](/en/ecology/forests/deforestation-statistics-explained) and through the separate problem that [degradation is not deforestation and is much harder to see](/en/ecology/forests/forest-degradation-vs-deforestation).

## System boundaries: a 6.7 GtCO₂ gap that was almost entirely definitional

The sharpest demonstration comes from land-use carbon accounting, because someone did the arithmetic on the reconciliation.

Global bookkeeping models estimate that land use was a net source of 4.8 GtCO₂ per year over 2000–2020. National greenhouse gas inventories, submitted by governments under the Paris Agreement, estimate a net sink of −1.9 GtCO₂ per year for the same period. That is a gap of 6.7 GtCO₂ a year in the sign as well as the size — comparable to the annual emissions of a large economy, on a quantity central to whether countries are meeting their commitments.

A 2023 study in *Earth System Science Data* traced it. Most of the difference — 3.5 GtCO₂ a year — sits on forest land, and arises because countries treat a broader area as managed than the models do, and because the indirect effects of environmental change such as CO₂ fertilisation are counted as non-anthropogenic by the models and as anthropogenic by most inventories. When the model estimates were adjusted to include natural sinks in the areas countries call managed, the residual global gap fell to 0.3 GtCO₂ a year.

A 6.7 GtCO₂ discrepancy that collapses to 0.3 GtCO₂ once the boundary is aligned is not a scientific dispute at all. It is two answers to two questions that were never the same question.

## Coverage, infilling, baseline and version, in one product family

Surface temperature demonstrates the remaining causes together, and it demonstrates them within single products rather than between rivals.

HadCRUT5 is issued in two forms. The non-infilled version averages observations onto a grid and provides "no value ... in grid cells containing no observations". The analysis version applies a statistical method "to extend coverage in data sparse areas and provide a more globally complete data set". The two are the same measurements. They differ on whether an unobserved Arctic grid cell is treated as absent or as estimated — and because the Arctic has warmed faster than the global mean, that choice alone moves the global figure.

NOAAGlobalTemp shows the version axis. Version 5.1 achieved "complete coverage of all land and ocean areas for the entire period of record" and substituted near-surface air temperature for sea surface temperature over the Arctic Ocean. Version 6.0 replaced the earlier empirical orthogonal teleconnections gap-filling with an artificial neural network. Version 6.1.0 ingests a new sea surface temperature reconstruction and switches the anomaly reference period to the current WMO standard normals of 1991–2020.

Three of the five causes are visible in that sequence. A figure quoted from version 5.0 and a figure quoted from version 6.1.0 are not the same estimate, and an anomaly stated against 1991–2020 is close to a whole degree smaller than the identical physical state stated against 1850–1900 — the IPCC assesses global surface temperature over 2001–2020 as 0.99 [0.84 to 1.10] °C above the earlier period. None of this is concealed; it sits in the release notes and the assessment footnotes.

The IPCC put a number on the same effect at assessment scale. Of the increase in assessed global warming between its fifth and sixth reports, "methodological advances and new datasets contributed approximately 0.1 °C". A tenth of a degree of the difference between two IPCC reports came from the products rather than from the atmosphere, and the report says so. The construction of these series is set out at length in the explainer on [how global temperature records are built](/en/ecology/climate-change/global-temperature-records-explained).

## The two wrong readings

The first wrong reading is that a discrepancy discredits both products. The land-flux case is the counterexample: the two estimates were 6.7 GtCO₂ apart and both were competently produced, and the reconciliation confirmed rather than undermined them. Divergence between well-documented products is ordinary and usually diagnosable. Treating it as evidence of general unreliability discards the most informative thing in the comparison, which is the size and location of the difference.

The second wrong reading is to select the convenient value. Because the products answer different questions, choosing between them without stating the difference is choosing a question and presenting it as a fact. Quoting FAO's net forest change to argue that little forest is being lost, or satellite gross cover loss to argue that vastly more is being lost than governments admit, are the same manoeuvre in opposite directions.

## The four questions, and the case they do not cover

Before concluding anything about the world from a difference between two figures, four questions settle most of it.

**What exactly is counted?** Gross or net; stock or flux; land use or land cover; which minimum thresholds. This is the largest single source of apparent disagreement, and it is the first place to look.

**What is covered?** Which regions, which depths, which years, and what happens where there are no observations. A product that infills and a product that does not are answering differently about the same places.

**What is it measured against?** An anomaly without its baseline period, or a change without its reference state, is not a number yet.

**Which version?** Reanalyses and reconstructions are reissued, sometimes with method changes large enough to alter a ranking, and figures quoted across editions can manufacture a trend that no dataset contains.

There is a residual category these questions do not resolve, and it should not be talked away. Sometimes a difference persists after every boundary is aligned — the 0.3 GtCO₂ residual in the land-flux comparison is small globally but larger in some regions — and sometimes one product simply has a defect that the comparison is the first thing to reveal. Both outcomes are found by the same forensic process; the difference is that the first is informative about the world and the second is informative about an instrument. Assessments that judge [what a satellite product can and cannot resolve](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty), or that reconcile [two assessments of the same fish stock](/en/ecology/oceans/fisheries-science-and-stock-assessment), spend most of their effort on exactly this separation.

None of this is a reason to distrust the figures. It is a reason to carry the qualifier with the number, which is the same discipline examined from the other end in the companion piece on [where uncertainty gets dropped between the dataset and the headline](/en/insight/uncertainty-lost-between-dataset-and-headline). A number without its definition is not a more convenient number. It is a different one.
