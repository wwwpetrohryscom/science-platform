An event-attribution study estimates how much more likely, or how much more severe, a defined class of weather event has become under human influence on the climate. It does not establish that a particular storm was caused by warming, and it does not divide the losses between climate and everything else — the two things its numbers are most often asked to do.

The mechanics are set out in the explainer on [how event attribution is performed](/en/ecology/climate-change/extreme-weather-attribution-basics). What follows is the gap between what the method produces and what its numbers are made to carry.

## The event is a specification, not a thing that happened

Before any model runs, someone decides what the event is — a region, a duration, a variable, a threshold. Was the July 2021 flood "72-hour rainfall over this river basin" or "24-hour rainfall at this gauge"? Both are defensible; they are not the same quantity and do not return the same answer.

The IPCC's assessment of weather and climate extremes is explicit that the outcome depends on the event definition and on the framing, and that attribution statements vary with the spatial and temporal extent chosen, because "large-scale averages generally yield higher attributable changes in magnitude or probability due to the smoothing out of noise."

A risk ratio is a property of a specification as much as of the atmosphere. Two competent teams analysing what the newspapers call the same event can report different multipliers without either being wrong.

## One heatwave, two papers, no contradiction

The clearest demonstration is the July 2010 western Russian heatwave. A [2011 analysis in *Geophysical Research Letters*](https://doi.org/10.1029/2010GL046582) concluded the event was mainly natural in origin: internal variability could produce an anomaly of that magnitude. A [paper in *PNAS* the same year](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3207670/) counted records instead, finding that the observed warming trend had roughly quintupled the July heat records expected in Moscow over the preceding decade, implying "an approximate 80% probability that the 2010 July heat record would not have occurred without climate warming". It said in terms that this contradicted the first. Note what that 80 per cent is: a property of a trend in one station series, from a method whose authors add that it says nothing about the physical causes of that trend.

A [third paper, in 2012](https://doi.org/10.1029/2011GL050422), showed there was no substantive contradiction. Using a large atmospheric-model ensemble, it found the same event can be "both mostly internally-generated in terms of magnitude and mostly externally-driven in terms of occurrence-probability", and concluded that the episode "illustrates the importance of specifying precisely what question is being asked."

This is also where the most common misreading lives. A risk ratio of 5 says that events in the defined class occur five times as often in the modelled present as in the modelled counterfactual. It does not say that the event was four-fifths caused by warming.

## Confidence is not uniform across the weather

[Climate attribution](/en/glossary/climate-attribution) is often discussed as a single capability. Assessed confidence varies enormously with the kind of weather involved.

| Event class | Assessed human influence | What limits the confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot extremes | Greenhouse-gas forcing is the main driver of the observed changes globally (*virtually certain*) and on most continents (*very likely*); the increase itself is at least *likely* in over 80% of AR6 regions | Little — a thermodynamic, large-scale, well-observed signal |
| Heavy precipitation | Human influence *likely* the main driver of the global-scale intensification over land; projected intensification tracks moisture capacity, about 7% per °C | Circulation-driven rather than moisture-driven events |
| Agricultural and ecological drought | Human-induced climate change has contributed to increases in some regions (*medium confidence*), via evapotranspiration rather than rainfall trends | A compound variable, sparse soil-moisture data, water management |
| Tropical cyclones | *Medium confidence* for a human contribution to extreme cyclone rainfall; the global proportion of Category 3–5 instances has *likely* risen over four decades, while total formation frequency is projected to fall or hold steady (*medium confidence*) | A short reliable record; frequency and intensity diverge |
| Severe convective storms | *Low confidence* in past trends in hail and severe winds | Storm-scale dynamics fall below the model grid |

Those are the IPCC Working Group I assessments. Its plain-language answer to whether climate change caused a particular event is blunter: attribution of certain classes, tornadoes among them, is "beyond current modelling and theoretical capabilities." A 2026 consensus study from the U.S. National Academies reaches the same ordering a decade after its first assessment of the field, and defines its weak end the same way: phenomena with short observational records, or that turn on small-scale dynamics global models resolve poorly. That limitation also makes [cloud and convective processes](/en/physics/climate-physics/convection-and-cloud-formation) hard to represent, and attribution inherits it along with the [uncertainties in the underlying projections](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-models-projections-uncertainty).

At the strong end the method is powerful. The rapid study of the June 2021 heat in western North America found that an event of that rarity — about one in a thousand years in today's climate — would have been at least 150 times rarer without human-induced warming, and roughly 2°C cooler. That study is candid that its numbers assume the heatwave was a very low-probability draw rather than the product of nonlinearities the models do not show.

## Hold the circulation fixed, or let it move

The real disagreement in the field is not whether warming has changed extremes. It is how much of the observed situation to hold fixed.

The unconditional, probability-based approach lets circulation respond and asks how the odds of the event class changed. The conditional or **storyline** approach prescribes the observed large-scale atmospheric pattern and asks how much worse the event was made given those dynamics. The IPCC makes the case for conditioning itself: events whose dynamics stretch current-generation models limit the applicability of the probability-based approach, whereas the storyline route "does not depend on the model's ability to represent the circulation reliably". It also reaches events too rare to analyse otherwise, or where the atmospheric set-up was central to the impact, and it permits very high-resolution simulations the unconditional method cannot afford.

The case on the other side is more concrete than it is usually made to sound. Only the unconditional framing returns a change in probability — the quantity risk pricing and loss-and-damage eligibility ask for. The model-evaluation criticism that motivated storylines was answered rather than conceded: the same assessment records methods for quantifying statistical uncertainty and for evaluating models against observations, since used to test the robustness of probability-based results. And conditioning is not neutral, because the fixed aspects may themselves have been affected by warming. In the hindcast approach often applied to tropical cyclones, the specified initial conditions "permit only a conditional statement about the magnitude of the storm", precluding any attribution statement about the change in frequency if used in isolation — though pairing conditional intensity results with a multi-model approach recovers it.

Neither framing is correct in general; each answers a different question. A [2025 paper in *Weather*](https://doi.org/10.1002/wea.7779) argues for combining results across methods and for explicitly defining which research questions each can answer, and where each stops.

## Where the hazard ends and the harm begins

The larger stretch is between the weather and the damage. Southern Madagascar's food crisis of 2021 was widely reported as a climate-change famine. The rapid attribution analysis found the two rainy seasons had each delivered about 60 per cent of normal rainfall, the 24-month deficit was roughly a one-in-135-year dry spell, and its likelihood had not significantly increased because of human-caused climate change. What it identified instead was a population around 90 per cent of which lived below the poverty line, dependent on rain-fed subsistence agriculture and hit at once by pandemic restrictions and pests. The hazard was real; the attributable climate signal was not; the catastrophe was built largely from exposure and vulnerability.

That is the general case, not the exception. Exposure and vulnerability determine the magnitude of a disaster given the hazard, which is why the IPCC says a specific disaster is difficult to attribute directly to climate change even when the meteorology is well attributed. A result about rainfall is not a result about a flood, and a result about a flood is not a result about the deaths.

## What the numbers are being asked to do next

Two uses press hardest. The first is loss-and-damage finance. A [2024 paper in *PNAS Nexus*](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/3/8/pgae277/7727702) makes the strongest case for using attribution now rather than waiting for it to mature: the map of published studies is blank across much of the tropics and the southern hemisphere, precisely where vulnerability is highest, so making quantitative attribution an eligibility test would penalise the countries with the fewest weather stations. Its proposal separates the levels — qualitative, physics-based assessment for international eligibility, quantitative multi-method analysis for national allocation — on the principle that a lack of evidence can never on its own be grounds to deny funding. On its own terms that holds.

The second is liability. A [2025 paper in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08751-3) sets out an end-to-end framework linking companies' scope 1 and 3 emissions to specific harms, illustrated with a figure of between US$791 billion and US$3.6 trillion in heat-related economic losses very likely caused by emissions linked to Chevron over 1991–2020. Notice which hazard the argument runs through. Heat is the class where every link — emissions accounting, thermodynamic response, damage function — is strongest. Run it through a convective outbreak or a regional drought and the same machinery inherits the weakest link.

## The weak end is a resolution problem

The National Academies report is specific about why. Many global climate models still run at horizontal grid spacings of about 50–300 km, which limits their use for regional and complex events, so high-resolution model development heads its priorities, with a common framework of agreed best practices, periodic peer review of the routine rapid studies that publish within days of a disaster, and targeted international investment in observational data and training in under-represented regions. Only the last is mostly a funding problem; the convective-storm limit is physics and computing, and will not close quickly.

## Four questions before repeating a multiplier

For anyone quoting a figure, four questions do the work. How was the event defined — which region, which window, which variable? Was the analysis conditioned on the observed circulation? Is the number a change in probability or in intensity, which are not interchangeable? And is the claim about the hazard or about the damage? That last distinction is where most overreach happens, and the one a headline is least likely to preserve — the discipline that applies wherever [two credible sources report different values](/en/insight/why-two-credible-datasets-disagree) for one quantity.
