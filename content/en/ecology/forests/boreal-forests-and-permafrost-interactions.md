Two numbers, from two different accounting systems, are worth putting side by side. FAO's 2025 assessment estimates the carbon in all the world's forests, across every pool it recognises, at 714 gigatonnes. NOAA's Arctic Report Card puts the carbon accumulated in terrestrial soils and permafrost across the northern permafrost region — 16.6 million square kilometres of tundra and boreal land — at no less than 1.4 to 1.6 trillion tonnes.

The two are measuring different things, and that is the point. A northern forest is a thin layer of wood standing on a very deep bank of cold organic matter, and the instruments and conventions built for forestry are aimed at the wood. Where the wider category boundaries come from is set out in [the definitions behind forest statistics](/en/ecology/forests/forest-ecosystems-explained).

## What a forest inventory counts, and how far down

The global forest carbon stock breaks into pools unevenly: soil organic carbon 329 Gt, or 46 per cent of the total; aboveground biomass 247 Gt, 35 per cent; belowground biomass 65.9 Gt, 9 per cent; litter 41.1 Gt, 6 per cent; deadwood 30.3 Gt, 4 per cent. Soil is already the largest single pool globally. In the north it is not close.

The reporting convention is where this becomes a measurement problem rather than an ecological one. Each country chooses the depth to which it reports soil organic carbon. The forest-area-weighted average depth used globally is 41 cm, and the regional spread runs from 30 cm in Asia and Oceania and 32 cm in Europe to 70 cm in North and Central America. Soil carbon was reported by 77 countries and areas covering 70 per cent of global forest area; for the rest, values were derived from a global soil-carbon grid for a depth of 0–30 cm.

A convention that stops between 30 and 70 cm is workable where most soil carbon sits in the upper profile. It is not workable where metres of frozen peat and cryoturbated organic material lie below, which is the ordinary condition across much of the boreal zone. The result is not an error in the assessment — it reports its own depth convention plainly — but it does mean the published forest soil-carbon figure and the permafrost-region soil-carbon figure cannot be added, subtracted, or compared. The wider problem of measuring this pool is treated in [soil carbon measurement and its uncertainty](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty).

| Accounting frame | Spatial unit | What it includes | What it leaves out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| National forest inventory (FAO) | Land classified as forest | Living biomass, deadwood, litter, soil to a country-chosen depth | Soil below that depth; non-forest tundra and peatland; disturbance flux |
| Permafrost-region flux synthesis (NOAA) | Tundra and boreal land within the permafrost region | Net ecosystem exchange plus direct fire emissions, CO₂ and CH₄ | Land outside the permafrost region; standing stock accounting |
| Forest plot synthesis | Boreal, temperate and tropical forest plots | Change in living biomass carbon over repeated censuses | Soil and permafrost pools; land not under plot coverage |

## The insulating layer is the control variable

What keeps permafrost frozen under a forest is not the air temperature alone. Mosses, lichens and the accumulated organic mat above the mineral soil are poor conductors of heat, and when dry they are poorer still; that layer, together with winter snow cover, sets how much summer warmth reaches the frozen ground and how much winter cold is retained. Thickness of the organic layer, rather than latitude, is often what separates a stand with permafrost close to the surface from one without.

The thermal record shows what happens as the climate warms above it. Across long-term Alaskan monitoring boreholes at 15–20 m depth, permafrost temperature rose by 0.3 to 0.7 °C per decade over four decades in the cold permafrost of the North Slope, and by 0.02 to 0.3 °C per decade in the warmer permafrost of Interior Alaska. The slower warming of the warmer sites is not reassurance: ground near 0 °C absorbs energy melting ice before its temperature can rise, so the latent-heat sink masks the change until it is exhausted. In 2024, permafrost temperatures were the highest on record at 9 of 20 Alaskan long-term sites. The same class of latent-heat and insulation effects appears throughout [the cryosphere in the Earth system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/cryosphere-in-the-earth-system).

## Fire acts on the ground, not just the canopy

Boreal fire is a soil process. Most of the carbon released comes from combustion of the organic layer rather than from the trees, and each fire that leaves part of that layer unburned allows carbon to accumulate across successive fire cycles. Soil radiocarbon dating after the 2014 Northwest Territories fires tested whether that arrangement still holds. In stands older than the historic fire-return interval of northwestern boreal forests, there was no evidence that older, deeper carbon burned. In dry landscapes where the stand was under 60 years old at the time of the fire, it did — roughly 0.34 million hectares of young forest in those fires could have experienced combustion of carbon that had survived the previous fire cycle. Shorten the interval between fires and the accumulated bank starts to be spent.

Fire also removes the insulation. In western Canadian permafrost peatlands, wildfire effects on the soil thermal regime persist for about 30 years, producing a warmer, deeper active layer and continuously thawed zones within the frozen ground, and are associated with a tripled rate of thermokarst bog expansion along permafrost edges. The attributed area is 2,200 ± 1,500 km² of thermokarst bog development over 30 years in the study region, about a quarter of all such expansion there.

Whether the forest returns as forest is a further question. A continental assessment of post-fire black spruce — the dominant tree across boreal North America — found resilience to be the most common outcome, but drier conditions and more severe fire consistently undermined it, in some cases producing complete regeneration failure. The general logic of disturbance regimes and their interactions is set out in [fire, insects and wind as ordinary processes](/en/ecology/forests/forest-disturbance-fire-insects-windthrow).

## Sink or source, and what is genuinely contested

Circumpolar wildfire emissions have averaged 207 teragrams of carbon per year since 2003. The largest years in that record are 2003, at 343 Tg C, and 2023, at 478 Tg C; in the latter, fires in the Canadian permafrost region burned more than twice the area of any previous year on record and emitted 381 Tg C. Over a longer view, burned area in the Alaskan and Canadian permafrost regions across 2004–2023 was 28 per cent higher than the 1981–2000 average and 62 per cent higher than the 1971–1990 average.

Against that, the regional carbon budget is closer to balanced than the fire numbers alone suggest. Accounting for net ecosystem exchange and fire together, the pan-Arctic permafrost region was CO₂ neutral over 2001–2020, with a budget of −24 ± 123 Tg C per year. Within that total the components diverge: the tundra region has shifted from a CO₂ sink, which it had been for millennia, to a small source, while the boreal region remains a sink. The permafrost region is a consistent methane source at 15 to 39 Tg CH₄-C per year over 2000–2020.

The forest-side evidence adds a trend the flux budget does not resolve. A synthesis of in-situ plot data across three decades found the boreal forest carbon sink down 36 ± 6 per cent, attributed to intensified disturbance — a large relative decline in a component that is, by the flux accounting above, still a sink. "Boreal forests are a sink" and "the boreal sink has weakened by a third" are both supported, and neither is a rebuttal of the other. The broader mechanics of sink weakening are covered in [carbon-cycle feedbacks](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-cycle-feedbacks).

## Why the uncertainty here is structural

The ±123 Tg C on a mean of −24 is the honest summary of this field: the sign of the regional budget is not established, and a term of that size cannot be resolved by adding stations. Site-level eddy-covariance records — the 13 long-term sites reported for 2024 had between 6 and 17 years of data each — are explicitly not representative of the biomes they sit in, and disturbance at regional scale is excluded from them altogether.

The projection side is weaker still. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment states with high confidence that thawing terrestrial permafrost will release carbon, but with low confidence in the timing, the magnitude, and the relative roles of CO₂ and methane. The assessed model ensemble puts CO₂ release from permafrost at 3–41 PgC per 1 °C of global warming by 2100 — a range spanning more than an order of magnitude — and attributes the low confidence to the incomplete representation of abrupt thaw processes combined with weak observational constraints. That is a different epistemic situation from the tropical plot record examined in [what maintains tropical diversity](/en/ecology/forests/tropical-forest-ecology), where the measurements are dense and the argument is about interpretation. Here the measurements themselves are sparse relative to the pool being measured, and the pool is very large.
