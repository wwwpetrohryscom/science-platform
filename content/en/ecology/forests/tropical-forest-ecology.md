Compiled records from 1,170 tree plots across the Amazon Basin and Guiana Shield put roughly 16,000 tree species in lowland Amazonia — and found that 227 of them, 1.4 per cent of the flora, account for half of all the individual trees. The authors of that 2013 synthesis noted the consequence: a group of species less diverse than the tree flora of North America makes up half of the most diverse tree community on Earth.

Those two facts frame the whole subject. The interesting question about a lowland tropical stand is not how many species it holds but why the common ones have not eliminated the rest, and why the rare ones — the overwhelming majority of the list — persist at densities that classical competition theory says should have driven them out. The definitional groundwork for what counts as forest at all sits in [the thresholds that decide forest statistics](/en/ecology/forests/forest-ecosystems-explained); what follows assumes it.

## Three explanations, and why they are not alternatives

The first mechanism was proposed independently around 1970 by Daniel Janzen and Joseph Connell. Seeds and seedlings that fall near a parent tree sit in a zone dense with that species' own herbivores and pathogens, so survival improves with distance from conspecific adults. The effect is a **negative density dependence** specific to the species itself: whenever a species becomes locally common, its own recruitment suffers. That is a stabilising mechanism in the technical sense — it gives every species an advantage when rare.

The second is straightforward niche partitioning. Tropical stands are heterogeneous in light, soil texture, phosphorus, drainage and topography, and species sort along those axes. Gap dynamics add a temporal axis: the light environment after a treefall is unlike anything under a closed canopy, and species differ in how fast they exploit it.

The third, neutral theory, was formalised by Stephen Hubbell in 2001 and takes the opposite tack. Assume species are demographically equivalent — same birth and death rates — and let dispersal limitation, random drift in abundances, and speciation generate the observed diversity and abundance distributions without any niche differences at all. Neutral models reproduce species-abundance curves surprisingly well, which is why they matter: a model with no coexistence mechanism can fit the pattern that coexistence mechanisms were invoked to explain.

The three are not mutually exclusive. The live disagreement is about their relative weight, and that is a quantitative question the plot networks were built to answer.

## What the plot networks have been able to test

The strongest test to date of the Janzen–Connell prediction used dynamic mortality data — repeated censuses of who died, rather than a single snapshot of who is standing — across 23 forest sites spanning temperate and tropical latitudes. Earlier analyses had relied on static data, and the 2024 assessment attributes part of the earlier support for a latitudinal gradient to the methodological limitations of that approach.

The result was mixed in an informative way. Stabilising conspecific negative density dependence was present at all but one of the 23 sites, so the mechanism is real and general. But averaged across species it was *not* stronger toward the tropics, which is the prediction the classical hypothesis makes. What did differ by latitude was the relationship with abundance: in tropical communities, rare and intermediately abundant species experienced stronger stabilising density dependence than common ones, while in temperate forests no such relationship appeared. Interspecific variation in the strength of the effect was high everywhere and did not differ significantly with latitude.

That is a more specific claim than the textbook version, and a weaker one. It supports density dependence as a regulator of relative abundance in the tropics — which is precisely the hyperdominance pattern — without supporting the simple story that the tropics are diverse because enemy pressure is stronger there. The relationship to abundance measures, and why a count of species is a poor summary of a community like this, is developed in [species evenness and diversity](/en/ecology/biodiversity/species-evenness-and-diversity).

## Fertility is not the explanation

A common misconception treats tropical luxuriance as evidence of rich soil. The opposite is closer to the truth over much of the lowland tropics, where soils are deeply weathered and their phosphorus has been leached or locked into forms plants cannot easily access. Nutrient capital sits largely in the living biomass and in a fast recycling loop through litter, fine roots and mycorrhizal associations, not in a mineral reserve.

How hard that limitation bites was ambiguous for a long time, because fertilisation experiments returned responses to nitrogen as large as to phosphorus and productivity appeared to hold up across soil phosphorus gradients. A nutrient-addition experiment established in old-growth Amazon forest on soil representative of about 60 per cent of the basin resolved part of it: net primary productivity increased with phosphorus addition and with nothing else. After two years the response appeared in fine-root production, up 29 per cent, and canopy productivity, up 19 per cent, but not in stem growth. The implication reaches beyond nutrition — the authors note that if phosphorus limits productivity it may also restrict how much these forests can respond to rising atmospheric CO₂, which is one of the larger unknowns in projecting the tropical land sink. The general treatment of nutrient limitation is in [nutrient availability and soil fertility](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-availability-and-soil-fertility).

## The forest as part of its own water supply

Amazonian rainfall is not simply delivered from the Atlantic. High-resolution tracking of transpired water through the atmosphere estimates that about one-third of rainfall in the basin originates within the basin itself, and that two-thirds of that recycled fraction has passed through tree transpiration rather than evaporating from surfaces. Forests in the southern half of the basin contribute most to the stability of forests elsewhere; those in the south-west depend most on subsidies from upwind.

The observational counterpart has now been measured. Combining satellite records with atmospheric moisture tracking over 1980–2019, one analysis finds a north–south dipole in Amazonian precipitation trends, with the decline in the southern basin reaching up to 3.9–5.4 mm per year each year — an 8 to 11 per cent fall in annual rainfall across the record — and attributes 52 to 72 per cent of that decline to deforestation in the southern basin and upwind. The same work reports that climate models underestimate the sensitivity of precipitation to forest loss, which matters directly for how [tipping elements in the Earth system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-tipping-points) are assessed. The physical machinery of the recycling loop belongs to [the global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained).

## A sink that has already turned

Forty-five per cent of the world's forest area lies in the tropical domain, and it is the domain in which FAO's 2025 assessment records sharp declines in growing stock, biomass and carbon since 2000, against significant gains in the boreal and temperate domains over the same period. National reporting of that kind captures area and stock. It does not capture what is happening inside a stand that is still standing, and for that the plot networks are the only long record available.

Long-term plot measurement has overturned an assumption built into most vegetation models. Analysis of biomass dynamics across 321 Amazonian plots over three decades confirmed a long-term net biomass sink but found the rate of net increase down by one-third in the most recent decade relative to the 1990s. The cause was not falling growth — growth increases had levelled off while biomass mortality rose persistently, shortening the time carbon spends in a tree.

Africa's forests behaved differently. Across 244 intact African plots in 11 countries, the aboveground carbon sink held steady for the three decades to 2015 at 0.66 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year (95 per cent confidence interval 0.53–0.79), with no detectable multi-decadal trend in mortality. The two continents have diverged. Taken together, the assessment is that uptake into Earth's intact tropical forests peaked in the 1990s, with a statistical model projecting the African sink 14 per cent smaller by 2030 and the Amazonian sink close to zero.

## What the plot record cannot settle

Three limits are worth holding onto. The networks are unevenly built: the African synthesis rests on fewer plots than the Amazonian one — 244 against 321 — and includes clusters first censused only in the 2010s, often over a single census interval, and neither continent is sampled in proportion to its variation in soil, climate and disturbance history. Mortality — the term that drove the Amazonian decline — is the harder of the two flux components to estimate from periodic censuses, because a tree that dies and decays between visits leaves a weaker record than one that grew. And the forward projections are statistical extrapolations of relationships fitted to the observed period, not process models; they inherit whatever the fitted period did not contain.

None of that undercuts the direction of the finding, which is corroborated by independent atmospheric evidence. It does mean the numbers attached to it will move as networks lengthen and coverage fills in. How the underlying biomass figures are produced in the first place, and where their error comes from, is the subject of [measuring forest carbon](/en/ecology/forests/forest-carbon-measurement); the contrasting case of a biome whose carbon is mostly below ground rather than in wood is [the boreal forest](/en/ecology/forests/boreal-forests-and-permafrost-interactions).
