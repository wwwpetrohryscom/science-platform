Three institutions can publish three different figures for the world's forest area in the same year and all three can be defensible. The disagreement is rarely about measurement. It is about where the thresholds sit, and about whether the thing being counted is a land use or a layer of vegetation seen from orbit.

That is why this page begins with definitions rather than with ecology. Most apparent contradictions in published forest statistics dissolve once you know which definition produced each number — and the same thresholds also decide what counts as loss, what counts as gain, and what the accounting cannot see at all.

## The thresholds that turn a stand of trees into a statistic

FAO's Global Forest Resources Assessment, the reference series for national forest reporting since the late 1940s, defines **forest** as land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 metres and a canopy cover of more than 10 percent, or trees able to reach these thresholds in situ, excluding land that is predominantly under agricultural or urban use.

Two clauses in that sentence do most of the work. The first is potential rather than presence: a young stand that has not yet reached 5 metres qualifies, and so does a clear-felled coupe expected to regenerate within about five years. Nothing has to be standing for the land to count. The second is the land-use test, which is why rubber-wood, cork oak and Christmas tree plantations are inside the category while oil palm plantations, fruit orchards and olive groves are outside it — a distinction with no basis in canopy structure whatsoever.

Immediately below the threshold sits **other wooded land**: canopy cover of 5 to 10 percent, or a combined cover of shrubs, bushes and trees above 10 percent. The 2025 assessment puts this at 1.09 billion hectares, 8 percent of global land area, and says plainly that the 5–10 percent canopy band is the range countries find hardest to measure, so its totals should be read with caution. The European Environment Agency, reporting on a continent where the two categories are usually quoted together, describes forests and other wooded areas as covering around 40 percent of the European land surface — a figure that is not comparable with a forest-only figure for anywhere else.

Other frameworks draw the line elsewhere, legitimately. Under the Kyoto Protocol's Marrakesh Accords, each industrialised party selects for its own carbon accounting a single minimum crown cover between 10 and 30 percent, a single minimum area between 0.05 and 1.0 hectare, and a single minimum tree height between 2 and 5 metres. Two neighbouring states can classify the same hillside differently and both be compliant. What that does to reported rates of loss is the subject of [why deforestation figures diverge between credible sources](/en/ecology/forests/deforestation-statistics-explained).

## Structure is the axis along which most function is arranged

Beneath the definitional argument is a real physical object with a distinctive property: it is organised vertically. Light is intercepted in a gradient from an upper canopy down through subcanopy and understorey layers to a floor that may receive a small percentage of incident radiation; temperature, humidity and wind speed follow that gradient; and the standing dead wood, coarse woody debris and rooting zone below extend the same arrangement into pools that are slow to turn over. Almost everything a wooded ecosystem does — buffer microclimate, hold water, store carbon, provide the nesting and foraging strata that generate habitat diversity — is a consequence of that vertical arrangement rather than of tree cover as such.

The global inventory quantifies it thinly but usefully. Growing stock, the volume of living trees, stood at 630 billion cubic metres in 2025, an average of 152 cubic metres per hectare, with roughly a third of the total in primary forest. Biomass is put at 709 gigatonnes, about 171 tonnes per hectare. Per-hectare averages of this kind hide a range spanning more than an order of magnitude between a dry open woodland and a lowland tropical stand, which is why structural measurements — canopy height, vertical profile, basal area — are the quantities instruments actually record, with mass inferred from them afterwards. That inference chain is unpacked in [how forest carbon stocks are actually estimated](/en/ecology/forests/forest-carbon-measurement).

## Where the world's forests are, and what kind they are

Forest covers 4.14 billion hectares, close to a third of global land area and about 0.50 hectares per person. Forty-five percent of it lies in the tropical domain, followed by the boreal at 28 percent, the temperate at 17 percent and the subtropical at 11 percent. Concentration is extreme: five countries — the Russian Federation, Brazil, Canada, the United States and China — hold 54 percent between them, and the ten largest hold about two-thirds.

The categories used to describe what kind of forest it is are nested rather than mutually exclusive, which is a frequent source of double counting when figures are lifted out of context.

| Category | Area in 2025 | Share of forest area | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Naturally regenerating | 3.83 billion ha | 92% | Predominantly established by natural regeneration, whether or not it has been managed |
| Primary forest | at least 1.18 billion ha | 29% | A subcategory of the above: native species, no clearly visible human impact, ecological processes not significantly disturbed |
| Planted forest | 312 million ha | 8% | Established by planting or deliberate seeding |
| Plantation forest | about half of planted area | ~4% | A subcategory of planted forest: intensively managed, one or two species, even-aged, regularly spaced |
| Within protected areas | 813 million ha | 20% | Legally established protection, cutting across every category above |

Primary forest is being lost at 1.61 million hectares a year over 2015–2025, less than half the 3.92 million hectares a year recorded for 2000–2015. The regional composition is counterintuitive: Europe holds the largest area of primary forest at 311 million hectares, ahead of South America and North and Central America, because the Russian Federation's boreal stands dominate the European total. What those stands are like, and why their carbon sits mostly below ground, is treated in [the boreal forests and their frozen soils](/en/ecology/forests/boreal-forests-and-permafrost-interactions); the very different question of what sustains several hundred tree species in a few hectares of lowland tropics is taken up in [how tropical diversity is maintained](/en/ecology/forests/tropical-forest-ecology).

## Disturbance is the normal condition, not the exception

Stand-replacing and partial disturbances are intrinsic to how these systems work, and a forest with no disturbance history is the unusual case rather than the reference state. Fire is the clearest example at global scale: an average of 261 million hectares of land was affected annually over 2007–2019, of which almost half was forested, and in 2019 about 123 million hectares of forest burned, 79 percent of it in the subtropical domain and only 8 percent in the boreal. Insects, diseases and severe weather damaged roughly 41 million hectares in 2020, concentrated in temperate and boreal regions.

What follows a disturbance is the second half of the same process. A gap in the canopy resets the light environment, and the sequence of colonisation, competition and gradual replacement that follows — the general case is covered in [how ecological succession works](/en/ecology/ecosystems/ecological-succession-explained) — is what produces the age-class mosaic that most temperate and boreal landscapes actually consist of. A mature closed canopy is one stage in that sequence rather than the destination of it, which is why "recovery" and "return to the pre-disturbance state" are not synonyms.

Those figures need reading carefully. Reporting on insect damage in 2020 came from 60 countries covering 49 percent of the world's forest area, and the single largest contribution — 17.8 million hectares in Canada — is flagged in the assessment as potentially double- or triple-counted because a given area may be mapped for more than one insect species at once. The compilers state that patchy data made it impossible to estimate the total area affected by disturbance globally at all. Disturbance regimes, their climate linkages and the compound cases are examined in [fire, insects and wind as ordinary forest processes](/en/ecology/forests/forest-disturbance-fire-insects-windthrow).

## What forests are doing to the atmosphere

A synthesis of in-situ inventory and plot data across boreal, temperate and tropical biomes puts the global forest [carbon sink](/en/glossary/carbon-sink) at 3.6 ± 0.4 petagrams of carbon per year in the 1990s and 2000s and 3.5 ± 0.4 in the 2010s — a strikingly steady global total that conceals opposing regional trends. Sinks strengthened in temperate forests (+30 ± 5%) and in tropical regrowth (+29 ± 8%) as forest area increased, and weakened in boreal (−36 ± 6%) and intact tropical (−31 ± 7%) forests as disturbance intensified and intact area shrank. The same analysis puts the global forest sink at almost half of fossil-fuel emissions over 1990–2019, with roughly two-thirds of that benefit offset by tropical deforestation.

Two implications follow. A global sink figure that holds steady is not evidence that nothing is changing, in the same way that the near-constant airborne fraction discussed in [the carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained) is not evidence of fixed sinks. And because the sink depends on area and on stand age together, the case for planting is not the same as the case for keeping existing stands — a distinction the evidence handles less kindly than the policy rhetoric does, as [the record on tree planting](/en/ecology/forests/reforestation-and-tree-planting-evidence) sets out.

## Where the global picture runs out

The assessment covers 236 countries and areas, of which 42 were handled as desk studies because no national report arrived; those account for 1.6 percent of forest area, so the coverage problem is small in area and larger in the specific places it bites. Trend estimates generally use only countries with complete time series, with gap-filling in some cases, which means global and regional aggregates cannot be reproduced by adding up the published country figures — a reconciliation failure that looks like an error and is not one.

The deeper asymmetry is in what sits underneath a national submission. A country running a statistically designed network of permanent plots — the United States remeasures its national inventory plots every five to ten years depending on location, adding down woody material, soils and understorey vegetation on a subset — is reporting something qualitatively different from a country extrapolating a single land-cover map. Both arrive in the same table, formatted identically.

Three gaps are structural rather than incidental. Soil organic carbon is reported to a depth each country chooses. Disturbance area cannot be totalled globally. And degradation has no agreed definition at all: the reporting instructions ask each country to define "degraded forest" for itself, and only 59 of 214 responding countries and areas, covering 37 percent of global forest area, had a national definition to offer. That absence is not a footnote — it means the single largest process affecting standing forest is measured against 59 different yardsticks, which is why it needs [a separate treatment of degradation](/en/ecology/forests/forest-degradation-vs-deforestation) rather than a paragraph here.
