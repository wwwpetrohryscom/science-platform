"Marine protected area" is a legal category, not an ecological one. It describes what a government has written down about a piece of sea, and it says almost nothing on its own about what happens inside the boundary. Two sites carrying the identical designation can differ by an order of magnitude in fish biomass, and the difference is predictable from a handful of design and governance features rather than from the label. That gap between the map and the water is what this page is about. A boundary drawn on a chart encloses a column of water that goes on exchanging heat, larvae and dissolved material with everything around it — the vertical structure set out in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained) does not stop at a legal line — so the only pressures a designation can act on are the ones taking place inside it.

## One label, four regimes

The framework that has done most to clean up the vocabulary is [The MPA Guide](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abf0861), published in Science in 2021, which separates a site's *stage of establishment* from its *level of protection* and defines four protection levels by the impact of the activities actually permitted.

| Level of protection | What is allowed | Expected biodiversity outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fully protected | No extractive or destructive activity | Strongest and most consistent recovery |
| Highly protected | Only minimal-impact extraction | Substantial recovery, close to fully protected |
| Lightly protected | Moderate-impact extraction permitted | Modest and often undetectable difference from open coast |
| Minimally protected | High total impact permitted | Little to no measurable biodiversity gain |

The framework matters because national reporting does not make this distinction. A site zoned to allow trawling over most of its area and a no-take reserve both enter the World Database on Protected Areas as marine protected area, and both count toward international coverage targets. When a study, a press summary or a policy document averages outcomes across that mixture, the resulting effect size is a weighted average of a real effect and no effect — and it is smaller than either.

## The effect sizes, and what averaging destroys

The clearest demonstration comes from a global underwater survey of 87 marine protected areas published in Nature in 2014. It found that conservation benefit accumulates with five features — no take, well enforced, older than ten years, larger than 100 km², and isolated by deep water or sand — and that the accumulation is exponential rather than additive. Sites with four or five of those features held twice as many large fish species per transect, [five times the large-fish biomass](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13022) and fourteen times the shark biomass of fished coasts. The same survey found that 59 per cent of the protected areas studied had only one or two of the five features and were not ecologically distinguishable from fished sites.

That last finding is the one that goes missing in summary, and the exponential form is why it matters. A site carrying two of the five features is not two-fifths of the way to a site carrying five; it is close to indistinguishable from open coast. Benefit appears once several conditions hold together, which makes the useful question about any given site *which* features it has rather than how many square kilometres it covers. It also means a pooled figure misleads in both directions at once: it understates what a well-designed reserve achieves and overstates what a typical designation achieves.

A regional meta-analysis of 24 Mediterranean sites published in Scientific Reports in 2017 reached the same structure from different data. Full protection produced stronger effects than partial protection for fished target species; benefits for fish biomass under full protection [correlated with the level of enforcement](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-08850-w) and with nothing else among the variables tested. Fish density was higher in older and better-enforced sites, and — against expectation — in smaller ones, which suggests that in a crowded sea a small reserve that is genuinely policed outperforms a large one that is not.

## Enforcement is the variable that keeps reappearing

Across the design features that predict outcome, enforcement is the one that recurs in every analysis and the one that is hardest to read off a map. It is also the least reported: coverage statistics are compiled from national submissions describing legal status, not from compliance monitoring. Satellite tracking of fishing vessels has begun to close that gap, but the record is short and it sees industrial vessels far better than small ones.

The practical consequence is that a coverage figure and an effectiveness figure are different quantities, and the gap between them is not small. The most widely cited estimate, from a 2021 Nature analysis of global ocean protection priorities, put the share of the ocean that is *highly* protected at [2.7 per cent](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03371-z) at that time — against total protected and conserved coverage several times larger. Assessing whether a designation is working is a distinct exercise from counting designations, and it is treated in more depth in the general article on [protected-area effectiveness](/en/ecology/biodiversity/protected-area-effectiveness).

## Where the protection actually is

Global marine coverage is also distributed very unevenly between the two legal domains of the ocean. Protected Planet, the reporting platform for the World Database on Protected Areas, records national waters — the exclusive economic zones, which make up 39 per cent of the ocean — as [23.05 per cent covered](https://www.protectedplanet.net/en/thematic-areas/marine-protected-areas) by protected areas and other effective area-based conservation measures. Areas beyond national jurisdiction make up the remaining 61 per cent and are 1.45 per cent covered. Nearly all marine protection is therefore coastal-state protection, and the high seas, along with most [deep-sea habitat](/en/ecology/oceans/deep-sea-ecosystems), remain almost entirely outside it. The United Nations agreement on marine biodiversity of areas beyond national jurisdiction, in force since 17 January 2026, creates the legal route to change that but has not yet changed the numbers.

This is also why the wording of [Target 3 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework](https://www.cbd.int/gbf/targets/3) repays reading in full. It calls for 30 per cent of coastal and marine areas to be "effectively conserved and managed through ecologically representative, well-connected and equitably governed systems" by 2030. Every qualifier in that clause is a condition that coverage statistics do not measure, and the evidence above indicates that dropping them would leave a target that a country could meet without changing anything in the water.

## Spillover, and why it is the hardest claim to test

The argument that closing an area increases catch outside it is the one most often made to fishing communities and the one with the thinnest evidence base, because testing it requires a counterfactual for a fishery that is changing for many other reasons at once. The strongest recent test used vessel-level observer data around Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument after its 2016 expansion and found [evidence of spillover benefits](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abn0098) for yellowfin and bigeye tuna — a result that matters because it concerns highly migratory species, for which spillover had been considered unlikely on the grounds that most reserves are small relative to the animals' ranges.

Spillover also has a companion effect that is easy to overlook. A machine-learning forecast trained on satellite vessel-monitoring data found that under a range of protected-area expansion scenarios [fishing effort inside new reserves would fall but not to zero](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2400592121), and that effort outside them would fall as well rather than simply relocating. If that holds, the standard objection that protection merely displaces pressure is too simple — though the same result implies that yields modelled from a clean displacement assumption, including in [stock assessments](/en/ecology/oceans/fisheries-science-and-stock-assessment), are misspecified too.

## Where the reserve literature is thinnest

The shape of the evidence base limits what any of it can support. Most site-level comparisons are space-for-time substitutions — protected sites against fished sites at one moment — rather than before-after designs with controls, and protected sites are not randomly located: they are often placed where the habitat was already in better condition, which biases the estimated effect upward. The outcome variables are dominated by fish biomass and species counts on shallow rocky and coral reefs, because that is where divers can survey; soft sediment, pelagic and deep habitats are far less represented in the record. And protection confers no immunity from warming: marine heatwaves have caused mass mortality inside fully protected reserves, a mechanism developed in the article on [coral bleaching and recovery](/en/ecology/oceans/coral-reef-ecology-and-bleaching). Well-designed protection is measurably effective against extraction. It is not a substitute for controlling the pressures that arrive from outside the boundary.
