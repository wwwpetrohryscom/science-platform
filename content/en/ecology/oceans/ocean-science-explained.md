The ocean is one connected body of water that behaves as though it were stacked. Density — a function of temperature, salinity and pressure — sorts seawater into layers that exchange slowly with each other, and most of the large-scale behaviour of the sea follows from that sorting: where added heat accumulates, where nutrients sit, how deep light reaches, and how long anything stays where it is.

The scale is worth stating plainly, because it is why the sorting matters so much. NOAA's National Ocean Service puts the volume of the ocean at about 1.335 billion cubic kilometres, holding roughly 97 per cent of Earth's water and covering more than 70 per cent of the planet's surface. Its average depth is 3,682 metres, and the deepest measured point, Challenger Deep, is about 10,935 metres. A habitat that is kilometres deep almost everywhere is not organised like a landscape. It is organised like a column.

## Stratification is the master variable

Sunlit surface water is warmer, and often fresher, than the water beneath it, so it is less dense and it floats. Wind and winter cooling stir the top tens to hundreds of metres into a **mixed layer** of near-uniform density. Below that, temperature drops steeply through the **[thermocline](/en/glossary/thermocline)** and density rises through the pycnocline. That density step is the most consequential structure in the sea, because it is a barrier to vertical exchange: it governs how readily heat, carbon, oxygen and dissolved nutrients pass between the surface and the interior.

That barrier has strengthened. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment concludes it is virtually certain that the upper ocean has become more stably stratified since at least 1970 across the vast majority of the globe, driven by surface-intensified warming and high-latitude freshening, and assesses the increase in 0–200 m stratification at 4.9 ± 1.5 per cent between 1970 and 2018. An analysis in Nature Climate Change that instead quantifies stability using the squared buoyancy frequency down to 2,000 metres reports a 5.3 per cent increase (5.0 to 5.8 per cent) over 1960–2018, about 0.90 per cent per decade, with roughly 71 per cent of the change in the upper 200 metres and more than 90 per cent of it attributable to temperature rather than salinity.

Those two stratification figures are close, but they are not estimates of the same quantity, and treating them as rival answers to one question is the commonest way ocean statistics get misread. One is a difference between two depths; the other is a depth-resolved measure of stability. The agreement between them is meaningful precisely because the methods differ.

The same layering is why the sea dominates the planet's energy accounting: the IPCC assesses that the ocean stored 91 per cent of the total energy the Earth system gained between 1971 and 2018, and a more stable surface layer changes how quickly that heat reaches the interior.

## Salt stays constant; the useful chemicals do not

Dissolved salt averages about 35 parts per thousand, or roughly 3.5 per cent of the weight of seawater. The proportions of the major ions barely change from place to place, which is what allows salinity to be treated as a single number and inferred from electrical conductivity. Where salinity does vary — under heavy rainfall, near melting ice, in evaporative basins — it changes density, and therefore feeds straight back into the layering described above.

The biologically active constituents behave nothing like the salt. Nitrate, phosphate, silicate and dissolved iron are stripped out of the lit surface layer by growth and regenerated at depth as sinking particles decompose, which is why the surface ocean over most of its area is chemically impoverished while the water a kilometre below is not. NASA's Earth Observatory describes the resulting biological pump as transferring about 10 gigatonnes of carbon from the atmosphere to the deep ocean each year, a flux that connects the sea directly to the [global carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained). The dissolved-gas side of the same chemistry has two consequences serious enough to carry their own treatments: the carbonate system is shifting as the ocean takes up CO₂, covered in [ocean acidification](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-acidification-explained), and dissolved oxygen is declining for two quite separate reasons, separated out in [open-ocean and coastal deoxygenation](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-deoxygenation-and-dead-zones).

## Life is concentrated in a layer that is almost none of the volume

Photosynthesis is confined to roughly the upper 200 metres, and NOAA Ocean Exploration notes that more than 90 per cent of the ocean lies deeper than that. Essentially all marine primary production therefore happens in a thin illuminated skin, and everything below it lives on what falls, swims or is mixed down from that skin. The consequences are strange enough that they are treated separately in [marine productivity and food webs](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity), where the standing stock of producers turns out to be smaller than the standing stock of the animals eating them.

At the two extremes of that vertical gradient sit the two marine ecosystems most often written about. Reef-building corals occupy the shallowest, clearest, most nutrient-poor water and solve the resulting energy problem through symbiosis, a system whose failure mode is examined in [coral reefs and bleaching](/en/ecology/oceans/coral-reef-ecology-and-bleaching). Far below, in permanent darkness and cold, sit the communities described in [deep-sea ecosystems](/en/ecology/oceans/deep-sea-ecosystems), including the chemosynthetic ones that do not depend on sunlight at all.

## How human use of the sea gets measured

Human use reaches the evidence base in three forms, and they are not equally tractable. Removal is quantified by stock assessment, a modelling exercise whose assumptions and disagreements are the subject of [fisheries science](/en/ecology/oceans/fisheries-science-and-stock-assessment); protection is quantified by area, which is exactly why the [evidence on marine protected areas](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-protected-areas-evidence) depends so heavily on distinguishing level of protection from hectares reported. Addition is harder still: the input estimates and the measured standing stock of [marine plastic](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-plastic-pollution-evidence) do not reconcile, and that gap is itself a finding.

## The instrumental record begins later than most readers assume

For most of the twentieth century the ocean interior was sampled by ships, which meant that observations were concentrated along commercial routes, in the Northern Hemisphere, and in summer. The Argo array changed the sampling geometry rather than the instruments. NOAA's Atlantic Oceanographic and Meteorological Laboratory records that the first float was deployed in 1999 and the initial target of 3,000 profiling floats on a 3° × 3° grid was met in November 2007, with the later Argo2020 design aiming for 4,000 Core Argo floats alongside 1,200 Deep Argo and 1,000 biogeochemical ones. Each float drifts at a mid-water level, descends to 2,000 metres every ten days and measures temperature and salinity on the way up. The array passed its millionth profile in October 2012, its two-millionth in September 2018 and its three-millionth in July 2024, with 26 countries contributing.

That is the observational break point. Statements about the ocean interior before roughly 2005 rest on sparse, unevenly distributed data; statements after it rest on a near-global array. The seafloor is further behind. NOAA Ocean Exploration reports that as of April 2026, 28.7 per cent of the global seafloor had been mapped with modern high-resolution multibeam sonar — around 56 per cent within United States waters — and that explorers have directly seen less than 0.001 per cent of the deep-sea floor, an area roughly the size of Rhode Island. Satellite-derived bathymetry covers the whole seafloor, but at a resolution that misses features as large as an individual seamount.

The result is a set of observing systems whose coverage differs by orders of magnitude, and conflating them produces most of the confident-sounding errors about how well the sea is known.

| Observing system | What it resolves | Coverage achieved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ship hydrography | Full-depth temperature, salinity and chemistry at high precision | Sparse, biased toward routes, hemispheres and seasons |
| Argo profiling floats | Temperature and salinity, surface to 2,000 m, every 10 days | Near-global open ocean; over three million profiles since 1999 |
| Satellite-derived bathymetry | Broad seafloor shape, inferred from satellite measurements of the sea surface | Entire seafloor, at detail too coarse for individual features |
| Multibeam sonar | Seafloor depth and morphology at high resolution | 28.7 per cent of the global seafloor as of April 2026 |

## What the observing system still cannot resolve

Four gaps are structural rather than incidental. The standard Argo floats profile only the top 2,000 metres, leaving roughly half the ocean's volume — and the slow deep circulation that will keep warming for centuries after the surface stabilises, as the IPCC assesses — thinly sampled. Ice-covered and marginal seas are poorly represented because a float that cannot surface cannot transmit. Biogeochemical variables such as oxygen, nitrate and pH are measured on a small fraction of the array compared with temperature and salinity, so the chemical ocean has a far shorter reliable record than the physical one. And the deep interior is ventilated slowly: NOAA's National Ocean Service estimates that a parcel of water takes about 1,000 years to complete the global conveyor circuit, which means today's deep-water properties partly record surface conditions from before instrumental records began — a lag developed further in [ocean circulation and climate](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate).

None of that makes the large-scale picture unreliable. It does mean that the honest form of most ocean statements specifies a depth range, a period and a product, and that a claim about "the ocean" without those qualifiers is usually a claim about its upper two kilometres since about 2005.
