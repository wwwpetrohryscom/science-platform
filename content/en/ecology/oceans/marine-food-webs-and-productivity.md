A global biomass census published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences puts about 1 gigatonne of carbon of primary producers in the ocean, supporting roughly 5 gigatonnes of carbon of consumers. On land the ratio runs the other way, and by a wide margin: plants account for around 450 of the roughly 550 gigatonnes of carbon in the entire [biosphere](/en/glossary/biosphere). The marine pyramid stands on its point.

This is not a paradox, and it is not an artefact of bad accounting. It is what happens when the producers are replaced faster than the consumers are. The same census notes that marine primary producers turn over on the order of days, while consumer biomass turns over on a scale of years — a few years, in the case of mesopelagic fish. Standing stock measures what is present at an instant; production measures what passes through. Divide the second by the first and the two realms separate sharply: on the figures below, marine producers replace their own mass something like fifty times a year, while land plants take roughly eight years to do it once. Almost every misreading of marine productivity comes from quoting one of these quantities and meaning the other.

## The flux is enormous; the crop is tiny

The founding estimate for global net primary production, integrating comparable satellite-driven models for land and sea, put the total at 104.9 petagrams of carbon per year with roughly equal contributions from the two realms. Half of Earth's photosynthesis, in other words, is performed by a standing stock of roughly 1 gigatonne of carbon, against about 450 gigatonnes for plants on land, and it happens inside the thin illuminated layer described in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained).

The mechanics are straightforward once the numbers are in front of you. NASA notes that the life span of an individual phytoplankton cell is rarely more than a few days. A population that is grazed down and regrown weekly can deliver an annual production far larger than any snapshot of its biomass, in the way that a lawn mown every week produces far more clippings in a year than its standing height would suggest. The fraction of that production which escapes recycling in the surface layer and sinks is the biological pump, which NASA describes as moving about 10 gigatonnes of carbon from the atmosphere to the deep ocean each year, and which is what everything below the sunlit layer ultimately eats.

The organisms doing the work are mostly very small. A quantitative niche model built on more than 35,000 discrete observations estimates global annual mean abundances of 2.9 ± 0.1 × 10²⁷ cells of the cyanobacterium *Prochlorococcus* and 7.0 ± 0.3 × 10²⁶ cells of *Synechococcus*. Two genera of picoplankton, invisible to the naked eye, account for a substantial share of marine primary production.

## What actually limits the crop

Light limits production at depth and at high latitudes in winter. Nutrients limit it nearly everywhere else, because the density step that stabilises the surface layer also keeps deep, nutrient-rich water from reaching the light.

Nitrogen and phosphorus are the classic limiting elements, but the discovery that reordered the field concerned iron. A synthesis of twelve mesoscale iron-addition experiments conducted between 1993 and 2005, published in Science, concluded that iron supply limits production in one-third of the world ocean — the high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll regions where surface nitrate and phosphate remain perennially high because there is not enough iron to use them. That synthesis is also careful about what the experiments do not license: extrapolating patch-scale iron additions to regional and seasonal scales is limited by the fact that the mode of iron supply in an experiment differs from natural supply. The finding that iron controls production in the Southern Ocean is robust; the inference that adding iron would reliably sequester carbon is not the same claim, and does not follow from it.

## Why fisheries cluster where they do

Production is not distributed evenly, and the unevenness is what makes the ocean fishable at all.

| Setting | Nutrient supply | Typical food chain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subtropical gyre | Strongly stratified; supply almost entirely from recycling | Long, dominated by picoplankton and microbial recycling |
| Coastal upwelling | Wind-driven delivery of deep, nutrient-rich water | Short: large diatoms to small pelagic fish to predators |
| High-nutrient, low-chlorophyll | Macronutrients abundant, iron scarce | Constrained by iron rather than by nitrogen or phosphorus |
| Polar shelf | Seasonal, pulsed by ice melt and light return | Short and intensely seasonal |

The economics of that table are stark. A study in Communications Earth & Environment notes that eastern boundary upwelling systems occupy only about 1 per cent of the ocean's surface yet produce around 20 per cent of the global fish catch. The reason is trophic geometry: upwelling supports large diatoms, which are eaten directly by small pelagic fish, so the chain from photosynthesis to a harvestable animal is often one or two steps rather than the four or five typical of the open ocean. Each step loses most of the energy passing through it, so removing steps multiplies the yield at the top. This is the physical basis for the stock-assessment work described in [fisheries science](/en/ecology/oceans/fisheries-science-and-stock-assessment), and it is why productive fisheries cluster in a handful of coastal regions.

## The microbial loop rewrote the diagram

Textbook marine food webs once ran phytoplankton to zooplankton to fish. The version that replaced it recognises that a large share of primary production never enters that chain at all: it is released as dissolved organic matter, taken up by heterotrophic bacteria, and returned to larger organisms only through grazing on those bacteria by protists. That is the **microbial loop**, and it changes the accounting completely, because carbon routed through it is respired near the surface rather than exported to depth.

Later work has complicated even that picture. A review in Science argues that unicellular eukaryotes cannot be sorted cleanly into producers and consumers at all: mixotrophy — photosynthesising and eating, in the same cell, sometimes in the same hour — is common, and these behavioural strategies are difficult to deduce from genome sequences. A marine food web is therefore less a ladder than a network in which the same organism occupies different rungs depending on light, prey availability and physiological state. This matters for anyone reading trophic-level statistics: the trophic level of a protist is not a fixed property.

## Satellites measure colour, not carbon

Nearly every global productivity number rests on satellite ocean colour, because ships cannot sample the surface ocean at the necessary density. The instruments measure water-leaving radiance; chlorophyll-a concentration is retrieved from that radiance; and net primary production is then modelled from chlorophyll together with light, temperature and assumptions about how efficiently cells fix carbon. The retrieval chain, and the atmospheric correction that dominates its error budget, are covered in [ocean-colour observations](/en/ecology/earth-observation/ocean-color-observations).

Two limitations deserve stating explicitly. First, each step in that chain adds assumptions, and productivity models built on the same chlorophyll field differ in what they return — the number quoted for global marine primary production depends on which algorithm produced it, which is why the algorithm should be named alongside the number. Second, detecting change is much harder than estimating a mean. A study in Nature reports that more than 30 years of continuous data had been thought necessary to detect a climate-driven trend in chlorophyll, but that trends emerge faster in the multivariate reflectance signal itself: analysing a 20-year record from the MODIS instrument on Aqua, it found significant trends in reflectance across 56 per cent of the global surface ocean, mostly equatorward of 40°, with low-latitude waters becoming greener. A correction to that paper has since been published, which is worth knowing when citing the exact percentages.

## The measurements that would settle the open questions are the hardest to make

Three gaps persist. Export production — the fraction of primary production that actually sinks past the twilight zone rather than being recycled above it — is measured at a small number of sites with methods that disagree, yet it sets the magnitude of the biological pump. Grazing rates are harder to measure than growth rates, so the loss side of the equation is consistently less well constrained than the production side. And below the surface layer, satellites see nothing at all: subsurface chlorophyll maxima, which can hold a large share of the water column's biomass, are inferred rather than observed. What lives on the flux that escapes all of this recycling is the subject of [deep-sea ecosystems](/en/ecology/oceans/deep-sea-ecosystems).
