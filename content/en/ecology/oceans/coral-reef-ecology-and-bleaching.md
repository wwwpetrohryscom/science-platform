A bleached coral is not a dead coral. It is a coral that has expelled the single-celled algae living inside its tissues, lost its colour along with them, and begun living on stored reserves and whatever it can catch. Whether it recovers depends on how long the stress lasts and how much energy it had banked. Getting this distinction right is the difference between reading a bleaching report as a mortality figure and reading it as what it is — a measurement of stress, with mortality as a variable consequence.

The dependency is real enough to make the distinction urgent. NOAA's National Ocean Service states that as much as 90 per cent of the organic material produced photosynthetically by the algal symbionts is transferred to the host coral tissue. A reef-building coral is, energetically, mostly a farm. It also sits at the shallowest, best-lit end of the vertical gradient set out in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained) — the layer where added heat arrives first.

## An alliance roughly 160 million years old

The symbionts were treated for decades as a single genus, *Symbiodinium*, informally split into lettered clades. A systematic revision published in Current Biology resolved those divergent clades as genera within the family Symbiodiniaceae and formally described seven of them, while recalibrating the molecular clock to place the family's earliest diversification around 160 million years ago, in the middle of the Mesozoic. That timing coincides with the Jurassic radiation of analogues to modern shallow-water stony corals — the partnership and the reef habit appear to have arisen together.

The taxonomic tidy-up has a practical consequence. Genera within Symbiodiniaceae differ in thermal tolerance, so "the coral's algae" is not one thing, and a colony's heat sensitivity depends partly on which symbionts it hosts and whether it can shuffle between them. A parallel line of evidence concerns the coral's bacterial community rather than its algae, examined separately in [coral microbiomes and bleaching resistance](/en/biology/cells/coral-microbiome-bleaching-resistance).

Reefs also sit at an odd point in the productivity landscape. They require clear, nutrient-poor water so that light reaches the symbionts, yet they are among the most productive and diverse marine environments — a paradox resolved by the tight internal recycling of nutrients between host and symbiont rather than by nutrient supply from outside. That is the opposite of the mechanism that makes upwelling systems productive, described in [marine productivity](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity).

## How heat stress is actually counted

Operational bleaching forecasting does not use temperature; it uses accumulated exposure above a locally defined threshold. NOAA Coral Reef Watch calculates a Coral Bleaching HotSpot as the amount by which satellite sea surface temperature exceeds the site's maximum monthly mean climatology — the long-term mean for the climatologically hottest month at that location. Only HotSpot values of 1 °C or more are accumulated, over a rolling twelve-week window, to give **degree heating weeks**, reported in °C-weeks on a scale from 0 to 20. Because the baseline is local, the same absolute temperature can be unremarkable on one reef and stressful on another.

The thresholds are published, and they are consequences rather than definitions — each was calibrated against observed reef outcomes.

| Accumulated stress | Expected outcome on the reef |
| --- | --- |
| 4 °C-weeks | Risk of coral bleaching |
| 8 °C-weeks | Reef-wide bleaching likely, with mortality of heat-sensitive corals |
| Above 12 °C-weeks | Multi-species mortality becomes likely |
| 16 °C-weeks or more | Risk of severe multi-species mortality, above 50 per cent of corals |
| 20 °C-weeks or more | Near-complete mortality, above 80 per cent of corals, is likely |

Field validation has generally supported the scale while sharpening its lower end. Work on the 2016 Great Barrier Reef marine heatwave published in Nature found that corals began to die immediately where accumulated exposure passed a critical threshold of 3–4 °C-weeks, and that eight months later an exposure of 6 °C-weeks or more had driven a regional-scale shift in assemblage composition, with fast-growing staghorn and tabular corals suffering a catastrophic die-off. That event transformed the three-dimensional structure and ecological functioning of 29 per cent of the 3,863 reefs making up the system.

Two cautions travel with any degree-heating-week figure. It is a satellite product measuring the skin temperature of the sea surface, so it can misrepresent conditions on a reef flushed by cool internal waves or shaded by turbidity. And it says nothing about the other stressors acting at the same time, including the carbonate-chemistry changes covered in [ocean acidification](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-acidification-explained).

## The gap between global bleaching events is closing

Bleaching at global scale has now been recorded four times, and NOAA reports that each successive event since 1998 has been more widespread and more severe than the last. Its National Environmental Satellite, Data, and Information Service reports that from early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress affected 84 per cent of the world's coral reef area across all three reef-bearing ocean basins, with mass bleaching documented in at least 83 countries and territories, and that the event likely ended in mid-2025.

The underlying driver is not in dispute. The IPCC assesses with high confidence that marine heatwaves have become more frequent over the twentieth century, and that since the 1980s they have approximately doubled in frequency, becoming more intense and longer with medium confidence. Projections put them at four times more frequent (2 to 9, likely range) in 2081–2100 relative to 1995–2014 under a low-emissions pathway, and eight times more frequent (3 to 15) under a high-emissions one, with the largest changes in the tropical ocean and the Arctic.

For reefs, frequency rather than peak intensity is the variable that decides outcomes, and frequency is the variable that is changing.

## What recovery actually requires

The recovery evidence is more encouraging than the bleaching statistics suggest, and more conditional. The best-documented case is an isolated reef system in north Western Australia, where coral cover rose from 9 per cent to 44 per cent within twelve years of a bleaching event despite a 94 per cent reduction in larval supply for six years afterwards. The recovery ran first on the growth and survival of remnant colonies, and only later on juvenile recruitment as those colonies matured — and it happened on a reef isolated from chronic local pressures.

That case establishes what recovery needs: surviving colonies, time measured in a decade or more, and the absence of additional chronic stress. It does not establish that reefs generally recover, and the twelve-year timescale is the point. A reef exposed to severe heat stress every five or six years does not get to run that sequence. That is the arithmetic behind the concern, and it is a different claim from saying that reefs cannot recover: what has changed is the ratio between the time recovery needs and the time it is given.

## Restoration and assisted evolution, honestly scaled

Active restoration has grown quickly, and a systematic review in PLOS ONE gives the clearest picture of what it has so far demonstrated. Across the compiled projects, the median size of a restored area was 100 square metres; 60 per cent of projects reported less than 18 months of monitoring; 59 per cent of studies focused on fast-growing branching corals; and reported survival ran between 60 and 70 per cent. The review identified the field's recurring problems as unclear objectives, non-standardised monitoring, and project designs poorly matched to their stated aims — and it stated plainly that restoration should not be viewed as a replacement for action on climate change.

The arithmetic is what settles the question of scale. A median project of 100 m² is a rounding error against reef systems whose area is measured in tens or hundreds of thousands of square kilometres. Assisted evolution — selective breeding for thermal tolerance, symbiont manipulation, microbiome intervention — is at an earlier stage still, with much of the published work at aquarium or single-plot scale and very little field evidence on whether conferred tolerance persists across generations or across the range of stressors a wild colony meets. Current evidence supports restoration as a local intervention with local benefits; it does not yet support it as a global response.

Two things would change the picture materially: longer monitoring, since 18 months cannot detect whether a restored patch survives the next heatwave, and evidence from unmanipulated reefs on whether thermal thresholds are shifting on their own. Some shift is expected — symbiont shuffling and community turnover both push in that direction — but the rate at which reefs are adapting, relative to the rate at which the heatwave interval is shortening, remains the central unresolved quantity in the field. Where protection is part of the response, its effectiveness depends on the distinctions set out in [marine protected areas](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-protected-areas-evidence).
