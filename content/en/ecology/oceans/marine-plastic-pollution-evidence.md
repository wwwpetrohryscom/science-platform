Around 2014 the field acquired an arithmetic problem. One set of studies estimated that several million tonnes of polymer waste were entering the sea every year. Another set, towing nets across every subtropical gyre, could account for only a few hundred thousand tonnes floating on the surface — a shortfall of one to two orders of magnitude that became known as the missing-plastic problem. A decade of work aimed at that discrepancy has produced a much sharper picture of what each measurement method can actually see, which is why the budget is a better entry point to this subject than any single headline figure. Where the material ends up is governed by the slow vertical exchange that organises everything else in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained), and that turned out to matter more than the tonnage did.

## Building an input number out of waste statistics

No one measures the flow of debris into the ocean. The input figure is an accounting construction, and its assumptions are where its uncertainty lives.

The 2015 study in Science that anchored the field worked forward from solid-waste statistics: it estimated that 275 million tonnes of plastic waste were generated in 192 coastal countries in 2010, and that 4.8 to 12.7 million tonnes of it [entered the ocean](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1260352). The chain runs from waste generation per person, to the share that is mismanaged, to the share of mismanaged waste within 50 km of a coast, to the fraction that reaches the water. Each step multiplies an uncertain ratio, which is why the published range spans a factor of 2.6 and why the true range is wider than the published one.

A 2021 analysis in Science Advances came at the same quantity from the river side, modelling the probability that discarded material reaches a watercourse and then the sea, and calibrating against field observations of riverine debris. It put annual riverine emissions at 0.8 to 2.7 million tonnes and found that more than 1,000 rivers account for [80 per cent of the total](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaz5803) — a far more dispersed picture than the earlier claim that a handful of large Asian rivers dominated, and one in which small urban rivers matter disproportionately.

## The net that defines the standing stock

The standing-stock side of the ledger rests overwhelmingly on one instrument: a surface net with a mesh around 0.33 mm, towed for a fixed distance. A 2014 synthesis in PLOS ONE combined 680 net tows and 891 visual surveys for large debris from 24 expeditions between 2007 and 2013 with a dispersal model, and estimated a minimum of [5.25 trillion particles weighing 268,940 tonnes](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0111913) afloat worldwide, sorted into four size classes from 0.33 mm up to items larger than 200 mm.

That study also identified the anomaly at the heart of the problem. Given the rate at which large items are known to fragment, the millimetre-scale fraction should have been far more abundant than it was; instead the surveys recorded what the authors described as a tremendous loss of small particles relative to expected fragmentation rates, and concluded that some mechanism was removing sub-4.75 mm fragments from the surface faster than fragmentation supplied them.

Two structural limitations of the method should be obvious from that description and often are not. A 0.33 mm mesh does not retain anything smaller, so the size class with the largest particle count is systematically absent from the count. And a surface tow samples the top few centimetres, so any material that is neutrally buoyant, biofouled, or simply mixed downward by wind is outside the sampled volume.

## The budget terms, side by side

| Budget term | Estimate | How it was obtained |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land-based waste entering the sea, 2010 | 4.8–12.7 Mt per year | Waste-generation statistics and mismanagement ratios |
| Riverine emissions, calibrated | 0.8–2.7 Mt per year | Probabilistic transport model tuned to field observations |
| Input implied by a data-assimilating budget | about 0.5 Mt per year | 3-D model constrained by coastline, surface and deep-ocean data |
| Floating surface standing stock | 0.27 Mt, 5.25 trillion particles | Surface net tows plus visual surveys and dispersal modelling |
| Total buoyant mass in the ocean, 2020 | about 3.2 Mt | Same data-assimilating budget, sizes 0.1–1,600 mm |

The bottom two rows are the resolution. A 2023 study in Nature Geoscience assimilated observations from coastlines, the surface layer and the deep ocean into a single model spanning particle sizes from 0.1 to 1,600 mm, and found that items larger than 25 mm hold more than [95 per cent of the buoyant mass](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-023-01216-0) — 3,100 of 3,200 kilotonnes for 2020. Its implied annual input, about 500 kilotonnes, is well below the earlier waste-accounting estimates, and its residence times are longer. Taken together, those two adjustments close the gap: the conclusion is that there is no missing sink, and that the original discrepancy was largely an overestimated input combined with a standing stock dominated by large items that surface tows under-sample and fragmentation had been assumed to consume faster than it does.

That is a reconciliation of the mass budget, not a resolution of every question. Independent work has confirmed that the water column below the tow depth does hold material: a 2025 synthesis in Nature of depth profiles from 1,885 stations sampled between 2014 and 2024 found subsurface microplastic abundances ranging across eight orders of magnitude, with the smallest particles declining only gradually with depth and larger ones concentrating at density interfaces. Its most striking number is that microplastics rise from about 0.1 per cent of total particulate organic carbon at 30 m to [5 per cent at 2,000 m](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08818-1) — a figure whose interpretation is complicated by the fact that particulate carbon is itself sparse at depth. The same paper is blunt that the absence of methodological standardisation across those 1,885 stations creates substantial uncertainty. Where the fraction that leaves the surface finally comes to rest, including in [deep-sea sediments](/en/ecology/oceans/deep-sea-ecosystems), remains the least constrained part of the picture.

## What a garbage patch is

Public understanding of accumulation zones runs well ahead of the observations. NOAA is direct about this: a garbage patch is not "a large and continuous patch of easily visible marine debris items such as bottles and other litter — akin to a literal island of trash", but a region where much of the debris is small pieces of floating material [not immediately evident to the naked eye](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/garbagepatch.html), continuously mixed by wind and waves and dispersed through the upper water column. NOAA also notes that the borders and content change with currents and winds, which is why estimates of a patch's size vary so widely between studies. The convergence itself is a straightforward consequence of the wind-driven subtropical gyres described in [ocean circulation and climate](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate); the concentrations found there are elevated relative to the open ocean, not visible from orbit.

## Harm: what has been demonstrated, and at what level

The evidence on ecological effect is uneven in a specific and useful way. A systematic review published in Ecology in 2016 catalogued 366 perceived threats from marine debris across levels of biological organisation, found that 296 had been tested, and that 83 per cent of those tests demonstrated an effect. But 89 per cent of the demonstrated impacts were [at suborganismal levels](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/14-2070.1) — molecular, cellular or tissue responses. Effects at higher levels, including death of individuals and changes in assemblage composition, were demonstrated mainly for items larger than 1 mm: rope, straws and fragments rather than microscopic particles.

Exposure at the individual level is nonetheless well documented and increasing. A 2015 PNAS analysis of seabirds found that 80 of 135 species studied between 1962 and 2012 [had ingested plastic](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1502108112), with 29 per cent of individuals affected on average within those studies, and projected ingestion across nearly all seabird species by mid-century. The honest summary is that entanglement and ingestion are thoroughly documented, sublethal physiological responses are widely demonstrated, and effects on population trajectories are demonstrated for far fewer cases — because attributing a population decline to debris requires separating it from fishing, warming and habitat loss acting on the same populations. This applies with particular force in the pelagic zone, where the same size fractions overlap with the particle field that supports [marine food webs](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity).

## Why the stock responds slowly to the flow

Because the buoyant material is long-lived — the persistence estimates that fall out of the reconciled budget are longer than earlier models assumed, giving these polymers an unusually long [residence time](/en/glossary/residence-time) in the marine environment — the stock in the ocean responds slowly to changes in the flow. A 2020 modelling study in Science quantified this: applying every intervention its authors judged feasible [reduced annual pollution by 40 per cent](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba9475) against 2016 rates and by 78 per cent against business-as-usual in 2040, and even then 710 million tonnes cumulatively entered aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems over the period. Reducing the input rate changes the rate of accumulation, not the accumulation already there, and no measurement programme currently in place would detect the difference on a timescale shorter than a decade.
