Depth organises the sea, and [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained) starts with the reason: water absorbs light, and it absorbs it unevenly across the spectrum. The consequence is that the ocean is optically shallow relative to its depth: a column several kilometres deep has a thin lit skin and an enormous dark interior, and the boundary between them is sharp enough to be used as a definition.

## The three zones

NOAA sets out the division and the depths. "Light may be detected as far as 1,000 meters down in the ocean, but there is rarely any significant light beyond 200 meters."

That gives the euphotic or sunlight zone as the upper 200 metres, where "sunlight rarely penetrates beyond this zone"; the dysphotic or twilight zone between 200 and 1,000 metres, where "sunlight decreases rapidly with depth; photosynthesis is not possible here"; and the aphotic zone below 1,000 metres, where "sunlight does not penetrate; the zone is bathed in darkness", subdividing into the bathypelagic from 1,000 to 4,000 metres, the abyssopelagic from 4,000 to 6,000 metres, and the hadopelagic below that.

The two thresholds carry different meanings and it is worth keeping them apart. Two hundred metres is where photosynthesis becomes impossible — a physiological limit, set by whether absorbed light exceeds the cost of the machinery. One thousand metres is where light becomes undetectable at all — an instrumental limit, and one that biology beats, since many deep-sea animals see light far dimmer than any photosynthesis could use.

## Why the lit layer is thin

Absorption in seawater is strongly wavelength-dependent. Red light is absorbed within the first few metres; blue penetrates furthest, which is why the deep sea is blue and why a photograph taken with a flash at depth looks nothing like the scene by eye.

For photosynthesis this compounds a problem the machinery already has. Chlorophyll absorbs strongly in the red and the blue and weakly in the green — the spectral gap discussed in [light harvesting](/en/biology/cells/light-harvesting-and-photosystem-efficiency) — and the red half of that absorption is stripped out in the top few metres of water. Deep phytoplankton therefore operate on a spectrum their pigments are only half matched to, which is one reason accessory pigments differ between surface and deep populations.

## What follows for the whole ocean

**Production and nutrients are separated by construction.** Photosynthesis is confined to the top 200 metres; the nutrients released when sinking organic matter decays accumulate below it. Everything about [stratification and nutrient supply](/en/ecology/oceans/stratification-mixing-and-nutrient-supply) follows from that vertical separation.

**Most of the ocean's volume is heterotrophic.** The euphotic zone is a few per cent of ocean depth. Everything below it depends on organic matter sinking from above, which is why the deep sea is food-limited rather than space-limited.

**The twilight zone is where the [carbon accounting](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty) is hardest.** Between 200 and 1,000 metres, sinking particles are intercepted and respired, and how much escapes to depth determines how long carbon fixed at the surface stays out of the atmosphere. It is a dark, dilute, hard-to-sample layer that sets a term in the global [carbon budget](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-budgets-and-remaining-emissions).

**Vision outruns photosynthesis by orders of magnitude.** The gap between 200 metres, where photosynthesis stops, and 1,000 metres, where light stops being detectable, is a zone where light is useless as energy and still useful as information. That is why the twilight zone is where bioluminescence, large eyes, and counter-illumination camouflage concentrate.
