The ocean's uptake of carbon dioxide is one of the load-bearing numbers in the [carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained), and one of the few quantities in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained) that is not measured directly. It is computed, as the product of a concentration difference across the sea surface — which is measured, extensively — and a gas transfer velocity, which is not.

The transfer velocity is where the uncertainty lives, and understanding why is a good lesson in how a well-constrained global quantity can rest on a poorly constrained local one.

## What the transfer velocity is

Gas crosses the sea surface through a thin layer where turbulence is suppressed and molecular diffusion has to do the work. How thick that layer is, and therefore how fast gas crosses it, depends on how vigorously the surface is being disturbed — by wind stress, by breaking waves, by bubbles injected below the surface, by convection driven from the water side.

None of those is routinely measured over the open ocean. Wind speed is, from satellites and from ships, everywhere and continuously. So the transfer velocity is parameterised as a function of wind speed, and the entire global estimate inherits whatever that substitution costs.

## What it costs

A 2022 study in *Biogeosciences* measured the exchange directly and compared it with the wind-based approach. Its finding is a useful two-part result.

The parameterisation works on average: "a wind-based model with a similar pattern to those developed for larger basins and open-sea conditions can, on average, be a good approximation for k, the gas transfer velocity."

It works much less well instant by instant. Wind-speed relationships alone explain only about 30% of the observed variability in half-hourly transfer-velocity values, and the authors are explicit that "in order to reduce the uncertainty associated with these averages and produce reliable short-term k estimates, additional physical processes must be considered."

The processes they identify differ by regime. Above about 8 m s⁻¹, sea spray and atmospheric conditions matter, with exchange enhanced during unstable stratification and low humidity. Below about 6 m s⁻¹, convection on the water side becomes a relevant control — a mechanism that has nothing to do with wind at all and is invisible to a wind-only formula.

## Why the averaging rescues the global number and not the regional one

This is the structurally interesting part. A parameterisation that captures 30% of half-hourly variance can still give a good annual global flux, because the errors are largely random at short timescales and average out over a large enough area and time.

What does not average out is any systematic dependence on a variable that is itself distributed unevenly. If low-wind convective enhancement is missing from the formula, and low-wind conditions are concentrated in particular regions and seasons, then the regional flux is biased even though the global total is approximately right. The same applies to bubble-mediated transfer at high wind speeds, which is concentrated in the storm tracks.

The practical consequence is that global ocean uptake is better known than the map of where it happens, and studies that use the map — to attribute a trend to a region, or to validate a model's spatial pattern — are working with a weaker quantity than the headline number suggests.

## The connection to everything else at the surface

The same thin layer governs the exchange of oxygen, of dimethyl sulfide, and of heat, and the same turbulence controls all of them. It also sits directly above the density structure that determines [stratification and nutrient supply](/en/ecology/oceans/stratification-mixing-and-nutrient-supply): a strongly stratified surface layer both suppresses nutrient resupply from below and changes how quickly a dissolved gas taken up at the surface is carried away from it.

And the chemistry on the water side is not passive. Dissolved CO₂ reacts, and the carbonate system's buffering determines how much the surface concentration rises for a given uptake — the same chemistry described in [ocean acidification](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-acidification-explained), read as a control on future uptake rather than as an impact.
