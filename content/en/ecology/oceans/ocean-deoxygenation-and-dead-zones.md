Below the top few metres, seawater has no way of acquiring oxygen. Every molecule in the ocean interior was dissolved at the surface, or released there by photosynthesis, and then carried down by sinking water. Interior oxygen is therefore a balance between a supply term set by circulation and a demand term set by respiration, and anything that slows the supply or raises the demand draws the balance down. Two very different human perturbations do exactly that, in different places, on different timescales, and they are frequently reported as though they were one phenomenon. Because the supply side is governed by the layering that [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained) treats as its master variable, dissolved oxygen doubles as one of the most sensitive available tracers of how fast the interior is ventilated at all. What follows takes the two mechanisms apart and asks what is actually measured for each.

## The solubility term is real but small

Warm water holds less dissolved gas than cold water, so a warming ocean must lose oxygen for purely physical reasons. That is the part of the story most often told, and it is the smaller part. The IPCC's Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere assesses that the open ocean lost a *very likely* 0.5 to 3.3 per cent of its oxygen between 1970 and 2010 in the layer from the surface to 1,000 m, at medium confidence, and states with high confidence that processes other than solubility [cause the majority of the decline](https://www.ipcc.ch/srocc/chapter/chapter-5/).

Those other processes are circulation and biology. As the upper ocean warms and freshens at high latitudes it becomes less dense relative to the water beneath it — the same report puts the increase in stratification of the upper 200 m at 2.18 to 2.42 per cent between 1970 and 2017 — and a more strongly stratified column ventilates its interior more slowly. Less ventilation means less resupply, and a longer residence time also means more accumulated respiratory demand before the water surfaces again. The link between the warming signal and the circulation response is developed in the article on [ocean circulation and climate](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate), and the heat input driving it is tracked as [ocean heat content](/en/glossary/ocean-heat-content).

The whole-inventory measurement is consistent with this. A 2017 analysis in Nature that assembled the complete water column over five decades put the global ocean's oxygen content at 227.4 ± 1.1 petamoles and found it had [fallen by more than 2 per cent](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature21399), or 4.8 ± 2.1 petamoles, since 1960, with the loss distributed very unevenly by basin and depth. The uncertainty on that change is 44 per cent of the change itself, which is a fair summary of how well this quantity is known.

## Oxygen minimum zones and an uncertainty that will not close

Parts of the tropical ocean sit permanently close to the threshold. Where sluggish circulation meets high surface production — the eastern tropical Pacific, the Arabian Sea, the eastern tropical Atlantic — a mid-depth layer develops in which concentrations fall low enough to exclude most large animals. These oxygen minimum zones are conventionally delimited at 80 µmol/L in assessment work, and their volume is one of the most quoted indicators of deoxygenation.

It is also one of the least well constrained. The same IPCC assessment gives a *very likely* range of 3 to 8 per cent for observed expansion, but immediately notes substantial decadal variability that complicates attributing the change to human activity in the tropics. The projection is more revealing still: under a high-emissions scenario the volume below 80 µmol/L is expected to grow by 7.0 ± 5.6 per cent by 2100, while under a low-emissions scenario it shows virtually no change. An uncertainty of ±5.6 on a central estimate of 7.0 is not a rounding detail: the bottom of that very likely range is a fifth of its midpoint. The same chapter is explicit that projections for the tropical zones specifically are the least reliable part of the picture, because oxygen loss from warming there is opposed by reduced respiratory demand if export production declines, and because the models resolve the tropics poorly. Anyone quoting a single figure for oxygen-minimum-zone expansion is quoting the midpoint of a range that was never tight.

## Coastal hypoxia is a different machine

Nothing in the previous two sections explains a dead zone. Coastal hypoxia is driven by nutrients: nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser, wastewater and atmospheric deposition arrive by river, fuel an algal bloom far above the local baseline, and the bloom sinks and decomposes. Bacterial respiration then strips oxygen from bottom water. The trigger that turns a bloom into a dead zone is physical — a density interface, usually the freshwater plume of a large river sitting above denser seawater, that prevents oxygenated surface water from mixing down. The US Environmental Protection Agency describes exactly this sequence for the Mississippi and Atchafalaya plume, and defines the hypoxic condition at less than [2 to 3 milligrams per litre](https://www.epa.gov/ms-htf/hypoxia-101).

The scale is regional rather than global, and it was already large when it was first inventoried. A 2008 review in Science reported dead zones from [more than 400 systems](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1156401) covering over 245,000 km², spreading roughly exponentially since the 1960s. A [2018 synthesis in the same journal](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aam7240) set the coastal and open-ocean processes side by side and identified the common mechanisms — increased microbial consumption, reduced solubility, and slower resupply from the atmosphere to the interior — while keeping the drivers distinct.

## The 2 mg/L line is a convention

That threshold is worth interrogating, because it is used as though it were biological. A comparative analysis of experimental tolerance data across marine benthic organisms, published in PNAS in 2008, found that thresholds vary enormously between taxa and that the conventional 2 mg/L definition lies [below the limits for half the species tested](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0803833105). Crustaceans and fish generally fail well above it; some bivalves and worms tolerate far less. The practical implication is that mapped hypoxic area, defined at a single concentration, systematically undercounts the water in which ecological function has already been lost — sublethal effects on growth, reproduction and predator avoidance begin higher up. Because oxygen availability sets which animals can occupy a depth layer at all, the consequences run through food-web structure and the vertical migrations described in [marine productivity and food webs](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity).

## What happens when the nutrient load is cut

The coastal mechanism is reversible in principle, which is why the two problems should not be reported together: one has a management lever and the other does not. The record on pulling that lever is mixed.

The longest instrumental series is the annual mid-summer survey of the Gulf of Mexico hypoxic zone, run since 1985. NOAA reported the 2025 zone at approximately [4,402 square miles](https://coastalscience.noaa.gov/news/below-average-summer-2025-dead-zone-measured-in-gulf/), the fifteenth smallest in 39 years of measurement, with a five-year running average of 4,755 square miles. The interagency task force target is a five-year average below 1,900 square miles by 2035, so the current average remains more than twice the goal after decades of nutrient-reduction effort. Year-to-year variation is dominated by river discharge, which is why a single small year says very little.

Europe's picture is similar in shape. The European Environment Agency's assessment of nutrients in transitional, coastal and marine waters, published in June 2025, covers 1980 to 2023 and finds that over 91 to 94 per cent of the assessed time series show [no significant change](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/indicators/nutrients-in-transitional-coastal-and). Where trends do emerge, dissolved inorganic nitrogen is decreasing in more assessment areas than it is increasing across the Baltic and Greater North Seas — a genuine response to wastewater treatment and agricultural regulation — but for phosphate the balance runs the other way in the Baltic and Black Seas.

Two things make recovery slower than the input reduction alone would suggest. Sediments store decades of accumulated organic matter and keep consuming oxygen after loading falls. And warming works against nutrient management directly, by lowering solubility and strengthening the stratification that isolates bottom water, so a given nutrient cut buys less oxygen in a warmer estuary than it did thirty years ago. Coastal hypoxia is a soluble problem in the sense that its cause is known and controllable; it is not a fast one, and where it interacts with the same warming signal that drives open-ocean loss and [ocean acidification](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-acidification-explained), the two mechanisms stop being fully separable at the coast.
