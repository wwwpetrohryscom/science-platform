NOAA Ocean Exploration states that more than 90 per cent of the ocean is deep ocean — water below 200 metres — and that explorers have directly seen less than 0.001 per cent of the deep-sea floor, an area roughly the size of Rhode Island. Those two figures sit oddly together, and holding them together is the whole discipline: the largest continuous habitat on the planet is described almost entirely from samples, sensors and inference rather than from observation.

The vertical structure that produces it is set out in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained). What follows is what that structure does to the organisms living inside it, and how much of the resulting picture is actually evidence.

## Pressure is the least of it

Popular accounts of the deep sea lead with pressure. It is the least interesting of the three constraints, because it is the easiest to accommodate: an organism with no gas-filled spaces experiences high pressure largely as a biochemical problem, solved by protein and membrane adaptations rather than by structure. Pressure rises by roughly one atmosphere for every ten metres of depth, so NOAA's figure of about 40 to more than 110 times surface pressure describes the upper reaches of the deep ocean; in the hadal trenches it is an order of magnitude higher again. NOAA puts the average temperature of the deep ocean at about 4 °C.

Light is the constraint that defines the zones. The upper 200 metres is the euphotic zone; between 200 and 1,000 metres lies the dysphotic or twilight zone, where light is present but too weak for photosynthesis; below 1,000 metres is the aphotic zone, in permanent darkness.

| Zone | Depth | Defining condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Euphotic | 0–200 m | Enough light for net photosynthesis |
| Dysphotic (twilight) | 200–1,000 m | Light detectable, insufficient for production |
| Aphotic | Below 1,000 m | Sunlight absent; average temperature near 4 °C |
| Hadal | 6,000–11,000 m | Isolated trenches and troughs, together about half the area of Australia |

The third constraint, and the operative one, is food. With no local photosynthesis, almost everything below the twilight zone depends on organic matter sinking from the layer above — the export flux described in [marine productivity](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity) — supplemented by animals that migrate up to feed at night and respire at depth by day. The magnitude of that second pathway is large and poorly pinned down: an acoustic analysis published in Nature Communications revised the standing estimate of mesopelagic fish biomass, then about 1,000 million tonnes, upward by at least an order of magnitude, and calculated that these fish may respire around 10 per cent of primary production in deep water. A revision of that size in the biomass of the ocean's most abundant vertebrates, arrived at within the last fifteen years, is a fair measure of how loosely constrained the deep sea remains.

## Two ways to build an ecosystem without sunlight

In 1977, scientists exploring an oceanic spreading ridge near the Galápagos Islands found hydrothermal vents surrounded by dense communities of animals nobody had seen before. NOAA notes that water in these vents may exceed 700 °F, roughly 370 °C, and that the communities depend on chemical processes driven by the interaction of seawater with hot magma rather than on light. Chemosynthetic microbes — oxidising sulphide, methane or hydrogen — occupy the position that photosynthesis occupies everywhere else, and larger animals either graze them or host them internally.

Seven years later the second variety appeared, and it mattered because it removed the volcanic prerequisite. A 1984 paper in Science described dense communities of large epifaunal animals, resembling those at East Pacific Rise vents, at the base of the Florida Escarpment in the abyssal Gulf of Mexico — a passive continental margin with no magma anywhere near it. That fauna was nourished by sulphide-rich hypersaline water seeping out at near-ambient temperatures. Chemosynthesis, in other words, did not require heat; it required a chemical gradient.

The conceptual consequence reached well beyond marine biology. Once an ecosystem could be shown to run on chemical disequilibrium at around 4 °C in complete darkness, the set of environments that might plausibly support life expanded — which is why deep-sea vents and seeps appear routinely in discussions of subsurface habitability elsewhere in the solar system.

## Counting what has not been collected

Deep-sea biodiversity figures are estimates built on a partial register, and the honest ones say so. A global compilation published in Current Biology reported roughly 226,000 described eukaryotic marine species, and partitioned the remainder explicitly: 58,000 to 72,000 species already collected but not yet described, and a further 482,000 to 741,000 not yet sampled at all. The total, allowing for cryptic species revealed by molecular methods, was put at 0.7 to 1.0 million marine species — with the authors noting that earlier estimates of well over a million now appear unlikely. In more than a hundred recent field studies, an average of 37 per cent of species encountered might be new to science.

NOAA Ocean Exploration quotes the same range — 700,000 to 1 million species, mostly animals and excluding most microorganisms — and adds that roughly two-thirds have yet to be described, with almost 2,000 new species accepted each year.

What these estimates rest on is worth stating plainly, because they are frequently quoted as if they were counts. They rest on extrapolating from the rate at which new species turn up in samples, on the ratio of described to undescribed material in collections, and on taxonomic expert judgement about how much of each group has been worked through. Every one of those inputs is sensitive to where sampling has happened. The methodological issues are the marine version of those set out in [species richness](/en/ecology/biodiversity/species-richness-explained): effort, area and detection probability shape the number before biology does. In the deep sea the effort term is extreme — sampling is concentrated near a handful of institutions, along a few transects, and at depths a research vessel can conveniently work.

## Mapping is not exploring

The distinction between having a map and having seen something is unusually sharp here. The entire seafloor has been mapped using satellite data, but at a resolution that gives only a general picture, missing features as large as an individual seamount; the technique and its limits are covered in [satellite altimetry](/en/ecology/earth-observation/satellite-altimetry-explained). High-resolution multibeam sonar had covered 28.7 per cent of the global seafloor as of April 2026, against about 56 per cent of the seafloor beneath United States waters. Direct observation remains three orders of magnitude behind that. A map showing a seamount does not say what lives on it, and this gap is the reason environmental baselines for the deep sea are so often absent when they are most needed.

## What the recovery evidence for mining actually shows

Polymetallic nodule mining is the case where the sampling gap has immediate consequences, because the relevant question — how long an abyssal community takes to recover — can only be answered by revisiting a disturbed site decades later. One such site exists. A study published in Nature revisited the track of a test-mining experiment 44 years afterwards and found that biological impacts persist across many groups of organisms, alongside persistent physical alteration of the seafloor, while populations of sediment macrofauna, mobile deposit feeders and some large sessile animals had begun to re-establish. Areas affected only by the sediment plume showed limited detectable residual impact, with some assemblages comparable in abundance to control areas.

The authors' own framing is the appropriate one: mining impacts in the abyss will persist over at least decadal timescales, and directly disturbed communities will remain altered despite some recolonisation. Note also the caveat they attach — modern collector designs may cause less physical impact than the equipment used in that test. That single site carries a great deal of evidential weight for a decision affecting a seabed area measured in millions of square kilometres, which is a statement about the state of the evidence rather than about the seabed.

## The gear decides what you find

One limitation deserves separating from the rest, because it distorts the record systematically rather than randomly. Nearly everything known about deep-sea communities comes from equipment that selects: trawls destroy fragile gelatinous animals and under-sample fast swimmers; sediment cores capture small infauna but nothing mobile; baited cameras attract scavengers and miss everything that does not scavenge; and remotely operated vehicles see conspicuous megafauna while their lights and thrusters displace the rest. Each method returns a different community from the same site. Chemical properties are better behaved — the aragonite saturation horizon discussed in [ocean acidification](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-acidification-explained) can be measured on a water sample — but for biology, a deep-sea species list is as much a description of the sampling gear as of the seafloor.
