Dissolve carbon dioxide in seawater and two things happen at once. The total amount of dissolved inorganic carbon goes up, and the concentration of carbonate ions goes down. Almost every consequence discussed under the heading of ocean acidification follows from that second, less obvious half.

The reaction chain is short. CO₂ dissolves and forms carbonic acid; carbonic acid dissociates to bicarbonate and a hydrogen ion; a small fraction of bicarbonate dissociates further to carbonate and a second hydrogen ion. Adding CO₂ pushes the system toward bicarbonate, and the extra hydrogen ions react with the carbonate already present to make yet more bicarbonate. The net effect is more carbon, more acidity, and fewer carbonate ions — the raw material that corals, molluscs, echinoderms and calcareous plankton use to build calcium carbonate structures. This is a downstream consequence of the same uptake described in the [global carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained), and it is the reason the sea's role as a [carbon sink](/en/glossary/carbon-sink) is not cost-free. How quickly that surface signal reaches the interior is set by the layering described in [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained).

## Saturation state, not pH, is the biologically relevant number

The quantity that determines whether calcium carbonate can form or will dissolve is the saturation state Ω, defined as the product of calcium and carbonate ion concentrations divided by the mineral's solubility product. Above Ω = 1 the water is supersaturated and precipitation is thermodynamically favoured; below it, existing carbonate structures tend to dissolve unless the organism spends energy protecting them.

Two mineral forms matter, and they behave differently. Aragonite — used by reef-building corals and by pteropods — is more soluble than calcite, so aragonite saturation falls below one at shallower depths. The depth where Ω = 1 is the **saturation horizon**, and it is rising. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment reports high confidence in the shoaling of carbonate saturation horizons, at roughly 1–2 metres per year in the Pacific, where the aragonite horizon already sits between a few hundred metres and 1,200 metres, and far faster in parts of the subpolar North Atlantic: 4 metres per year to a depth of 1,710 metres in the Iceland Sea over 1984–2008, and 10–15 metres per year to 2,250 metres in the Irminger Sea over 1991–2016. Organisms that live near that boundary — including calcifiers in the cold, dark habitats described in [deep-sea ecosystems](/en/ecology/oceans/deep-sea-ecosystems) — do not need surface chemistry to change much for their habitat to move.

## What has actually been measured

The surface trend is one of the better-constrained signals in marine chemistry, because it is driven by a well-measured atmospheric forcing and recorded at long-running time-series stations. The European Environment Agency's indicator, built on the Hawaii station ALOHA record and Copernicus Marine Service estimates, puts global mean surface seawater pH at 8.11 in 1985 and 8.04 in 2024 — about an 18 per cent increase in hydrogen ion concentration over that period. The IPCC assesses the very likely rate of surface pH decline at 0.016 to 0.020 units per decade in the subtropics, equivalent to roughly a 4 per cent rise in hydrogen ion concentration per decade, and 0.002 to 0.026 units per decade in subpolar and polar zones.

The regional spread is not noise. It tracks how much old, CO₂-rich subsurface water reaches the surface.

| Region | Assessed surface pH trend | What drives the difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subtropical open ocean | −0.016 to −0.019 per decade | Near-equilibration with rising atmospheric CO₂ |
| Central and eastern tropical Pacific | −0.022 to −0.026 per decade | Upwelling of CO₂-rich subsurface water on top of uptake |
| Western tropical Pacific warm pool | −0.010 to −0.013 per decade | Surface water carrying an older, extratropical CO₂ signal |
| Subpolar and polar zones | −0.002 to −0.026 per decade | Sea-ice loss, freshwater input and sparse time series |

Aragonite saturation has fallen alongside pH, at 0.07 to 0.12 per decade in subtropical surface waters. The signal is no longer confined to the surface: the IPCC notes that acidification has penetrated beyond 2,000 metres in the northern North Atlantic and the Southern Ocean, carried down by the same water masses that ventilate the interior.

## Why the word is chemically exact and rhetorically awkward

Seawater is alkaline and will remain so on any assessed pathway. A pH of 8.04 is not acidic, and the largest projected change in the IPCC assessment — a decline of 0.44 ± 0.005 units between 1870–1899 and 2080–2099 under the highest-emissions scenario in CMIP6 — leaves the surface ocean well above 7. "Acidification" names a direction of travel — a decrease in pH — in the same way that "warming" names a direction without implying that something is hot. That is standard chemical usage, and it is also, predictably, the point at which the terminology gets attacked.

Two habits make the argument worse than it needs to be. The first is quoting percentage changes in acidity without the baseline: NOAA's Ocean Acidification Program states that the ocean has become about 26 per cent more acidic on average over the past 250 years, while the European indicator gives about 18 per cent for 1985 to 2024. Both are correct; they are different intervals, and a percentage change in hydrogen ion concentration is meaningless without one. The second is treating pH as the biological variable when saturation state usually is. An organism building aragonite responds to Ω, and Ω can fall for reasons — temperature, upwelling, freshwater dilution — that pH alone does not capture.

## The biology is real, uneven, and smaller than early work suggested

The most useful synthesis remains a meta-analysis of 228 studies published in Global Change Biology, which found that pooling across the full range of marine organisms yields decreased survival, calcification, growth, development and abundance under acidification. The pooled result conceals most of the interesting structure. Sensitivity varies systematically among taxonomic groups, mollusc larvae are notably vulnerable while enhanced sensitivity of early life stages is not universal, and effects are amplified when acidification is combined with elevated temperature. The same analysis reported that variability in species' responses increases when organisms are exposed in multi-species assemblages, and cautioned explicitly against forecasting abundance from single-species laboratory experiments.

That caution proved well placed in the one sub-field where it has been tested hardest. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 91 studies on the direct effects of acidification on coral-reef fish behaviour, published in PLOS Biology, documented a severe **decline effect**: large effect sizes reported in early studies had all but vanished in later ones, the largest effects came disproportionately from small-sample studies in high-impact journals, and three plausible biological explanations for the decline failed to account for it. Its authors contend that acidification has a negligible direct impact on fish behaviour. That conclusion has not gone unchallenged: a formal comment in the same journal argues that the reported decline is a statistical artefact arising from how zero-percentage values were handled in the effect-size calculation, together with data errors and exclusions. This is therefore a live methodological dispute rather than a closed question — but the underlying pattern, that effect sizes in this literature have shrunk as sample sizes and replication improved, is not itself in doubt.

## What this leaves open

Three areas remain genuinely unresolved. Adaptation over many generations is poorly constrained, because most experiments run for weeks to months on organisms whose populations may respond over decades; the handful of multi-generational studies are not yet enough to generalise from. Coastal chemistry is far more variable than open-ocean chemistry, driven by river inputs, nutrient loading and local respiration, so a coastal shellfish bed can experience swings larger than a century of open-ocean change within a single season, and attributing harm there to atmospheric CO₂ specifically is difficult. Those same coastal regions compound the problem: the IPCC notes that low oxygen, low pH and shallow aragonite saturation horizons co-occur in the oxygen minimum zones of eastern boundary upwelling regions, which means the stressors covered under [deoxygenation](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-deoxygenation-and-dead-zones) and those covered here arrive together in exactly the places that support the largest fisheries. And ecosystem-level outcomes — as opposed to organism-level responses — remain largely projected rather than observed, because the field experiments that would settle them are hard to run at the necessary scale.

What is not uncertain is the forcing. As long as the ocean continues to take up roughly 30 per cent of the CO₂ released to the atmosphere, as NOAA's National Ocean Service puts it, the carbonate chemistry will keep moving in the same direction, and it will keep doing so in whatever emissions scenario continues to raise atmospheric concentrations. The chemistry is the well-constrained part; the biology is where the honest uncertainty lies, and the reefs at the shallow end of that gradient are examined in [coral reefs and bleaching](/en/ecology/oceans/coral-reef-ecology-and-bleaching).
