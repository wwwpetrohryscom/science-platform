Depth organises the sea, as [ocean science](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-science-explained) has it, and nowhere more consequentially than here: the surface ocean has light and no nutrients. The deep ocean has nutrients and no light. Almost everything about where marine production happens follows from the fact that these two resources are vertically separated and that the separation is actively maintained.

## The barrier

NOAA describes the structure that keeps them apart: a thermocline is "the transition layer between warmer mixed water at the ocean's surface and cooler deep water below", within which "the temperature decreases rapidly from the mixed layer temperature to the much colder deep water temperature."

Warm water is less dense than cold, so a heated surface layer floats on the cold interior, and moving anything across the boundary requires work against that density difference. Where the boundary is strong, nutrients taken up by phytoplankton in the lit layer sink out of it and are not replaced.

The strength varies in a way that predicts productivity. The same NOAA description notes the thermocline is "semi-permanent in the tropics, variable in temperate regions (often deepest during the summer), and shallow to nonexistent in the polar regions." A permanently stratified tropical gyre is a nutrient desert with abundant light; a temperate shelf mixes each winter and blooms each spring; polar water mixes readily and is limited by light and season rather than by supply.

## Upwelling: the exception that makes the rule visible

Where wind moves surface water aside, deeper water rises to replace it, and the effect is dramatic. NOAA's description is compact: "Winds blowing across the ocean surface push water away. Water then rises up from beneath the surface to replace the water that was pushed away."

The consequence follows immediately: upwelled water "is typically colder and is rich in nutrients. These nutrients 'fertilize' surface waters, meaning that these surface waters often have high biological productivity", and therefore "good fishing grounds typically are found where upwelling is common."

Upwelling occurs "in the open ocean and along coastlines", with coastal conditions favourable "when winds blow along the shore". The geometry matters: alongshore wind, not onshore wind, is what drives coastal upwelling, because the surface transport that removes water is at an angle to the wind rather than along it.

The important structural point is that these regions are a small fraction of ocean area and a large fraction of fish catch. Production is concentrated wherever the vertical barrier is broken.

## Why this is a transport problem, not a chemistry problem

Nothing here is about the nutrients being scarce in absolute terms. The deep ocean holds an enormous inventory of nitrate and phosphate. The limitation is entirely one of delivery across a density gradient — which is the marine instance of the general result about [transport by diffusion and by flow](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/transport-by-diffusion-and-by-flow): molecular diffusion is useless at these distances, so everything depends on whether bulk motion is available.

That framing predicts the consequences of change more reliably than a nutrient budget does. Warming the surface increases the density contrast, which strengthens stratification, which reduces the supply. The effect does not require any change in the nutrient inventory at all.

## What is genuinely uncertain

Three things are worth separating from the settled part.

**How much stratification has already changed, and where.** Trends in mixed-layer depth are harder to measure than surface temperature trends, because they depend on the profile rather than the surface, and the historical profile record is sparser.

**How productivity responds.** Reduced supply lowers production in nutrient-limited regions, but the same warming lengthens growing seasons and reduces light limitation at high latitudes. The sign of the global response is not the sign of the local one, and different regions move in different directions.

**What happens to the food web above it.** Changes in the size structure of the phytoplankton community can alter how much production reaches higher [trophic levels](/en/ecology/ecosystems/food-webs-and-trophic-structure) even when total production is stable, which is one of the ways [marine productivity](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity) and fishery yield decouple.
