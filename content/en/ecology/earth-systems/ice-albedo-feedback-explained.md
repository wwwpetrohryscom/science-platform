Of all the amplifying loops in the climate system, this is the one that is easiest to explain and the one most often over-credited. Bright surfaces reflect sunlight; dark surfaces absorb it; melting ice replaces the first with the second. The mechanism is genuine and well quantified, and it is a textbook case of the [component coupling that Earth system science exists to study](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-science-explained). Its global magnitude is smaller than the intuition suggests, and understanding why is more instructive than the mechanism itself.

## The mechanism, with the numbers attached

[Albedo](/en/glossary/albedo) is the fraction of incoming shortwave radiation a surface reflects. The contrast across a melting ice edge is large: NSIDC reports that bare sea ice reflects between 40 and 60 per cent of incident sunlight and that snow is more reflective still, returning up to 90 per cent. Open ocean, by contrast, absorbs the overwhelming majority of the light that reaches it.

When ice retreats, the newly exposed water absorbs energy that was previously reflected, warming the surface and promoting further retreat. The loop closes, which is the definition of a positive [climate feedback](/en/glossary/climate-feedback).

The assessed strength is where the intuition needs adjusting. AR6 puts the global surface-albedo feedback at +0.35 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, with a very likely range of 0.10 to 0.60 and a likely range of 0.25 to 0.45. The assessment attaches high confidence to the sign: the basic phenomena are well understood, and estimates from CMIP5 models, CMIP6 models, satellite observations, and theory agree at the global scale. Confidence in the magnitude is stated slightly differently in different parts of the chapter — the summary table records medium confidence for this term, while the section text attaches high confidence to the assessed range — so the honest reading is that the sign is settled and the value is not tightly pinned.

For comparison, the [water-vapour and lapse-rate feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/water-vapor-feedback-explained) is assessed at +1.30, nearly four times larger. Surface albedo is the smallest of the three major amplifiers in the global mean.

## Why the global number is modest

Three constraints limit how much a large local contrast can do globally.

**Area.** Sea ice and seasonal snow cover a small fraction of the planet's surface. A large reflectivity change over a small area is a small change in the global energy budget.

**Sunlight.** The polar regions receive weak, highly oblique sunlight, and during the winter months when sea-ice extent is greatest they receive almost none. Ice lost in the dark contributes nothing to the feedback. This seasonality is why September Arctic minimum extent — the metric most often quoted — is not the metric that maximises the albedo effect.

**Clouds.** Polar skies are frequently overcast, and a cloud deck already reflects much of the incoming light before it reaches the surface. The albedo contrast that matters is the one seen from the top of the atmosphere, not the one at the surface, and cloud cover substantially mutes it. AR6 notes that the assessed studies span a range of hypotheses about how cloud evolution modifies this feedback.

## Where it does dominate: polar amplification

The global mean understates the regional effect severely. High latitudes warm faster than the global average, and surface albedo change is one of several contributors to that pattern, alongside changes in atmospheric and oceanic heat transport, the vertical structure of polar warming, and reduced insulation as sea ice thins. NOAA's Arctic Report Card describes the region as warming far faster than the rest of the planet.

The regional consequences follow from the regional magnitude rather than the global one: earlier melt onset, longer open-water seasons, warmer coastal permafrost, and altered stratification of the upper ocean. These are handled together in [the cryosphere in the Earth system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/cryosphere-in-the-earth-system), and the indicator side is covered in the existing article on [cryosphere indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/cryosphere-indicators-glaciers-sea-ice).

## What it is not

The [ice-albedo feedback](/en/glossary/ice-albedo-feedback) is frequently invoked to support the idea that summer sea-ice loss is a tipping point beyond which recovery is impossible. AR6's cross-chapter assessment is explicit in the other direction: it classifies Arctic *summer* sea ice as **not** subject to abrupt change, with high confidence, and as reversible within years to decades, also with high confidence, while projecting likely complete loss under continued warming. Arctic *winter* sea ice is assessed as capable of abrupt change with high confidence, but also as reversible on the same timescale.

The distinction matters. "Likely to be lost" and "irreversible once lost" are different claims, and the evidence supports the first without supporting the second for summer ice. The wider treatment of which components genuinely qualify is in [Earth-system tipping points](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-tipping-points).

## Measurement and its limits

The feedback is estimated in two independent ways, and their agreement is what supports the assessed value.

Satellite radiation budget instruments measure top-of-atmosphere reflected shortwave directly, so the albedo change can be observed rather than inferred — but the record is short relative to the variability, and separating a forced trend from interannual noise takes decades of data. Model-based kernel estimates decompose simulated radiative changes into feedback components, but they inherit each model's sea-ice and snow biases.

AR6 notes that Southern Hemisphere albedo feedback is likely larger in models than in observations, though it contributes only about a quarter of the global total, and that hemispheric differences and the role of internal variability need further work. The satellite retrieval side of this problem is covered in [remote-sensing limitations and uncertainty](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty).
