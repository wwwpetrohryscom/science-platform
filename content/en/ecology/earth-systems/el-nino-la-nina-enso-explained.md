The single most consequential recurring pattern in the climate system is not a trend. It is an oscillation in the tropical Pacific that reorganises circulation across half the planet every few years, and it exists because the ocean and atmosphere there are coupled tightly enough to push each other.

## The neutral state, and why it is unstable

NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory describes the ordinary configuration: trade winds blow westward along the equator from South America toward Asia, dragging warm surface water with them and piling it up in the western Pacific. The consequence is a sloping thermocline — the boundary NOAA identifies with the depth of the 20 °C isotherm — that sits deep in the west and shallow in the east.

In the east, where the thermocline is shallow, the trade winds drive upwelling that brings cool, nutrient-rich water to the surface. That cool eastern surface reinforces the east–west temperature contrast, which strengthens the trades, which strengthens the upwelling. This mutual reinforcement between wind and ocean temperature is what makes the system capable of oscillating: any weakening of the winds warms the east, which weakens the winds further.

## The two phases

During **[El Niño](/en/glossary/el-nino)**, NOAA describes the trade winds relaxing in the central and western Pacific. Warm water sloshes eastward, the thermocline deepens in the east and shoals in the west, and the eastern upwelling loses its efficiency at cooling the surface and its supply of nutrient-rich water to the sunlit layer. The atmospheric convection that normally sits over the western Pacific migrates east with the warm water, which is the mechanism by which a Pacific temperature anomaly becomes a global rainfall anomaly.

During **[La Niña](/en/glossary/la-nina)**, the same loop runs in the opposite direction: stronger trades, a steeper thermocline, more vigorous eastern upwelling, and a cooler than average eastern Pacific.

Neither phase is a departure from a static normal. Both are excursions of one coupled system, which is why the family name — the El Niño–Southern Oscillation — refers to the oscillation rather than to either end of it.

## The operational definition

Public discussion often treats "El Niño" as a mood. NOAA treats it as a threshold crossing on a defined index, and knowing the definition is what allows a claim to be checked.

The **Oceanic Niño Index** is a running three-month mean of sea-surface temperature anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region — the box spanning 5°N to 5°S and 120°W to 170°W — measured against a thirty-year climatological baseline. NOAA identifies El Niño conditions at an ONI of +0.5 °C or above and La Niña conditions at −0.5 °C or below.

Crucially, conditions are not an episode. To be classified as a full El Niño or La Niña event in NOAA's historical record, the threshold must be met for at least **five consecutive overlapping three-month seasons**. A month above threshold is not an event, and the distinction is the source of much confusion in reporting.

The pattern shifts back and forth irregularly every two to seven years. "Irregularly" is doing real work in that sentence: the recurrence is not periodic, which is why ENSO cannot be predicted by extrapolation.

## Why it matters beyond the Pacific

Three consequences propagate outward.

**Global mean temperature.** Because El Niño moves heat from the ocean interior to the surface and La Niña does the reverse, individual years are warmer or cooler than the underlying trend depending on ENSO phase. This is the main reason a single record-warm year is weak evidence about trend, a point developed in the existing article on [global temperature records](/en/ecology/climate-change/global-temperature-records-explained).

**Rainfall.** The displacement of tropical convection alters precipitation across the Pacific rim, and through atmospheric wave propagation reaches much further — the mechanism covered in [teleconnections](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-variability-and-teleconnections).

**The carbon cycle.** The IPCC notes that interannual variability in atmospheric methane is dominated by ENSO cycles, through their effect on biomass burning, wetland emissions, and atmospheric chemistry. Land carbon uptake varies with ENSO for similar reasons, which is why the annual [carbon budget](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained) has a visible ENSO signature.

## Prediction, and its ceiling

ENSO is the reason seasonal forecasting works at all. Because the ocean's heat content changes slowly and constrains the atmosphere, an ocean state observed today carries information about atmospheric conditions months ahead — the difference between a forecast that depends on initial conditions and one that depends on slowly varying boundary conditions, discussed in [Earth-system predictability](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-predictability-explained).

The ceiling on that skill is real. Forecasts issued across the northern spring have systematically lower skill, and the amplitude of an event is harder to predict than its sign. AR6 also projects that variability in ENSO-related rainfall will increase significantly by the second half of the century under mid- and high-emissions scenarios, regardless of whether the amplitude of the sea-surface temperature variability itself changes — a distinction worth preserving, since the two are often reported as one.

## What the index does not capture

Two limits are worth stating.

The ONI reduces a three-dimensional coupled state to a single number over one rectangle of ocean. Events with similar ONI values can have quite different spatial patterns — warming concentrated in the central Pacific behaves differently from warming concentrated in the east — and those differences change the regional impacts substantially.

And the baseline moves. Because the index is defined against a thirty-year climatology that is periodically updated, a given absolute temperature can fall on different sides of the threshold depending on which base period is in use. Comparing historical event catalogues compiled under different baselines is a known source of error. The observing infrastructure behind all of this, including the equatorial mooring arrays and the satellite records, is covered in [ocean-colour observations](/en/ecology/earth-observation/ocean-color-observations) and the wider [Earth observation](/en/ecology/earth-observation/earth-observation-and-remote-sensing-explained) cluster.
