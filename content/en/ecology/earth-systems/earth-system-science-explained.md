A hurricane is a weather event, a fishery collapse is an ecology problem, and a shift in the Sahara's rainfall belt is a climatology question. That is how the sciences were organised for most of the twentieth century, and it worked well enough as long as each field stayed inside its own boundary. It stopped working when the questions people most wanted answered — how much warming follows a given emission, whether a forest will keep absorbing carbon, why a Pacific temperature anomaly changes East African rainfall — turned out to depend on what happens *between* the components rather than inside any one of them.

Earth system science is the response to that. It treats the [Earth system](/en/glossary/earth-system) as a single coupled object: air, water, ice, rock and soil, and life, exchanging energy and matter continuously, with the exchanges themselves as the primary object of study. This article is the entry point to EcoScienceHub's Earth-system cluster and sets out the framework the supporting articles use.

## What the field actually studies

The subject matter is not a list of components. It is the set of couplings between them, and four questions about those couplings recur throughout the field.

**Where does the energy go?** Sunlight enters, infrared radiation leaves, and in between the energy is stored, transported, and converted. The accounting is covered in the applied-physics articles on [Earth's energy balance](/en/physics/energy/solar-radiation-and-earth-energy-balance) and the [planetary heat engine](/en/physics/thermodynamics/earth-energy-budget-and-the-second-law); the Earth-system question is which reservoir absorbs the surplus and how quickly it moves between them.

**Where does the matter go?** Carbon, water, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulphur move between reservoirs on their own characteristic timescales. The shared grammar for describing that movement — reservoirs, fluxes, and [residence time](/en/glossary/residence-time) — is set out in [biogeochemical cycles](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biogeochemical-cycles-explained), and worked through in detail for carbon, water, and nitrogen.

**What amplifies or damps a change?** A perturbation to one component alters another, which alters the first. These loops — [climate feedbacks](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-feedback-mechanisms) — determine how much response a given push produces.

**What is predictable, and over what horizon?** Some behaviour is forced and follows the push; some is internally generated and merely oscillates. Separating the two is the subject of predictability, and the reason a two-week weather forecast and a century-scale projection are different kinds of statement.

## The components and their clocks

The conventional division is into five components, described individually in [Earth system components](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-components-explained): the atmosphere, the hydrosphere, the cryosphere, the biosphere, and the lithosphere or geosphere. The division is a convenience, not a physical boundary — soil moisture belongs to three of them at once, and sea ice sits at the junction of two more.

What makes the division useful is that each component has a characteristic response time, and those times differ by many orders of magnitude. The atmosphere mixes globally within weeks to a year or two and holds only about 12,900 cubic kilometres of water at any moment, roughly a thousandth of one per cent of the planet's total, which NASA notes is cycled through some 495,000 cubic kilometres a year — a complete turnover in a matter of days. The surface ocean responds over years, the deep ocean over centuries; NOAA estimates that a parcel of water takes on the order of a thousand years to complete a circuit of the global overturning circulation. Ice sheets respond over millennia, and the rock cycle over hundreds of millions of years — NASA's Earth Observatory describes carbon taking 100 to 200 million years to move through the slow cycle between rocks, soil, ocean, and air.

That spread of timescales is why the same forcing produces effects that arrive at wildly different times, and why "the climate has adjusted" is never a single statement. It is also why a fast measurement can mislead: the atmosphere's temperature is the noisiest indicator in the system precisely because it is the component with the least thermal inertia.

## Energy: one budget, several destinations

Averaged over the sphere and over the year, the planet absorbs roughly 240 watts per square metre of sunlight and emits about the same in infrared. Greenhouse-gas accumulation has opened a gap between the two, and the gap is where Earth-system thinking earns its keep: the surplus energy does not stay where it enters.

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment estimates that the global energy inventory grew by 282 zettajoules over 1971–2006 and a further 152 zettajoules over 2006–2018, corresponding to an energy imbalance that rose from about 0.50 to about 0.79 watts per square metre. The distribution of that energy is strikingly lopsided. Ocean heat uptake accounts for about 91 per cent of the total change, land heating about 5 per cent, melting ice about 3 per cent, and the atmosphere about 1 per cent. Different inventories and periods put the ocean share in the high eighties to low nineties, so the figure should be read as an assessed value rather than a constant.

This is the physical reason that [ocean heat content](/en/ecology/climate-change/ocean-heat-content-indicators) is a steadier indicator than surface temperature, and it is also why the surplus does not simply raise temperature: it is partitioned into warming water, melting ice, and — through thermal expansion and mass loss — raising [sea level](/en/ecology/climate-change/sea-level-rise-indicators). Energy also moves horizontally. Atmosphere and ocean together carry heat from the tropics towards the poles, and the division of labour between them, together with the [overturning circulation](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate) that does much of the ocean's share, sets the temperature contrast between equator and pole.

## Matter: reservoirs, fluxes, and turnover

The energy budget has a matter counterpart. Every element that cycles through the Earth system can be described by the size of its reservoirs, the fluxes between them, and how long an atom typically stays put.

Carbon shows the pattern most clearly. Over 2010–2019, human activity released an average of 10.9 ± 0.9 petagrams of carbon a year; the IPCC assesses that 46 per cent accumulated in the atmosphere, 23 per cent was taken up by the ocean, and 31 per cent by land vegetation. The airborne fraction — the share that stays in the air — has held near 44 per cent across six decades, which is a statement about the *sinks*, not about emissions, and whether it holds is one of the field's live questions. That question is taken up in the existing article on [carbon-cycle feedbacks](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-cycle-feedbacks).

Water and nitrogen tell different stories with the same grammar. Water's atmospheric reservoir is tiny and turns over in days, so its cycle is fast and its perturbations are felt almost immediately. Nitrogen's atmospheric reservoir is enormous and almost inert, so the interesting flux is the small stream of nitrogen converted into biologically reactive forms — a stream that industrial fixation has substantially enlarged. The consequence shows up in the atmosphere: the WMO's greenhouse-gas monitoring put the 2024 global mean for nitrous oxide at 338.0 ± 0.1 parts per billion, about 25 per cent above its pre-1750 level.

## Feedbacks: why the response is not the forcing

A forcing is a push. A feedback is the system's answer to being pushed, and it can be much larger than the push. The AR6 assessment of individual feedbacks, expressed as watts per square metre per degree of warming, gives a clear ranking: the base Planck response is −3.22, the combined water-vapour and lapse-rate feedback is +1.30, surface albedo is +0.35, and clouds are +0.42. Summed with the smaller biogeophysical terms, the net feedback parameter is −1.16, with a very likely range of −1.81 to −0.51.

Two things follow. First, the [water-vapour feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/water-vapor-feedback-explained) is the largest single amplifier, and the [ice-albedo feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ice-albedo-feedback-explained) is the most geographically concentrated one. Second, the width of the net range — a factor of more than three between its ends — is the dominant reason long-run warming estimates have a range at all. Clouds contribute most of that width, which is why cloud physics receives attention out of proportion to the size of its central estimate.

Feedbacks are not confined to physics. Vegetation changes reflectivity and moisture supply as well as carbon storage, which is the subject of [biosphere–climate interactions](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biosphere-climate-interactions); ice changes reflectivity, freshwater input, and ocean stratification at once, covered in [the cryosphere in the Earth system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/cryosphere-in-the-earth-system).

## Variability that is not a trend

Not everything that moves is being pushed. Coupled components generate their own oscillations, and the largest of them is the [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](/en/ecology/earth-systems/el-nino-la-nina-enso-explained), an ocean–atmosphere mode that reorganises tropical Pacific circulation every two to seven years and redistributes heat and rainfall worldwide. It has close relatives in the Atlantic, Indian, and Southern Ocean basins, surveyed in [modes of variability and teleconnections](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-variability-and-teleconnections).

Distinguishing internal variability from a forced signal is a core methodological skill in this field, and the failure mode is symmetric: a warm ENSO year read as a trend, or a genuine trend dismissed as a cycle. The existing article on [what climate change is](/en/ecology/climate-change/what-is-climate-change) addresses the attribution question directly.

## How the whole thing is watched and simulated

Two instruments make coupled study possible. The first is a sustained global observing system. The Global Climate Observing System defines 55 [Essential Climate Variables](/en/ecology/earth-systems/essential-climate-variables-explained) across the atmosphere, ocean, and land domains, chosen for relevance, technical feasibility, and cost-effectiveness; they are the agreed shortlist of what has to be measured continuously. Much of that measurement now comes from orbit, which is the subject of the [Earth observation cluster](/en/ecology/earth-observation/earth-observation-and-remote-sensing-explained), and the same infrastructure supports [climate indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-indicators-earth-system-monitoring) and [biodiversity monitoring](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-monitoring-and-ecosystem-health).

The second is the coupled model. An [Earth system model](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-models-explained) joins atmosphere, ocean, sea ice, and land components and adds interactive biogeochemistry, so that carbon is a variable the model computes rather than a number the modeller supplies. Models are compared under the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, coordinated by the World Climate Research Programme, whose sixth phase gathered output from 132 registered models across 48 institutions in 26 countries. Where their projections disagree, and why, is covered in [climate models and projection uncertainty](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-models-projections-uncertainty).

## Reading the evidence honestly

Three habits separate careful Earth-system reasoning from the confident version of it.

**Name the component, not just the number.** "The planet is warming" is ambiguous about which reservoir. Surface air temperature, ocean heat content, and ice mass are different measurements with different noise properties, and a claim that mixes them is weaker than it sounds.

**Match the timescale to the claim.** Deep-ocean and ice-sheet responses continue for centuries after a forcing stabilises. A statement about equilibrium and a statement about 2100 are different statements, and conflating them overstates near-term change while understating the long-term commitment.

**Keep the uncertainty attached to the number.** The AR6 feedback assessment is useful precisely because it is published as a central estimate with a range and a confidence level. Quantities in this field are also revised — the airborne fraction, the ocean heat share, the assessed feedbacks — as observing systems lengthen. A number without its provenance and its range is not much of a number.

The related question of which coupled changes might be abrupt or hard to reverse is handled separately in [Earth-system tipping points](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-tipping-points), where the honest answer is that confidence differs sharply between elements and the popular framing tends to flatten that difference.
