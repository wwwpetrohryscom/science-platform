A pressure anomaly over Iceland is statistically linked to winter temperatures in Turkey. Nothing physically travels between the two; the link exists because both are expressions of one large-scale circulation pattern. That kind of correlation-at-distance is a **teleconnection**, and the recurring spatial patterns that generate them are **modes of variability**. Together they account for a large share of what looks, in any short record, like climate change.

## What a mode of variability is

[Climate variability](/en/glossary/climate-variability) refers to fluctuations that arise from the internal dynamics of a coupled system rather than from an external push. A mode is a spatial pattern that recurs — the same geographical structure appearing repeatedly, with an amplitude that varies in time and can be summarised as an index.

The distinction from forced change is physical, not statistical convenience. A forced change reflects an alteration to the energy budget; a mode redistributes energy and moisture that are already in the system. Over a long enough record the two behave differently: internal modes oscillate around a stable mean, forced change does not.

## The main modes, and what each is an index of

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center monitors ten Northern Hemisphere teleconnection patterns operationally, deriving monthly indices through rotated principal component analysis of atmospheric height anomalies against a 1981–2010 climatology, with tabulated values reaching back to 1950. That methodological detail matters: most named modes are the leading statistical patterns of a field, not independently defined physical objects.

The ones that carry most explanatory weight are these.

**[ENSO](/en/glossary/enso)** is the largest, an ocean–atmosphere coupled mode in the tropical Pacific recurring irregularly every two to seven years. It is the only mode with substantial predictive skill months in advance, and it has its own article on [El Niño and La Niña](/en/ecology/earth-systems/el-nino-la-nina-enso-explained).

**The North Atlantic Oscillation** describes the strength of the pressure contrast between the Icelandic low and the Azores high, which steers the Atlantic storm track and therefore Northern European winter weather. Unlike ENSO it is primarily atmospheric, which makes it far less predictable beyond a couple of weeks.

**The Pacific/North American pattern** links tropical Pacific forcing to the position of the North American jet stream, and is one of the main routes by which ENSO's influence reaches mid-latitudes.

**The Southern and Northern Annular Modes** describe the strength and latitude of the westerly wind belts in each hemisphere, and are among the modes AR6 assesses for projected change.

Others — the Indian Ocean Dipole, the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, the Atlantic Multidecadal Variability — operate on longer timescales, and their longer periods create a specific problem discussed below.

## How teleconnections propagate

The physical mechanism is atmospheric wave propagation. Anomalous tropical convection heats the atmosphere locally, which generates large-scale waves that travel poleward and eastward, altering the position and strength of jet streams thousands of kilometres away. Because the waves are steered by the background flow, the same tropical anomaly produces different mid-latitude responses depending on the season and the state of the jet.

This has an important implication for interpreting impact statements. "El Niño causes drought in region X" is never unconditional; it is a statement about a shift in the probability distribution, conditional on season and on the background circulation. Individual events routinely depart from the composite pattern.

## The decadal-mode problem

Modes with periods of decades create a difficulty that shorter modes do not: the observational record contains only a handful of cycles.

With roughly 150 years of reliable global data, a mode with a 60-year period has been observed two or three times. That is not enough to establish that the oscillation is real, stationary, or internally generated rather than a response to changing forcing. Statements about multidecadal modes therefore carry substantially more uncertainty than statements about ENSO, and the reader should treat confident period estimates for them with caution.

The practical consequence is that attributing a multidecadal trend to "natural cycles" is usually a claim the data cannot support, in either direction.

## Separating internal variability from a forced signal

Three methods are used, and they are complementary rather than competing.

**Long records.** Internal variability averages toward zero over enough cycles; a forced trend does not. This is why century-scale records are qualitatively more informative than decadal ones, and why the existing article on [global temperature records](/en/ecology/climate-change/global-temperature-records-explained) emphasises record length.

**Large model ensembles.** Running the same model many times from slightly different initial conditions produces many possible realisations of internal variability under identical forcing, which gives a distribution against which an observation can be placed. The infrastructure for this is described in [Earth system models](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-models-explained).

**Fingerprinting.** Forced and internal patterns differ in spatial and vertical structure, so the two can be separated by pattern rather than by magnitude alone. This is the basis of formal attribution, covered in the existing article on [extreme-weather attribution](/en/ecology/climate-change/extreme-weather-attribution-basics).

## The two symmetrical errors

Because both mistakes are common, it is worth naming them together.

Reading variability as trend produces the "record warm year proves acceleration" error, where an ENSO-boosted year is treated as a step change. Reading trend as variability produces the "it's just a natural cycle" error, where a forced signal is dismissed by appeal to an oscillation whose existence the record cannot establish.

Both are failures of the same kind — comparing a short sample against an unstated null — and both are avoided by asking what the expected magnitude of internal variability is for the quantity and period in question, and whether the observation exceeds it. The indicator framework that supports that comparison is set out in the existing article on [climate indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-indicators-earth-system-monitoring).
