Descriptions of "the ocean conveyor belt" usually merge two physically distinct circulations that happen to be drawn on the same map. Separating them is the first step to understanding why some ocean-climate claims concern next season and others concern the next thousand years.

## The wind-driven circulation

Surface currents are driven by the wind, organised by the Earth's rotation into large basin-scale gyres. Their most consequential feature for climate is the western boundary currents — the Gulf Stream in the North Atlantic, the Kuroshio in the North Pacific — which are narrow, fast, and carry large quantities of warm water poleward.

This circulation responds on seasonal to interannual timescales and is where most air–sea coupling happens. Where wind pushes surface water away from a coast or away from the equator, deeper water rises to replace it — upwelling — bringing cool, nutrient-rich water to the surface. NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory notes that the cooler water off South America is nutrient-rich and supports high primary productivity, which is why the equatorial Pacific's circulation matters for fisheries as well as for temperature.

## The density-driven circulation

Beneath the wind-driven layer, the interior ocean circulates because of density differences, which are set by temperature and salinity together — hence [thermohaline circulation](/en/glossary/thermohaline-circulation). NOAA describes the process directly: in polar regions, water cools and sea ice forms, and because ice excludes salt as it freezes, the surrounding seawater becomes saltier, denser, and sinks.

That sinking water spreads through the deep basins and eventually returns to the surface through mixing and upwelling. NOAA estimates a full circuit takes on the order of a thousand years. This is the circulation that connects the modern surface to water that last saw the atmosphere in the Middle Ages, and it is the reason the deep ocean is a long-term reservoir for heat and carbon.

The equatorial asymmetry of this overturning is responsible for a large share of the ocean's poleward heat transport, which together with atmospheric transport sets the temperature contrast between tropics and poles.

## The AMOC specifically

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is the Atlantic component, and it receives disproportionate attention because it is unusually strong and because the North Atlantic is one of the few places where deep water forms. NOAA describes it as a system that brings warm water north and cold water south, with warm surface water cooling at high latitude, densifying, sinking, and returning at depth.

Two questions about it are frequently conflated.

**Is it weakening?** NOAA states that research shows a weakening over the past century, and notes some evidence of further slowing, while treating the future trajectory as uncertain. AR6's cross-chapter assessment projects a very likely decline over the twenty-first century.

**Will it collapse?** This is a different question with a different answer. AR6 assesses the AMOC as capable of abrupt change with *medium* confidence, as reversible within centuries with high confidence, and assigns *medium* confidence to there being no collapse before 2100. Medium confidence is a deliberately weaker statement than the very-likely language used for the decline, and the honest reading is that a collapse this century is not expected but is not confidently excluded either. AR6 marked this as a change from AR5, which had used stronger language ruling out abrupt transition.

Observational constraint is genuinely hard here. Continuous direct measurement of the overturning at a given latitude requires a moored array, the records are a couple of decades long, and the natural variability is large relative to any expected trend over that window. Longer-term inferences rely on proxies rather than direct measurement, which is why "AMOC is slowing" claims vary in strength depending on which evidence is being weighted.

## Why circulation is a climate variable, not a consequence

Three couplings make circulation an active participant rather than a passive result.

**Heat storage and release.** The ocean is where the planetary heat engine described in [Earth's thermodynamic energy budget](/en/physics/thermodynamics/earth-energy-budget-and-the-second-law) does much of its work, and it holds most of the energy the climate system takes up — AR6 assesses ocean heat uptake at about 91 per cent of the total change in the global energy inventory. Where circulation carries that heat determines where it re-emerges, on delays of years to centuries. The indicator view is in the existing article on [ocean heat content](/en/ecology/climate-change/ocean-heat-content-indicators).

**Carbon transport.** Deep water carries dissolved carbon away from contact with the atmosphere. A change in overturning changes the ocean sink, linking circulation directly to [the carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained).

**Coupled variability.** Where the ocean's surface temperature responds to wind and then alters the wind in turn, the coupling generates its own oscillations. The strongest example is [ENSO](/en/ecology/earth-systems/el-nino-la-nina-enso-explained), and the broader family is surveyed in [modes of variability and teleconnections](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-variability-and-teleconnections).

## Reading claims about the ocean carefully

The recurring error is a timescale mismatch: applying an interannual observation to a millennial circulation, or the reverse. A single warm year in the North Atlantic says nothing about the overturning; a change in the deep circulation would take decades to show up in surface indicators.

The second error is treating "the conveyor" as a single object that can switch off like a machine. The circulation is a continuum of processes with different sensitivities, and a weakening is not a partial version of a shutdown. AR6's separation of "very likely decline" from "medium confidence of no collapse" is precisely a refusal to collapse those two into one statement, and it is a good model for how to phrase the claim. The wider question of abrupt change is taken up in [Earth-system tipping points](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-tipping-points).
