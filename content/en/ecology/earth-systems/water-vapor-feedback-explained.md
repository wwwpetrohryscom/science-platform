Two claims about water vapour are both true and are frequently set against each other: it is the most important greenhouse gas in the atmosphere, and it plays no role as a driver of climate change. Reconciling them takes one observation about timescale, and doing so is the cleanest available illustration of what a [climate feedback](/en/glossary/climate-feedback) actually is.

## Why vapour is a feedback and not a forcing

Water vapour absorbs strongly across much of the infrared spectrum and contributes more to the natural greenhouse effect than any other constituent. But its atmospheric concentration is not set by emissions. It is set by temperature, through the physics of evaporation and condensation, and the adjustment is fast: as the article on [the global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained) sets out, the atmosphere replaces its entire water content roughly forty times a year.

Add water vapour to the atmosphere artificially and it rains out within days, returning the concentration to whatever the temperature supports. Add a long-lived gas such as CO₂ and it accumulates for centuries. That asymmetry in residence time is the whole distinction between a forcing and a feedback, and it is why vapour amplifies a change it cannot start.

## The magnitude

The governing relation is Clausius–Clapeyron, which sets the saturation vapour pressure of water as a function of temperature. The IPCC's assessment states that low-altitude specific humidity increases by about 7 per cent per degree Celsius of warming, assuming relative humidity remains approximately constant — an assumption that holds reasonably well at global scale, though not necessarily region by region.

More vapour, particularly in the upper troposphere, means more absorption of outgoing longwave radiation and therefore less energy escaping to space per degree of surface warming. That is an amplifying feedback, and AR6 assesses that it is very likely that near-surface specific humidity has increased since the 1970s and that total atmospheric water content will continue to rise at close to the thermodynamic rate.

## Why the lapse rate is bundled in

Published feedback tables almost never give a water-vapour number alone. They give a **combined water-vapour plus lapse-rate** term, and the reason is that the two are physically entangled rather than merely correlated.

The lapse rate is the rate at which temperature falls with altitude. Under warming, the tropical troposphere warms more aloft than at the surface, which raises the temperature of the layer that radiates to space and therefore *increases* outgoing radiation — a damping effect. But the same moist-convective processes that steepen the upper-tropospheric warming also moisten the upper troposphere, producing the amplifying vapour effect. The two partially cancel, and their errors are anti-correlated, so their sum is much better constrained than either term separately. Reporting them together is a statement about uncertainty structure, not a shortcut.

AR6 assesses the combined water-vapour plus lapse-rate feedback at +1.30 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, with a very likely range of 1.1 to 1.5 and high confidence. It is, in AR6's own words, the largest single contribution to global warming among the feedbacks, and it is also one of the better constrained — a range of ±0.2 around the central value, against a range from −0.10 to +0.94 for clouds.

## What is well settled and what is not

The physics of the feedback is about as firmly established as anything in the field: it rests on a laboratory-measured thermodynamic relation, is confirmed by satellite and radiosonde humidity records, and appears with consistent magnitude across independent model families. The narrow assessed range reflects that.

Three genuine open questions remain. **Relative humidity is not exactly constant**, and small systematic departures — particularly in the dry subtropical free troposphere, where the radiative sensitivity to moisture is highest — would shift the feedback slightly. **Upper-tropospheric humidity observations are difficult**, since the layer is very dry and satellite retrievals there carry larger uncertainty than near the surface. And the **separation between the vapour and lapse-rate components is method-dependent**: AR6 notes two different decompositions in use, one based on specific humidity and one on relative humidity, which give the same sum but different individual terms. A quoted "water-vapour feedback" figure therefore depends on which decomposition produced it.

None of these unsettle the sign or the approximate magnitude. They are reasons to prefer the combined term with its published range over a standalone number.

## How this sits in the larger picture

Set against the Planck response of −3.22 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, a +1.30 amplification is substantial but not runaway — the stabilising term remains far larger, which is why the [net feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-feedback-mechanisms) stays negative at −1.16. Water vapour makes the planet considerably more sensitive without making it unstable.

Its geographical profile also differs from the other major amplifier. Vapour feedback operates strongly in the tropics where the atmosphere is warm and moist; the [ice-albedo feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ice-albedo-feedback-explained) operates at high latitudes where there is ice to lose. That contrast is part of why warming is not spatially uniform, and it is picked up in the existing article on [what climate change is](/en/ecology/climate-change/what-is-climate-change).
