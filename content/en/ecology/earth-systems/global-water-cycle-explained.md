Of all the reservoirs in the [Earth system](/en/glossary/earth-system), the atmosphere's water store is the smallest that anyone cares about and the one that turns over fastest. Both facts follow from the same number, and together they explain why the [water cycle](/en/glossary/water-cycle) intensifies under warming in a way that is neither uniform nor proportional.

## Where the water is

USGS figures put the total volume of water on Earth at about 1,386 million cubic kilometres, of which roughly 96.5 per cent sits in the oceans as saline water. Of the small freshwater remainder, over 68 per cent is locked in ice and glaciers and another 30 per cent is groundwater — leaving surface fresh water, the part most visible to people, as a minor fraction of a minor fraction.

The atmosphere holds about 12,900 cubic kilometres, which USGS puts at approximately 0.001 per cent of the global total. If it all precipitated at once it would cover the planet to a depth of about 2.5 centimetres.

## The flux, and what it implies

NASA estimates that around 495,000 cubic kilometres of water cycles through the atmosphere annually — replacing the atmospheric store close to forty times a year, which is a residence time on the order of nine days. NASA puts the share of atmospheric moisture supplied by evaporation from oceans, seas, and other water bodies at nearly 90 per cent, with plant transpiration and human activity accounting for the remainder.

That short residence time has three consequences worth stating plainly.

**Water vapour cannot accumulate.** Add moisture to the air and it rains out within a fortnight, so atmospheric water vapour cannot behave as a long-lived forcing agent. Its concentration is set by temperature, which is why it acts as a feedback instead — the subject of [the water-vapour feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/water-vapor-feedback-explained).

**The cycle responds immediately.** Unlike carbon, which accumulates over centuries, changes to evaporation and precipitation are expressed within weeks. There is no lag to wait out.

**Transport matters more than storage.** Because the reservoir is small relative to the flux, where water falls depends on where the air carrying it goes — meaning atmospheric circulation, not moisture supply, sets most regional patterns.

## Why moisture rises faster than rainfall

Here is the part of the water cycle most often stated incorrectly. Warming increases the atmosphere's capacity to hold moisture and increases precipitation, but not by the same amount.

The IPCC's assessment sets out both constraints. The Clausius–Clapeyron relation implies that low-altitude specific humidity rises by about 7 per cent per degree Celsius of warming, assuming relative humidity stays roughly constant — which holds approximately at the global scale though not regionally. But global mean precipitation and evaporation are constrained by the atmosphere's energy budget rather than by its moisture capacity, and AR6 gives a very likely range of only 1 to 3 per cent per degree for their increase.

The gap between the two rates is not an inconsistency. Precipitation is limited by how fast the atmosphere can shed the latent heat released when water condenses, which is an energetic constraint; moisture content is limited by thermodynamics. The practical consequence is that a warmer atmosphere holds substantially more water but rains somewhat less often and more heavily when it does. AR6 attaches high confidence to the finding that a roughly 7 per cent per degree increase in near-surface moisture-holding capacity explains a similar magnitude of intensification in heavy precipitation events.

## Intensification is a redistribution, not an increase everywhere

"The water cycle is intensifying" is a compact phrase covering several distinct changes, and AR6 is careful to note that there is no single agreed definition of global water-cycle intensity. What the assessment does support with high confidence is an overall strengthening observed since at least 1980, and an increase in the severity of both very wet and very dry events.

Two mechanisms pull in different directions regionally. Thermodynamics makes wet regions and wet seasons wetter. Circulation changes move where the wet and dry zones sit, and those shifts have larger regional uncertainty than the thermodynamic term. Where the two reinforce, the change is large and confidently projected; where they oppose, regional projections diverge between models.

The cryosphere adds a third element: snowpack and glaciers act as seasonal and multi-year water storage, so warming changes not only how much precipitation falls but when it is released downstream. That coupling is developed in [the cryosphere in the Earth system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/cryosphere-in-the-earth-system).

## Measuring a cycle that mostly happens over the ocean

Most evaporation occurs over oceans, where there are few instruments, and most of the water cycle's fluxes are therefore inferred rather than measured. Precipitation over land is measured by gauges and radar with reasonable density in some regions and almost none in others; over the ocean it comes largely from satellite retrievals with their own biases.

Global closure of the water budget — making evaporation, precipitation, runoff, and storage change balance — remains an active problem, and residual imbalances in published budgets are usually larger than the trends being sought. AR6 notes explicitly that although an increase in global precipitation is consistent with physical expectations, it has not yet been detected and attributed to human activity, given observational uncertainty and a weak signal-to-noise ratio. That is a useful calibration: a physically expected change is not automatically an observed one.

The satellite side of this problem — what can and cannot be retrieved about soil moisture, precipitation, and water storage — is covered in [drought monitoring systems](/en/ecology/earth-observation/drought-monitoring-systems) and in the broader treatment of [remote-sensing uncertainty](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty).
