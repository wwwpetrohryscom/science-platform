About 78 per cent of the air is nitrogen, and almost none of it is available to almost anything alive. The triple bond in N₂ is one of the strongest in ordinary chemistry, and until it is broken the atom is inert. That single chemical fact organises the whole [nitrogen cycle](/en/glossary/nitrogen-cycle) — and explains why an element in vast surplus behaves as a limiting nutrient across most of the [biosphere](/en/glossary/biosphere). It is a clear case of the reservoir-and-flux logic set out in [biogeochemical cycles](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biogeochemical-cycles-explained): a huge store paired with a small, biologically gated flux.

## Fixation: the bottleneck step

Converting N₂ into a biologically usable form is called fixation, and until the twentieth century it happened by two routes. Lightning does a small amount through high-temperature chemistry. Far more is done biologically, by prokaryotes carrying the nitrogenase enzyme — free-living soil and marine bacteria, cyanobacteria, and the symbionts housed in the root nodules of legumes.

Once fixed, nitrogen enters a set of transformations that microbial communities run, each with a characteristic [residence time](/en/glossary/residence-time) in its own reservoir: **nitrification**, in which ammonium is oxidised through nitrite to nitrate; **assimilation**, in which plants and microbes incorporate it into amino acids and nucleic acids; **mineralisation**, returning organic nitrogen to ammonium as tissue decomposes; and **denitrification**, in which anaerobic microbes reduce nitrate back toward N₂ and close the loop.

The point of listing them is that every step is biologically mediated. Unlike the [water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained), which runs on physics, the nitrogen cycle is a cycle that living things operate — which makes it responsive to land management in a way physical cycles are not. Nutrient limitation is also what ties this cycle to the land carbon sink discussed in [the carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained) and in [biosphere–climate interactions](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biosphere-climate-interactions).

## The industrial addition

The Haber–Bosch process made industrial fixation possible at scale, and synthetic fertiliser is now a major input to the terrestrial cycle alongside manure, legume cultivation, and the reactive nitrogen released by fossil-fuel combustion. FAO maintains the international statistics on agricultural nitrogen use by nutrient, which is where the quantities are tracked.

The direction of the change is not disputed: the flow of reactive nitrogen through terrestrial systems is substantially larger than it was pre-industrially. The exact multiple depends on what is counted — whether marine biological fixation is included, whether combustion sources are counted as fixation, and which inventory year is used — so it is worth being wary of a single confident global ratio quoted without those qualifications.

## Where the nitrogen goes when it leaks

Reactive nitrogen applied to a field does not stay there. It moves through what is often called the nitrogen cascade: one atom can successively contribute to air pollution, water eutrophication, and greenhouse forcing before finally being denitrified.

**To water.** Nitrate is highly soluble and leaches readily, driving eutrophication in fresh water and coastal seas. The European Environment Agency tracks atmospheric nitrogen deposition against ecological critical loads, and in 2023 found the largest exceedances in the Po Valley, along the Netherlands–Germany and Denmark–Germany borders, and in north-eastern Spain.

**To air as ammonia and nitrogen oxides.** These contribute to fine particulate formation and to acidification and eutrophication downwind of where they were emitted.

**To air as nitrous oxide.** This is the climate-relevant leak. N₂O is produced as a by-product of nitrification and denitrification, so it scales with how much reactive nitrogen is circulating rather than with any deliberate emission.

## Nitrous oxide as a climate term

The WMO's greenhouse-gas monitoring put the 2024 global mean N₂O concentration at 338.0 ± 0.1 parts per billion, roughly 25 per cent above the pre-1750 level. The IPCC assesses the growth rate at 0.95 ± 0.04 parts per billion per year over 2010–2019, up from 0.85 ± 0.03 over 1995–2019, and attributes the increase to human activity, with fertiliser and manure use contributing about two-thirds of the rise between 1980 and 2016.

Two features make N₂O awkward to manage. Its atmospheric residence time is assessed at 109 ± 10 years, so today's emissions constrain composition well into the next century — the IPCC notes explicitly that it will take more than a century for abundances to stabilise even after emissions do. And the relationship between nitrogen input and N₂O emission is non-linear: beyond the amount crops can take up, the emitted fraction rises disproportionately, which means the same total application produces very different emissions depending on how it is timed and placed.

The radiative side of this is covered in the existing article on [greenhouse gases and radiative forcing](/en/ecology/climate-change/greenhouse-gases-and-radiative-forcing); AR6 assesses the effective radiative forcing from nitrous oxide over 1750–2019 at 0.21 W m⁻², with a range of 0.18 to 0.24.

## What is genuinely uncertain

Nitrogen budgets are less well constrained than carbon budgets, for structural reasons rather than neglect.

Fluxes are spatially heterogeneous to an extreme degree — emissions from a fertilised field vary by an order of magnitude within a season depending on moisture and temperature — so scaling point measurements to regions is hazardous. Bottom-up inventories and atmospheric inversions do not always agree, and AR6 reports a wide range of 4.2 to 11.4 teragrams of nitrogen a year for anthropogenic N₂O emissions over 2007–2016, which is a factor of nearly three between the ends.

Marine nitrogen fixation and denitrification are the least observed terms of all, and whether the marine cycle is currently in balance is not settled.

The consequence for reading claims about nitrogen is the same as for carbon: prefer the assessed range over the point estimate, and check whether a figure refers to fertiliser applied, reactive nitrogen created, or nitrogen actually lost, which are three different quantities routinely conflated. The soil-biology dimension of the same problem is examined in the existing article on the [soil microbiome in regenerative agriculture](/en/ecology/ecosystems/soil-microbiome-regenerative-agriculture).
