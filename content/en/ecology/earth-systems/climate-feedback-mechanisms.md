The word "feedback" does a lot of loose work in public writing about climate, usually meaning something like "things get worse". In the technical literature it means something precise and quantitative, and the precision is what makes the concept useful. Feedbacks are also the clearest case of a property that belongs to the coupling rather than to any one component, which is why [Earth system science treats the planet as a single system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-science-explained). This article sets out the definition, the sign convention, and the assessed values — and then the part that matters most, which is where the uncertainty sits.

## Definition and sign convention

A **forcing** is an imposed change to the planet's energy budget, measured in watts per square metre. A [climate feedback](/en/glossary/climate-feedback) is a process that changes the energy budget *in response to* the temperature change that the forcing produced. It is expressed as a feedback parameter in watts per square metre per degree Celsius of global surface warming.

The sign convention trips people up, because it is the opposite of intuition. A feedback parameter is written so that **negative values are stabilising** and positive values are amplifying. The reason is that the total parameter measures how strongly the planet increases its radiation to space per degree of warming; a strongly negative total means the planet sheds the extra energy easily and warms little.

The baseline term is the **Planck response**: a warmer body radiates more, following the Stefan–Boltzmann relation. AR6 assesses it at −3.22 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, with a very likely range of −3.4 to −3.0. Every other feedback is a modification of this baseline. The radiative physics it rests on — why surfaces emit where they do, and why the atmosphere is selectively transparent — is set out in the applied-physics articles on [Earth's energy balance](/en/physics/energy/solar-radiation-and-earth-energy-balance) and the [electromagnetic spectrum](/en/physics/quantum-basics/electromagnetic-spectrum-applications).

## The assessed values

AR6 Working Group I assesses the individual feedback parameters as follows, all in watts per square metre per degree Celsius.

| Feedback | Central estimate | Very likely range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planck response | −3.22 | −3.4 to −3.0 |
| Water vapour plus lapse rate | +1.30 | +1.1 to +1.5 |
| Surface albedo | +0.35 | +0.10 to +0.60 |
| Clouds | +0.42 | −0.10 to +0.94 |
| Biogeophysical and non-CO₂ biogeochemical | −0.01 | −0.27 to +0.25 |
| **Net** | **−1.16** | **−1.81 to −0.51** |

Three readings follow directly from the table.

The **combined water-vapour and lapse-rate term is the largest single amplifier**, and AR6 states so explicitly. It is treated on its own in [the water-vapour feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/water-vapor-feedback-explained), where the reason for combining the two terms is explained.

The **surface albedo feedback is the smallest of the three physical amplifiers** in global mean terms, at +0.35 with medium confidence — which is worth noting because it is the one most often assumed to dominate. Its importance is regional rather than global, as [the ice-albedo feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ice-albedo-feedback-explained) sets out.

The **cloud feedback contributes most of the uncertainty**. Its central estimate is +0.42, comparable to albedo, but its very likely range spans from slightly negative to nearly a full watt. AR6 assesses with high confidence that the net cloud feedback is positive and that a net negative cloud feedback is very unlikely — a change from earlier assessments, and one achieved by combining models, satellite observations, and explicit cloud-resolving simulation.

## Why the net number matters more than any single one

The net feedback parameter is what converts a forcing into an equilibrium temperature response, so its width sets the width of the climate sensitivity range. At −1.16 the net is a factor of about three narrower in magnitude than the Planck response alone, meaning feedbacks roughly triple the warming that bare radiative physics would give.

The very likely range of −1.81 to −0.51 is highly asymmetric in its consequences. Because sensitivity depends on the reciprocal of the parameter, the weakly negative end of the range implies far more warming than the strongly negative end implies less. This is why AR6's assessed equilibrium climate sensitivity — a best estimate of 3 °C, likely range 2.5 to 4 °C, very likely 2 to 5 °C — has a long upper tail rather than a symmetric error bar. The projection-side consequences are worked through in the existing article on [climate models and projection uncertainty](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-models-projections-uncertainty).

## Feedbacks are not constant

Two properties of the feedback parameter are easy to miss and both matter for long-horizon claims.

**State dependence.** AR6 notes that the net feedback parameter will become less negative as global temperature rises, based on evidence from both paleoclimate records and models. Feedback strength is a function of the climate state, not a fixed property of the planet.

**Pattern effect.** Radiative feedbacks, particularly cloud feedbacks, depend on *where* the surface warms, not only on how much. Because the spatial pattern of warming evolves over decades, feedbacks inferred from the historical record are not the same as the feedbacks that will operate at equilibrium. AR6 identifies this as the reason warming over the instrumental record constrains the lower end of the sensitivity range robustly but does not, on its own, constrain the upper end.

## Beyond the physical feedbacks

The table above covers the fast physical response. Slower processes act as feedbacks too, on timescales the standard parameter does not capture.

Carbon-cycle feedbacks operate through the sinks: if warming reduces land or ocean uptake, more of each tonne emitted stays airborne, which is a positive feedback on concentration rather than on radiation directly. That mechanism has its own article on [carbon-cycle feedbacks](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-cycle-feedbacks). Vegetation feedbacks work through albedo, evapotranspiration, and carbon at once, as covered in [biosphere–climate interactions](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biosphere-climate-interactions). And on millennial timescales AR6 assesses the ice-sheet feedback parameter as very likely positive, promoting additional warming as ice sheets equilibrate.

## How to read a feedback claim

Three checks separate a well-formed feedback statement from a loose one.

Ask whether a **number and a range** are attached. A feedback without a magnitude is a description of a mechanism, not a quantified claim, and mechanisms that sound alarming can be small.

Ask which **timescale** applies. Fast physical feedbacks operate within years; carbon-cycle feedbacks over decades to centuries; ice-sheet feedbacks over millennia. Summing them into one statement overstates near-term response.

Ask whether the claim distinguishes **feedback from forcing**. Water vapour is a powerful greenhouse gas but not a driver, because its concentration is set by temperature. Treating it as a forcing is a common and consequential error.
