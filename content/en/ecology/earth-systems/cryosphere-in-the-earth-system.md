The existing article on [cryosphere indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/cryosphere-indicators-glaciers-sea-ice) treats ice as something measured — extent, mass balance, snow cover, each with its own record and its own error structure. This article takes the opposite view and asks what ice *does* to the rest of the system. The answer runs through four separate channels, and they operate on different timescales and in different directions.

## What counts as the cryosphere

NSIDC groups the frozen part of the planet into six categories: snow on the ground, lake and river ice, frozen ground and permafrost, the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets along with ice caps and glaciers, ice shelves and icebergs, and sea ice.

Grouping them under one heading is convenient but conceals a spread of response times running from days for snow cover to millennia for ice sheets. Almost every error in reasoning about the [cryosphere](/en/glossary/cryosphere) comes from applying one member's timescale to another.

## Channel one: reflectivity

The most familiar channel. Snow reflects up to about 90 per cent of incident sunlight and bare sea ice between 40 and 60 per cent, according to NSIDC, against a much darker ocean or land surface underneath. Losing that cover raises absorbed energy locally.

The global magnitude is more modest than the local contrast implies — AR6 assesses the surface-albedo feedback at +0.35 W m⁻² °C⁻¹, very likely range 0.10 to 0.60 — for reasons of area, sunlight angle, and cloud cover set out in [the ice-albedo feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ice-albedo-feedback-explained).

## Channel two: sea level

Land ice and sea ice do opposite things here, and the difference is often muddled. Sea ice already floats and displaces its own mass, so its melting has a negligible direct effect on sea level. Land ice does not, and its loss adds mass to the ocean.

The stored volumes are large. NSIDC states that complete melting of the Greenland ice sheet would raise sea level by about 7.4 metres and the Antarctic ice sheet by about 60 metres — figures worth reading as measures of how much water is stored, not as projections, since they describe complete loss over millennia rather than any plausible century-scale change. NSIDC also notes that glaciers, ice caps, icefields, and the two ice sheets together hold more than 70 per cent of Earth's freshwater ice.

The century-scale contribution is a different and much smaller number, treated in the existing article on [sea-level rise indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/sea-level-rise-indicators).

## Channel three: ocean density and circulation

This is the channel most often left out, and the most consequential for coupling.

When sea ice forms, it excludes salt. NOAA describes the process directly: the surrounding water becomes saltier and denser and begins to sink, which is one of the mechanisms that drives the deep [thermohaline circulation](/en/glossary/thermohaline-circulation). When ice melts instead, it delivers fresh water to the surface, which lowers density and stabilises the water column against sinking.

Ice formation and ice melt therefore push the overturning circulation in opposite directions, and both happen in the same regions. The relevance to AMOC projections is direct, and is developed in [ocean circulation and climate](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate).

Sea ice also insulates. A closed ice cover suppresses heat and moisture exchange between a relatively warm ocean and a much colder atmosphere; open water in winter releases large quantities of heat upward. Loss of winter ice cover is therefore an atmospheric heating term independent of the albedo effect, which operates only when the sun is up.

## Channel four: the carbon cycle

Permafrost stores organic carbon that has been frozen, in places, for tens of thousands of years. Thaw exposes it to microbial decomposition, releasing carbon dioxide under aerobic conditions and methane under waterlogged anaerobic ones.

AR6's cross-chapter assessment treats permafrost carbon carefully, and the wording repays attention: it assesses abrupt change as possible with **high** confidence and irreversibility over centuries with high confidence, and it considers decline in frozen carbon **virtually certain** under continued warming — but assigns **low** confidence to the net carbon change. That combination is unusual and honest. The direction of thaw is not in doubt; the net carbon consequence is, because thawing landscapes also support more plant growth, and the balance between release and uptake is not resolved.

## Which cryosphere changes are abrupt or irreversible

AR6's Table 4.10 assigns different answers to different components, and flattening them into a single statement about "the ice" loses the substance.

Arctic **summer** sea ice: no abrupt change, high confidence; reversible within years to decades, high confidence; likely complete loss under continued warming — where AR6 defines practically ice-free as a sea-ice area below one million square kilometres.

Arctic **winter** sea ice: abrupt change possible, high confidence; reversible within years to decades, high confidence; moderate declines projected.

**Greenland ice sheet**: no abrupt change, high confidence; irreversible for millennia, high confidence; virtually certain mass loss under all scenarios.

**West Antarctic ice sheet and shelves**: abrupt change possible, high confidence; irreversible for decades to millennia, high confidence; likely mass loss, with deep uncertainty in projections above 3 °C of warming.

The pattern is that reversibility and abruptness are independent properties. Greenland is effectively irreversible but not abrupt; Arctic summer sea ice is neither. The general framework is in [Earth-system tipping points](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-tipping-points).

## How the cryosphere is watched

Most of this is measured from orbit, because the regions are remote and the quantities are areal. Passive microwave sensors give sea-ice extent through cloud and polar night; satellite gravimetry gives ice-sheet mass change; radar and laser altimetry give surface elevation. Ground networks supply glacier mass balance and permafrost borehole temperatures, and the two kinds of measurement are used together rather than interchangeably.

The relevant caveats — sensor changes across missions, the difference between extent and area, and the short length of satellite records relative to the variability — are covered in [remote-sensing limitations and uncertainty](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty), and NOAA's annual Arctic Report Card assembles the regional picture each year.
