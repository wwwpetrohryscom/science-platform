Every [biogeochemical cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biogeochemical-cycles-explained) that [Earth system science](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-science-explained) tracks is specified by reservoirs, fluxes and residence times. The phosphorus cycle is unusual for what it lacks: there is no significant atmospheric reservoir. Phosphorus has no stable gas phase under Earth-surface conditions, and the entire structure of the cycle is a consequence.

## What follows from the missing reservoir

**Transport is by water and by rock.** Nitrogen moves globally through the air in hours to days; phosphorus moves at the speed of rivers and continental erosion. There is no equivalent of atmospheric deposition to resupply a depleted soil.

**There is no biological fixation route.** Organisms can pull nitrogen out of the air at a cost of [sixteen ATP per molecule](/en/biology/microbiology/nitrogen-fixation-and-its-cost); nothing can do the equivalent for phosphorus, because the source is not there. A phosphorus-limited system cannot spend energy to escape the limitation, only to acquire what already exists nearby.

**The cycle is one-way on human timescales.** Phosphorus weathered from rock moves through soils and rivers to the ocean and is buried in marine sediment. Returning it to land requires tectonic uplift. The loop closes on geological time, not on ecological time.

## The reservoirs that matter

The USGS's water-science account gives the practical form of the terrestrial cycle: "Phosphorus is a common constituent of agricultural fertilizers, manure, and organic wastes in sewage and industrial effluent", and it moves largely with soil rather than in solution — "Soil erosion is a major contributor of phosphorus to streams. Bank erosion occurring during floods can transport a lot of phosphorous from the river banks and adjacent land into a stream, lake, or other water body."

That phosphorus travels attached to particles is the operational difference from nitrogen, which travels dissolved. It means phosphorus management is largely erosion management, and that a measure which reduces sediment loss reduces phosphorus loss with it.

The primary industrial input is mined. The USGS notes that "The United States is the world's leading producer and consumer of phosphate rock, which is used to manufacture phosphate fertilizers and industrial products." Unlike nitrogen fertiliser, which is synthesised from an inexhaustible atmospheric feedstock, phosphate fertiliser is extracted from a finite deposit.

## The ecological consequence of excess

Because it is often the limiting nutrient in fresh water, added phosphorus produces a large response. The USGS states the mechanism: phosphorus "is an essential element for plant life, but when there is too much of it in water, it can speed up eutrophication", and "excessive nutrient enrichment triggers algal overgrowth, leading to decreased dissolved oxygen as the algae decay." The endpoint is familiar: "The decay process consumes oxygen, so the decay of a large bloom can leave 'dead zones' — low oxygen areas where fish can't survive."

The EPA's framing adds the source attribution, naming "agriculture and stormwater" as key contributors, and notes that the resulting blooms can release toxins that "can make people and animals sick if they touch, drink, or eat contaminated fish or shellfish."

## Why recovery is slow

The property that makes phosphorus a good fertiliser — it binds to soil particles and stays put — is what makes cleanup slow. Phosphorus accumulated in agricultural soils over decades continues to be released long after inputs are reduced, and phosphorus stored in lake sediments is returned to the water when bottom water goes anoxic.

The result is a lag between management and improvement that has no counterpart in the nitrogen case, where the pool is more mobile and flushes faster. A catchment that stops applying phosphorus today is still exporting legacy phosphorus years later, and that legacy is not a modelling artefact but a measurable inventory in the soil.

## The stoichiometric connection

Phosphorus scarcity constrains what can be done with the other elements, because organisms need them in proportions. Marine particulate matter has a median N:P near 22 with wide regional variation, against Redfield's canonical 16 — a relationship examined in [nutrient stoichiometry](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nutrient-stoichiometry-and-the-redfield-ratio). Where phosphorus is short, additional nitrogen produces less growth than the nitrogen alone would suggest, which is why [nutrient management](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-losses-and-use-efficiency) that addresses one element and not the other frequently disappoints.
