Ask why an atom of carbon dioxide matters for centuries while a water molecule in the same parcel of air matters for about nine days, and the answer is not chemistry. It is a ratio. Divide the size of a reservoir by the rate at which material leaves it and you get a [residence time](/en/glossary/residence-time) — the single number that does most of the explanatory work in this part of [Earth system science](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-science-explained).

## The three quantities

A [biogeochemical cycle](/en/glossary/biogeochemical-cycle) describes the movement of an element between reservoirs by biological, geological, and chemical processes. Three quantities define it.

A **reservoir** is a place where the element accumulates: the atmosphere, the surface ocean, soil organic matter, living biomass, sedimentary rock. Reservoirs are defined by the question — "soil carbon" may be one reservoir or five depending on the timescale being studied.

A **flux** is a transfer rate between two reservoirs, in mass per unit time. Photosynthesis, respiration, evaporation, and burial are fluxes.

**Residence time** is the reservoir size divided by the total outgoing flux. It answers "how long does a typical atom stay here?", and it is the property that determines whether a perturbation is felt immediately or accumulates.

## Reading the arithmetic

The [water cycle](/en/glossary/water-cycle) makes the calculation vivid. NASA puts the atmosphere's water content at about 12,900 cubic kilometres at any moment, and the amount cycled through it at roughly 495,000 cubic kilometres a year — a replacement about forty times over per year, or a residence time of around nine days. That short residence is why atmospheric moisture cannot accumulate: add water vapour and it rains out within a fortnight. The full picture is in [the global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained).

Carbon in the atmosphere behaves differently, and the reason is not that its gross fluxes are small — photosynthesis and respiration exchange enormous quantities each year. It is that those fluxes are nearly balanced, so the *net* removal of an added pulse is slow. Over 2010–2019 the IPCC assesses that 46 per cent of human CO₂ emissions stayed in the atmosphere, 23 per cent entered the ocean, and 31 per cent went into land vegetation. The share remaining airborne has held near 44 per cent for six decades. The consequence is accumulation rather than throughput, developed in [the carbon cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained).

Nitrogen inverts the picture again. Its atmospheric reservoir is vast — roughly 78 per cent of the air by volume — but almost all of it is unreactive N₂ that most organisms cannot use. The cycle's dynamics live in the small reactive fraction, so a modest change in fixation rates produces a large proportional change in what circulates, as [the nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained) explains.

## Fast and slow cycles are the same cycle

Most elements run two loops at once, and confusing them is a common source of error. NASA's Earth Observatory describes carbon's slow cycle — the exchange with rocks through weathering, subduction, and volcanism — as taking 100 to 200 million years per circuit, moving something like 10 to 100 million tonnes of carbon a year. The fast cycle, running through living organisms, ocean surface, and atmosphere, moves between 1,000 and 100,000 million tonnes a year.

The slow cycle is what stabilises atmospheric composition over geological time. It is also far too slow to respond to a perturbation delivered over two centuries, which is the essential asymmetry behind the current situation: fossil carbon that took the slow cycle tens of millions of years to bury is being returned to the fast cycle in a few hundred.

## What couples the cycles to each other

Cycles are not independent. Three couplings recur.

**Stoichiometry.** Organisms build tissue in roughly fixed elemental ratios, so carbon uptake by vegetation is limited by the availability of nitrogen and phosphorus. A model that lets plants absorb carbon without tracking nutrients will overestimate the land sink — which is why interactive nutrient cycles were among the significant additions in modern [Earth system models](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-models-explained).

**Shared media.** The ocean carries carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen in the same water, so a change in circulation moves all of them together. The IPCC notes that ocean acidification and deoxygenation are both consequences of the same uptake and warming, expressed in different chemistry.

**Radiative side effects.** Several cycles emit greenhouse gases as by-products. Nitrous oxide is a nitrogen-cycle product with an atmospheric residence of about 109 ± 10 years, according to the AR6 assessment — long enough that today's agricultural emissions constrain composition well into the next century.

## Where the numbers are soft

Flux estimates are much less certain than reservoir estimates, and the imbalance matters when interpreting budgets.

Gross fluxes are typically inferred rather than measured directly — photosynthesis and respiration at global scale are estimated from a combination of flux towers, satellite proxies, and models, and each route carries its own bias. Because the two are large and nearly equal, small proportional errors in either translate into large errors in the net.

Reservoir boundaries also move with the method. "Soil carbon" measured to one metre and to three metres are different numbers, and studies that report only one depth are not comparable with those that report the other. Residence times inherit both problems, since they are a ratio of two uncertain quantities.

Finally, a residence time is an average over a distribution, not a lifetime. The often-quoted idea that added CO₂ has a single lifetime is a simplification: removal happens through several processes with very different timescales, so the decay of a pulse is not exponential. The related discussion of whether the sinks will keep behaving as they have is in the existing article on [carbon-cycle feedbacks](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-cycle-feedbacks), and the wider framing of natural cycling within an ecosystem is in [what an ecosystem is](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem).
