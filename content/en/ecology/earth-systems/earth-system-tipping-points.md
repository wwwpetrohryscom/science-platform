Few concepts in Earth system science have travelled further from their technical meaning than the tipping point. In public use it has become a single dramatic threshold; in the assessment literature it is a defined term applied separately to a dozen or so components, most of which score low or medium confidence. This article sets out the definitions and then reproduces what the assessment actually says about each element, because the element-by-element picture is the whole substance.

## Three distinct properties

The literature keeps three things apart that popular usage merges, and holding them apart is most of the work.

**Abruptness.** AR6 defines an abrupt climate change as a large-scale change taking place over a few decades or less, persisting or expected to persist for at least a few decades, and causing substantial impacts. Speed is the criterion.

**Irreversibility.** A perturbed state is considered irreversible on a given timescale if recovery through natural processes would take substantially longer than the timescale of interest. Note that this is defined relative to a stated horizon — "irreversible" is never an absolute.

**A [tipping point](/en/glossary/tipping-element)** is a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganises, often abruptly or irreversibly; a **tipping element** is a component susceptible to one. AR6 adopts both definitions explicitly.

The three are independent. A change can be irreversible without being abrupt — an ice sheet losing mass steadily over centuries — or abrupt without being irreversible. Conflating them is the single most common error in this area.

## What the assessment says

AR6's Table 4.10 is a cross-chapter assessment of components proposed as susceptible to tipping, with a confidence level for each property. Reproducing its judgements plainly is more useful than summarising them.

| Element | Abrupt change possible? | Reversibility if forcing reversed | Projected 21st-century change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Global monsoon | Yes under AMOC collapse, medium confidence | Reversible within years to decades, medium confidence | Medium confidence in global increase |
| Tropical forest | Yes, low confidence | Irreversible for multi-decades, medium confidence | Medium confidence of increasing carbon storage, depending on disturbance |
| Boreal forest | Yes, low confidence | Irreversible for multi-decades, medium confidence | Medium confidence in dieback offset by poleward extension |
| Permafrost carbon | Yes, high confidence | Irreversible for centuries, high confidence | Virtually certain decline in frozen carbon; low confidence in net carbon change |
| Arctic summer sea ice | No, high confidence | Reversible within years to decades, high confidence | Likely complete loss |
| Arctic winter sea ice | Yes, high confidence | Reversible within years to decades, high confidence | High confidence in moderate declines |
| Antarctic sea ice | Yes, low confidence | Unknown, low confidence | Low confidence in moderate declines |
| Greenland ice sheet | No, high confidence | Irreversible for millennia, high confidence | Virtually certain mass loss under all scenarios |
| West Antarctic ice sheet and shelves | Yes, high confidence | Irreversible for decades to millennia, high confidence | Likely mass loss; deep uncertainty above 3 °C |
| Global ocean heat content | No, high confidence | Irreversible for centuries, very high confidence | Very high confidence of continued warming |
| Global sea-level rise | Yes, high confidence | Irreversible for centuries, very high confidence | Very high confidence in continued rise; deep uncertainty above 3 °C |
| AMOC | Yes, medium confidence | Reversible within centuries, high confidence | Very likely decline; medium confidence of no collapse |
| Southern overturning circulation | Yes, medium confidence | Reversible within decades to centuries, low confidence | Medium confidence in weakening |
| Ocean acidification | Yes, high confidence | Reversible at surface, irreversible for centuries to millennia at depth | Virtually certain to continue with rising CO₂ |
| Ocean deoxygenation | Yes, high confidence | Reversible at surface, irreversible at depth, medium confidence | Medium confidence in rates and increased hypoxia |

## What the table shows that summaries do not

Four patterns emerge from reading down the columns.

**Confidence is uneven, and the low-confidence entries are the ones most cited in public.** Tropical and boreal forest dieback — the "Amazon tipping point" of popular coverage — carries **low** confidence for abrupt change. Antarctic sea ice carries low confidence on both properties. Meanwhile permafrost carbon and the West Antarctic ice sheet, which score high confidence, receive less attention.

**Some famous candidates are assessed as not tipping elements at all.** Arctic summer sea ice is assessed as *not* subject to abrupt change, with high confidence, and as reversible within years to decades. It is likely to be lost under continued warming, but that is a different claim, as [the ice-albedo feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ice-albedo-feedback-explained) discusses.

**Irreversibility and abruptness genuinely decouple.** Greenland is not abrupt but is irreversible for millennia; Arctic winter sea ice is abrupt but reversible within decades. Neither maps onto the popular image.

**"Deep uncertainty" appears above 3 °C.** For the West Antarctic ice sheet and global sea-level rise, AR6 uses this phrase specifically — meaning experts do not agree on, or cannot confidently assign, probabilities. That is a stronger admission than a wide range, and it is confined to the high-warming end.

## Why the science is hard here

Three structural reasons, none of which will be resolved soon.

**No observed precedent under the current forcing.** Paleoclimate records contain abrupt transitions, but under different configurations of ice, geography, and forcing rate. Transferring a threshold from one state to another is an extrapolation.

**Thresholds are defined by processes below model resolution.** Ice-shelf buttressing, marine ice-cliff behaviour, and convective organisation are all sub-grid processes in most models, so the physics that would produce a threshold is parameterised rather than resolved — as [Earth system models](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-models-explained) explains.

**Early-warning signals are statistically weak.** Methods based on critical slowing down require long, well-sampled records of the specific variable that is approaching a threshold, and for most candidate elements no such record exists.

## Reading a tipping-point claim

Four questions dispose of most weak claims.

Which element, specifically? The assessment differs sharply between them, and a general claim about "tipping points" cannot inherit the confidence of the best-supported case.

Abrupt, irreversible, or both? These require different evidence and appear in different combinations.

Over what timescale is irreversibility asserted? Centuries and millennia carry very different policy meaning, and AR6 distinguishes them.

What confidence level does the source assign? Low confidence in the assessment does not become high confidence by being restated more forcefully.

The associated question of whether the AMOC in particular is weakening — a very likely decline, distinct from a collapse — is set out in [ocean circulation and climate](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate), and the cryosphere entries are developed in [the cryosphere in the Earth system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/cryosphere-in-the-earth-system).
