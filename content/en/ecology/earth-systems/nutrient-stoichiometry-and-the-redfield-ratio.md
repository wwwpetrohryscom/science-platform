Biogeochemical cycles are usually described one element at a time. What links them is that organisms need the elements in roughly fixed proportions, so a shortage of one constrains what can be done with the others. That coupling has a name and a number, and the number turns out to be softer than its use in models implies.

## The ratio

Alfred Redfield identified a mean carbon-to-nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio in marine particulate matter of 106:16:1, and observed that it matched the ratio of dissolved nitrate to phosphate in the deep ocean. His inference reversed the expected direction of causation: rather than plankton composition being set by seawater chemistry, deep-ocean nutrient concentrations are controlled by the elemental requirements of the surface plankton that produce the sinking material.

That is one of the more consequential ideas in [Earth system science](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-science-explained), because it makes biology a control on ocean chemistry rather than a passenger.

## What a global compilation shows

A 2014 dataset paper in *Scientific Data* assembled particulate organic carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus measurements across the global ocean and reported the central values directly: "The global median C:P, N:P, and C:N ratios are 163, 22, and 6.6, respectively, but the data also includes extensive variation between samples from different regions."

A median N:P of 22 against a Redfield value of 16 is not a rounding difference. Nor is a median C:P of 163 against 106. The deviations also have structure: surface particulate ratios tend to exceed Redfield proportions in the subtropical gyres and fall below them in high-latitude and equatorial upwelling regions.

That pattern makes physical sense. Nutrient-poor subtropical water selects for cells that economise on phosphorus, which is used disproportionately in ribosomes and therefore in fast growth; nutrient-rich upwelling water supports fast-growing cells that are ribosome-rich and phosphorus-hungry. Stoichiometry tracks growth strategy, and growth strategy tracks supply.

## Why treating it as a constant matters

Global carbon-cycle models routinely convert between carbon and nutrients using a fixed ratio. Three things go wrong when the ratio is not fixed.

**Export estimates inherit the error.** Converting a measured nutrient drawdown into a carbon export flux uses C:P or C:N directly, so a 50% error in the ratio is a 50% error in the flux.

**The response to change is misrepresented.** If warming shifts the ocean toward more subtropical-like conditions, and subtropical plankton have higher C:P, then the same phosphorus supports more carbon export than a fixed-ratio model predicts. The sign of that feedback depends on a quantity the model has assumed away.

**Limitation is misdiagnosed.** Whether a region is nitrogen- or phosphorus-limited is judged partly by comparing ambient N:P against the ratio organisms need. If that requirement varies, the diagnosis varies with it.

## The link to the land

Terrestrial systems have their own stoichiometric constraints and much wider ranges, because wood is mostly carbon and holds very little nitrogen or phosphorus. The consequence is that a forest can accumulate carbon with far less nutrient than a plankton community would need for the same carbon — up to a point, after which nutrient supply becomes the binding constraint on growth.

That is the mechanism behind progressive nutrient limitation, and it is one reason the land [carbon sink](/en/ecology/earth-systems/carbon-cycle-explained)'s future is uncertain in a way that does not depend on climate physics at all. The nitrogen half of the problem runs through [the nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained) and through the [energetic cost of fixing it](/en/biology/microbiology/nitrogen-fixation-and-its-cost); the phosphorus half has no biological fixation route at all, because there is no atmospheric reservoir to draw on.
