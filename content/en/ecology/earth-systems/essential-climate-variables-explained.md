Behind every long climate record is a prior decision that someone should keep measuring that particular thing, indefinitely, at global scale, whatever else changes. Those decisions are not made ad hoc. They are codified as **Essential Climate Variables**, and the list is short enough to be worth knowing and structured enough to be worth reading closely.

## What an ECV is

The Global Climate Observing System, run under the auspices of the WMO, defines an [Essential Climate Variable](/en/glossary/essential-climate-variable) as a physical, chemical, or biological variable — or a group of linked variables — that critically contributes to characterising Earth's climate. GCOS currently specifies **55** of them, organised across three observation domains: atmosphere, ocean, and land, with ten subdomains between them.

The stated purpose is to provide the empirical evidence needed to understand and predict how climate evolves, to guide mitigation and adaptation, to assess risk, to enable attribution of events to causes, and to underpin climate services.

## The three selection criteria

GCOS applies three tests, and the third is the one that reveals what kind of list this is.

**Relevance.** The variable is critical for characterising the climate system and its changes.

**Feasibility.** Observing or deriving it at global scale is technically feasible using proven, scientifically understood methods.

**Cost effectiveness.** Generating and archiving the data is affordable, relying mainly on coordinated observing systems using proven technology and taking advantage of historical datasets where possible.

The list is therefore not "the 55 most scientifically interesting quantities". It is the intersection of what matters, what can currently be measured globally, and what can be sustained financially. Several quantities of genuine importance are absent because they fail the second or third test — which is a statement about the observing system rather than about the science, and worth remembering when a variable turns out to be missing from a dataset.

## What is actually on the list

Reading the variable names is more instructive than the count, because the list is structured by who measures what rather than by conceptual tidiness.

In the **atmosphere** domain, the surface subdomain covers precipitation, surface pressure, the surface radiation budget, surface temperature, surface water vapour, and surface wind; the upper-atmosphere subdomain adds the Earth radiation budget, lightning, and upper-air temperature, humidity, and wind; and atmospheric composition covers clouds, aerosols, ozone, aerosol and ozone precursors, and the greenhouse gases.

The **land** domain is the most heterogeneous. Its hydrology subdomain includes groundwater, lakes, river discharge, terrestrial water storage, evaporation from land, and soil moisture. Its cryosphere subdomain covers glaciers, ice sheets and ice shelves, permafrost, and snow. Its biology subdomain covers above-ground biomass, albedo, fire, the fraction of absorbed photosynthetically active radiation, land cover, land-surface temperature, leaf area index, and soil carbon. A fourth subdomain covers human use of natural resources, comprising anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions and anthropogenic water use.

The **ocean** domain splits three ways. Physical variables include sea-surface and subsurface temperature and salinity, surface and subsurface currents, sea level, sea state, sea ice, surface stress, and the ocean surface heat flux. Biogeochemical variables include inorganic carbon, oxygen, nutrients, nitrous oxide, ocean colour, and transient tracers. The biological subdomain is the shortest on the whole list: marine habitats and plankton.

Two things stand out. The **cryosphere and biosphere are not top-level domains** here, unlike in the conceptual five-component picture used in [Earth system components](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-components-explained) — they appear as subdomains of land, which reflects who operates the instruments rather than any physical judgement. And the **biological variables are conspicuously sparse** relative to the physical ones, which is the feasibility criterion showing through: a satellite can retrieve sea-surface temperature globally every day, and nothing does the equivalent for marine ecosystems.

## Why the concept is useful

Standardising the list does three things that ad-hoc measurement cannot.

It makes **records comparable across agencies and decades**. When NASA, NOAA, ESA, and Copernicus all produce a version of the same ECV to a common specification, the products can be cross-validated rather than merely coexisting.

It **anchors model evaluation**. Comparing an [Earth system model](/en/ecology/earth-systems/earth-system-models-explained) to observations requires agreement on what is being compared; ECVs supply the shared vocabulary.

It **justifies continuity funding**. A satellite mission is easier to sustain when it is understood as maintaining an agreed variable rather than as a discrete science project. Continuity is the scarce resource in climate observation, and gaps cannot be filled retrospectively.

## The relationship to indicators

An ECV and a climate indicator are related but not the same, and the distinction is easy to lose.

An ECV is a **measured variable** — sea-surface temperature, surface albedo, above-ground biomass. A climate indicator is a **derived quantity chosen for communication**, usually aggregating several ECVs into something with a clear physical interpretation and a good signal-to-noise ratio. Global mean surface temperature is an indicator built from temperature ECVs; ocean heat content is an indicator built from subsurface temperature and salinity.

The existing article on [climate indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-indicators-earth-system-monitoring) covers the indicator layer. ECVs sit beneath it, and the mapping between the two is many-to-one.

## The parallel in biodiversity

The same idea was deliberately reproduced for living systems. Essential Biodiversity Variables serve the analogous function of specifying what must be measured to track biodiversity comparably across countries and methods, and the design borrowed directly from the ECV framework — including the insistence that a variable be feasibly observable and not merely desirable.

The parallel is covered in the existing article on [Essential Biodiversity Variables](/en/ecology/biodiversity/essential-biodiversity-variables-monitoring), and reading the two together shows how much harder the biological case is: living systems have no equivalent of a thermometer. The climate list registers that difficulty in its own structure — its ocean biological subdomain contains just two entries, marine habitats and plankton, against eleven physical ocean variables. What can be inferred about life from orbit, and what cannot, is the subject of [remote sensing for biodiversity monitoring](/en/ecology/biodiversity/remote-sensing-for-biodiversity-monitoring).

## Where the framework strains

Three limitations are worth stating.

**Feasibility drifts.** A variable excluded on technical grounds a decade ago may now be measurable, and the list is periodically revised. That means an older document's ECV list is not the current one, and specifications matter as much as names.

**Global coverage is uneven.** Meeting the specification for an ECV in the North Atlantic and in the Southern Ocean are very different propositions. A variable can be formally observed while being poorly constrained across large regions, which is a limitation the headline count conceals.

**A variable is not a product.** Several independent groups may produce different datasets for the same ECV using different retrievals and corrections, and these can disagree beyond their stated uncertainties. The record for a given ECV is therefore a family of products, and comparing across them requires the same care described in [remote-sensing limitations and uncertainty](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty).

The satellite half of the observing system that supplies most ECVs is described in the [Earth observation](/en/ecology/earth-observation/earth-observation-and-remote-sensing-explained) cluster.
