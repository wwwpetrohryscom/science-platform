There are two [carbon cycles](/en/glossary/carbon-cycle), running side by side at speeds that differ by roughly seven orders of magnitude, and almost every confusion about carbon comes from treating them as one. This article separates them, then follows the human perturbation through the fast one.

The complementary article on [carbon-cycle feedbacks](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-cycle-feedbacks) takes up what happens to the sinks under continued warming; the aim here is the prior question of how the cycle works when it is behaving normally.

## The slow cycle: rock as the long-term regulator

By far the largest carbon reservoir is sedimentary rock. NASA's Earth Observatory puts around 65,500 billion tonnes of carbon in rocks, dwarfing the ocean, atmosphere, soil, and biomass combined.

Carbon enters rock through the weathering of silicate minerals, which consumes atmospheric CO₂ and delivers dissolved carbonate to the ocean, where organisms and chemistry precipitate it into sediment. It returns through volcanism and metamorphism. NASA describes a full circuit as taking 100 to 200 million years, with something on the order of 10 to 100 million tonnes of carbon moving through this loop annually, and volcanoes contributing between 130 and 380 million tonnes of CO₂ per year.

The slow cycle acts as a thermostat over geological time: warmer, wetter conditions accelerate weathering, which draws down CO₂ and cools the planet. It is a genuine damping [feedback](/en/glossary/climate-feedback) of the kind described in [climate feedback mechanisms](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-feedback-mechanisms), and it is also far too slow to respond to anything happening on a human timescale.

## The fast cycle: air, water, and life

The fast cycle moves between 1,000 and 100,000 million tonnes of carbon a year through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, and air–sea gas exchange. Its reservoirs are the atmosphere, the surface and deep ocean, vegetation, and soils.

Two features of this loop matter for interpreting any carbon claim. First, the gross fluxes are very large and very nearly balanced — the land takes up and releases similar quantities each year, and so does the ocean surface. Second, because they are nearly balanced, the observable net is a small residual of two large numbers, which is why net fluxes carry proportionally much larger uncertainty than gross ones.

Seasonality makes the fast cycle visible: atmospheric CO₂ measured in the Northern Hemisphere rises and falls each year as Northern land vegetation leafs out and senesces, a signal recorded continuously in NOAA's global monitoring network and described from the measurement side in the existing article on [greenhouse-gas concentration monitoring](/en/ecology/climate-change/greenhouse-gas-concentrations-monitoring).

## What human activity changed

The perturbation is not that carbon is being emitted — the fast cycle has always cycled carbon. It is that fossil carbon, buried by the slow cycle, is being injected into the fast one faster than the fast cycle's sinks can move it back out.

The IPCC's assessment for 2010–2019 gives an average of 10.9 ± 0.9 petagrams of carbon released per year, of which 81 to 91 per cent came from fossil-fuel combustion and the remainder from land-use change. That release was partitioned three ways: 46 per cent stayed in the atmosphere at 5.1 ± 0.02 petagrams a year, 23 per cent was taken up by the ocean at 2.5 ± 0.6, and 31 per cent by land vegetation at 3.4 ± 0.9. The ocean and land sinks together have therefore absorbed a bit more than half of what was emitted.

The atmospheric consequence is measured directly, and here it is worth showing how much the exact figure depends on the product. NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory records a global growth rate of 3.76 parts per million for 2024, against annual increases of roughly 2.2 to 2.7 parts per million in the preceding several years. NOAA Climate.gov reports the 2024 global annual average as 422.8 parts per million with a 3.75 ppm increase, while the WMO's Global Atmosphere Watch puts the same year's global mean at 423.9 ± 0.2 parts per million, about 52 per cent above the pre-1750 level. The roughly one-part-per-million gap is not a disagreement about the atmosphere; it reflects different station networks, averaging conventions, and calibration scales. Quoting any of these numbers without naming the product is the most common way carbon reporting goes slightly wrong.

## The airborne fraction, and why it is the number to watch

The **airborne fraction** — the share of emissions that remains in the atmosphere — has stayed near 44 per cent across six decades, according to the AR6 assessment. That constancy is easy to misread.

It does not mean the sinks are fixed. It means the sinks have so far grown roughly in proportion to emissions: as atmospheric CO₂ rises, the ocean absorbs more by simple partial-pressure difference, and vegetation photosynthesises somewhat faster. The IPCC assesses with high confidence that both sinks have continued to grow over the past six decades.

It also does not mean they will continue to. Each reservoir that absorbs more than it releases acts as a [carbon sink](/en/glossary/carbon-sink), and a sink's capacity is a property of its chemistry and biology rather than a constant. The same assessment notes that ocean carbon chemistry is beginning to change in response to the growing sink, in ways expected to weaken future uptake under medium- to high-emissions scenarios, even though no weakening trend is yet detectable in the 1960–2019 record. Land uptake is the more variable of the two, responding strongly to drought and to [ENSO](/en/ecology/earth-systems/el-nino-la-nina-enso-explained) phase — one reason a single year's land sink says little about the trend.

## Where the accounting is hardest

Three parts of the budget are systematically harder than the rest.

**Land-use change fluxes** are the least constrained term, because they require knowing not just how much forest was cleared but what carbon stock it held and what replaced it. The uncertainty on this term is large relative to its size.

**Soil carbon** is a very large reservoir measured to inconsistent depths, and its response to warming involves competing effects — faster decomposition against increased plant input — that are difficult to separate in field data.

**Ocean uptake** is measured by several methods, including surface CO₂ observations, interior inventory changes, and models, and these do not always agree. The uncertainty range quoted above reflects that spread rather than measurement noise on any one method; the ocean's role in absorbing energy as well as carbon is covered in [ocean circulation and climate](/en/ecology/earth-systems/ocean-circulation-and-climate).

These uncertainties matter beyond science reporting: the same measurement problems determine what a forest-carbon credit is actually certifying, an issue examined in the insight on [carbon offset markets and climate science](/en/insight/carbon-offset-outsourcing-science). For anyone reading carbon-budget figures, the practical implication is that the annual global budget is revised each year as datasets are updated, and comparing a number from one release against a number from another can manufacture a trend that is not there. Satellite constraints on the land component are covered from the measurement side in [Earth observation](/en/ecology/earth-observation/earth-observation-and-remote-sensing-explained), and the forest-carbon aspect is examined in the existing article on the [temperate forest carbon sink](/en/ecology/climate-change/temperate-forest-carbon-sink-decline).
