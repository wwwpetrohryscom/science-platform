Every other environmental contaminant can be sampled after the event. A metal stays in the sediment, a persistent compound stays in the fat, a particulate leaves a filter to weigh. Sound and artificial light leave nothing. They exist only while the source is running, which means an unmeasured exposure is gone for good and a monitoring programme has to be present at the time rather than clever afterwards. That property runs through everything else about these two stressors — how they are quantified, how effects are attributed to them, and why they arrived so late in the regulatory architecture that the [triad of source, pathway and receptor](/en/ecology/pollution/environmental-pollution-explained) is built to describe.

It also cuts the other way, and this is the unusual part. Because there is no residue, there is no legacy burden. Switching a light off ends its contribution that night; slowing a ship reduces the received level in the water immediately. Remediation is instantaneous in a way it never is for a metal or a fluorinated surfactant.

## Two quantities that are not concentrations

Noise is reported as a sound pressure level and light at night as a brightness, and neither is an accumulated quantity. Both are rates, and both standard metrics carry a hidden calibration: they are weighted for a human observer. A-weighted decibels compress the spectrum according to the sensitivity of human hearing, which discards exactly the low- and high-frequency energy that matters to species hearing outside that range. Sky brightness in the visible band is defined against human vision, which is a problem when the biologically active wavelengths for many organisms sit at the blue end or beyond it. The metrics are not wrong; they are calibrated to the wrong receptor, and every threshold expressed in them inherits that.

The measurement problem this creates is not hypothetical. Analysis of [51,351 naked-eye star-visibility observations collected by citizen scientists](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abq7781) between 2011 and 2022 found the number of visible stars falling at a rate consistent with sky brightness increasing 7 to 10 per cent a year in the human visible band. That rate is faster than satellite observations of emissions indicate, and the authors attribute the discrepancy to changes in the spectrum of the light being emitted and to the average angle at which it leaves fixtures — properties the orbital record does not capture well. An instrument that looks authoritative can therefore under-read a trend for reasons that have nothing to do with the trend itself, which is the same class of problem that recurs whenever an index is mistaken for the thing it summarises, as with [air quality indices](/en/ecology/pollution/air-quality-measurement-and-standards).

## Masking is the mechanism doing most of the work

The dominant ecological effect of noise is not damage to hearing. It is masking: raising the background level against which a signal has to be detected, so that a call, an echolocation click or the sound of an approaching predator becomes unusable at ranges where it previously worked. This converts an acoustic habitat into a smaller one without altering a single physical feature of it, which is why noise belongs in the same conversation as [habitat connectivity](/en/ecology/conservation/habitat-fragmentation-and-connectivity) rather than in a separate compartment.

A systematic review of research published between 1990 and 2013 found that terrestrial wildlife responses begin at noise levels of approximately 40 dBA, with 20 per cent of the papers documenting impacts below 50 dBA — levels far below anything that would damage an ear. The same review is candid about the shape of its own evidence base: studies concentrate on European and North American species that rely on vocal communication, with roughly two-thirds of the dataset covering songbirds and marine mammals.

The breadth question was addressed separately by a phylogenetically controlled [meta-analysis of anthropogenic noise effects](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsbl.2019.0649) covering amphibians, arthropods, birds, fish, mammals, molluscs and reptiles. Its most useful result is a negative one: phylogeny contributed only a little to the variation in response. Effects are not concentrated in a handful of unusually sensitive lineages; the majority of species tested responded. That distinction matters for policy, because a stressor affecting a few sensitive taxa can be managed by protecting those taxa, and a stressor affecting most of them cannot.

## Underwater, the physics inverts

Water absorbs light within the upper part of the column and carries sound far beyond it, which makes the ocean an acoustic environment in a way no terrestrial habitat is. A 2021 review of [the ocean soundscape](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba4658) frames the change as a shift in three components at once: anthrophony, the sound of human activity, has risen with shipping, resource exploration and construction; biophony, the sound of life, has fallen with hunting, fishing and habitat degradation; and geophony, the abiotic soundscape, is being altered by climate change. The signal-to-noise ratio has therefore moved twice, from both directions.

Animals do compensate, and the limits of that compensation are measurable. A study of male bearded seals in the Arctic found that [vocalising individuals raised their call amplitude as ambient noise rose, up to a threshold](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2020.2712) — beyond which source levels stopped increasing and the calls could no longer keep pace with the background. The existence of that ceiling is the finding. Behavioural flexibility does not make a species safe from noise; it makes the dose-response curve non-linear, with a region where nothing appears to happen followed by a region where it does.

## Light at night: amount, timing and spectrum

The global picture was quantified by [an atlas of artificial sky brightness](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.1600377) published in 2016 and built from high-resolution satellite data together with ground-based sky-brightness measurements, which found more than 80 per cent of the world's population and more than 99 per cent of the US and European populations living under light-polluted skies. The Milky Way is hidden from more than a third of humanity, including 60 per cent of Europeans and nearly 80 per cent of North Americans, and 23 per cent of the world's land surface between 75°N and 60°S experiences light-polluted nights, rising to 88 per cent of Europe. These are exposure figures, not effect figures, and the distinction is worth holding.

On effects, a meta-analysis drawing on a database of published studies found that exposure to [artificial light at night](/en/glossary/artificial-light-at-night) induces strong responses in physiological measures, in daily activity patterns and in life-history traits, with particularly strong effects on hormone levels, on the onset of daily activity in diurnal species, and on traits including number of offspring, predation, cognition and the sea-finding behaviour of turtles. The mechanism is not brightness alone: the timing and the spectral composition of the light determine whether a given illuminance disrupts a circadian or seasonal cue, which is why the shift in the emitted [electromagnetic spectrum](/en/glossary/electromagnetic-spectrum) towards shorter wavelengths changed the biological question even where total emissions were flat. The same meta-analysis notes that very few studies have addressed consequences at the level of ecosystem function, so the well-supported findings are individual and population-level, and the community-level extrapolation is not yet earned.

## Why regulation lagged, and what it still leaves out

Noise did eventually acquire a regulatory apparatus, built around human receptors. The World Health Organization's 2019 guidelines for the European Region recommend exposure levels for road traffic, railway, aircraft, wind turbine and leisure noise on health grounds, and the EU Environmental Noise Directive obliges member states to produce strategic noise maps and action plans using harmonised indicators. The scale that machinery describes is substantial: on the European Environment Agency's latest estimates, which are revised as member states report under the directive, about 92 million people are exposed to harmful day-evening-night road traffic noise, more than a fifth of Europeans to unhealthy long-term levels, at least 17 million are highly annoyed and 4.6 million highly sleep-disturbed, with long-term transport noise estimated to cause around 66,000 premature deaths and 50,000 new cardiovascular disease cases each year. Against a 2017 baseline, the bloc's zero-pollution ambition sets 2030 as the date by which the number of people chronically disturbed by transport noise should fall by 30 per cent.

Every one of those numbers has a human in the receptor position. There is no equivalent set of ecological limit values, because there is no agreed exposure metric for a wildlife receptor and no dose-response function general enough to write one — the responses vary by species, by frequency, by season and by what the animal was doing at the time. Artificial light has less than that: no directive, no mapping obligation, and a measurement standard defined against human vision. Both stressors also travel with the same land-use pattern, which makes them substantially a question of how settlements are built and lit, as taken up in [urban ecology](/en/ecology/ecosystems/urban-ecology-explained).

The honest position is that exposure is well characterised and rising, mechanisms are understood at the level of individual organisms, and the population and community consequences are inferred rather than demonstrated for most taxa. That is a weaker evidence base than the one behind particulate air pollution, and a stronger one than the absence of regulation would suggest.
