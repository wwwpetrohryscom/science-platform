A substance is not pollution because of what it is. It becomes pollution when three things line up: a **source** that releases it, a **pathway** that carries it somewhere, and a **receptor** — a person, a population, a species, a habitat — sitting at the far end of that pathway. Cut any one of the three and the substance is still present and still toxic, but the harm is gone. Cap a contaminated site, and the source remains while the pathway closes. Move a drinking-water intake, and the receptor steps out of the way.

That is the operational logic of nearly every environmental regulation in force, and it is why two identical drums of solvent — one buried in dry rock, one buried above an aquifer that supplies a town — are not the same problem. What follows is the machinery that turns the triad into numbers, and the places where it strains.

## Hazard travels with the substance; risk travels with the situation

The US Environmental Protection Agency defines a stressor as "any physical, chemical, or biological entity that can induce an adverse effect in humans or ecosystems", and risk as "the chance of harmful effects to human health or to ecological systems resulting from exposure to an environmental stressor". The first is a property. The second is a statement about a specific configuration of source, pathway and receptor.

Most arguments that go badly in public confuse the two. "Compound X causes cancer in animals" is a hazard claim; it can be true while the risk from a given product is negligible, because no pathway connects them. "There is no detectable X in the water" is a pathway claim; it can be true while the hazard is severe, and it says nothing about the air or the soil. The reply that the dose makes the poison does not settle either dispute — it relocates it to the dose–response relationship, which is where the genuine scientific disagreement lives.

## Where the argument actually happens

Formal assessment proceeds in four steps: identify the hazard, characterise the dose–response relationship, estimate the exposure, then combine them into a risk characterisation. Steps one, three and four are laborious but rarely contested in principle. Step two is different, because it requires an assumption about what happens at doses too low to test. Two are available. The first is the threshold hypothesis, which the EPA describes as holding "that a range of exposures from zero to some finite value can be tolerated by the organism with essentially no response". The second is that no such range exists, and effects scale down to zero dose without ever reaching a floor. Regulators choose between them by mode of action, and where the mechanistic evidence is thin the agency's stated default is explicit: "if 'mode of action' information is insufficient, then linear extrapolation is typically used as the default approach for dose-response assessment."

The two assumptions produce different kinds of number. Under a threshold, the output is a reference dose — defined by the agency's Integrated Risk Information System as "an estimate (with uncertainty spanning perhaps an order of magnitude) of a daily oral exposure to the human population (including sensitive subgroups) that is likely to be without an appreciable risk of deleterious effects during a lifetime". Under linearity, the output is a slope: an oral slope factor, or an inhalation unit risk expressing the added lifetime cancer risk from breathing 1 µg/m³. A slope never crosses zero, so there is no concentration the method can call safe — only concentrations at which the modelled risk is small.

The misconception worth correcting here is that a reference dose marks a boundary between safe and unsafe. It does not. The parenthesis in its own definition concedes roughly an order of magnitude of uncertainty, and the value is built by dividing an observed effect level by uncertainty factors covering extrapolation between species, variation between people, and gaps in the database. It is a defensible engineering margin, not a cliff edge. The empirical version of the same question — whether any threshold is visible in real populations — is examined for one pollutant in [the evidence on particulate matter and health](/en/ecology/pollution/particulate-matter-and-health-evidence).

## Screening before assessing

Full assessment is expensive, and there are far more chemicals in commerce than there are assessments. So regulators screen first, on three properties: how long a compound persists, whether it concentrates in tissue, and whether it is toxic.

These are not three independent worries. The screen works because of how they interact along the pathway. Persistence widens the window in which exposure can occur and lets a compound travel far from where it was used. Bio-accumulation breaks the intuitive link between ambient concentration and delivered dose, so a level far below analytical concern in water can still deliver a substantial dose to an animal near the top of a food chain. Toxicity supplies the effect. Any two without the third is usually manageable; the combination is not. The treaty version of that screen, with its published thresholds, is set out in the article on [persistent organic pollutants](/en/ecology/pollution/persistent-organic-pollutants), and the complication that a metal's behaviour depends on its chemical form rather than on the element is taken up in [heavy metals and speciation](/en/ecology/pollution/heavy-metals-in-the-environment).

Screening has legal teeth. Under section 6(h) of the amended US Toxic Substances Control Act, five chemicals were identified as persistent, bio-accumulative and toxic and made subject to final rules issued in January 2021, with revisions to two of them in November 2024. The shape of the action matters more than the list: expedited restriction on the basis of properties, without waiting for a full quantitative risk assessment of every use. The awkward inverse case, where persistence is beyond doubt and the effect evidence is not, is the subject of [the microplastics evidence](/en/ecology/pollution/microplastics-evidence-and-uncertainty).

## How a number becomes a legal limit

Two distinct judgements get made, and conflating them is the most common reason people conclude that regulators disagree about science when they do not.

US drinking-water regulation separates them explicitly. A maximum contaminant level goal is "the maximum level of a contaminant in drinking water at which no known or anticipated adverse effect on the health of persons would occur, allowing an adequate margin of safety"; it is unenforceable and, in the agency's words, considers "only public health and not the limits of detection and treatment technology effectiveness". For a chemical carcinogen with no dose considered safe, that goal is set at zero. The enforceable maximum contaminant level is then set "as close to the MCLG as feasible", taking cost into consideration. One number is a health statement. The other is a health statement filtered through analytical chemistry, treatment engineering and money.

Air standards have the same architecture without always advertising it. The World Health Organization's 2021 guideline level for annual fine particulate matter is 5 µg/m³, derived from health evidence alone. The European Union's binding annual limit value for the same pollutant, in force since 2015, is 25 µg/m³. Those two numbers are not competing scientific claims; one is a target and the other is an attainability judgement made across twenty-seven economies. Which figure a report quotes changes how many people appear to be breathing acceptable air, a point developed in [what air quality standards and indices actually report](/en/ecology/pollution/air-quality-measurement-and-standards). The same distinction shapes how monitoring results are read in [freshwater quality measurement](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained).

## Where the framework strains

Four weaknesses are structural rather than incidental.

Real exposure is to mixtures, and the machinery evaluates one substance at a time. A working group on chemical mixtures and cancer risk put the obstacles plainly: people encounter many chemicals across a lifetime, and studying their joint action runs into long latency and hard experimental design choices before it runs into anything else. Cumulative methods exist; they are not the default.

Exposure is also unequally distributed, so an assessment run on a population average can be right about the population and wrong about the people carrying the highest burden — and non-chemical stressors such as heat, crowding and deprivation modify response while sitting outside chemical frameworks entirely.

Some pollutants leave no residue to measure. Anthropogenic noise and artificial light satisfy the triad exactly, but there is nothing to sample from a river or filter from the air, which is part of why their regulation lagged; that case is examined in [noise and light as pollutants](/en/ecology/pollution/noise-and-light-pollution-ecology).

And a single release can generate several successive problems with different receptors, so no one instrument addresses it. Reactive nitrogen is the standing example, traced through its stages in [the nitrogen cascade](/en/ecology/pollution/nitrogen-pollution-and-eutrophication). A National Academies consensus study, *Science and Decisions*, was commissioned partly because of these limits, and framed its task around uncertainty characterisation, probabilistic analysis and the default assumptions used where knowledge runs out.

None of which makes the framework ornamental. Where a pathway has been cut cleanly — leaded petrol withdrawn, a sulphur limit imposed on fuel, a solvent restricted at source — the monitoring networks that set the limit have gone on to record the decline, and in the United States the aggregate fall in the criteria pollutants has run alongside decades of economic and population growth rather than tracking them. The honest summary is narrower than either enthusiasts or critics of environmental regulation usually offer: the triad is a good account of when a substance causes harm, an adequate account of how much, and a poor account of what happens when many substances and many stressors arrive together.
