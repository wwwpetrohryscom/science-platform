Nitrogen pollution is usually discussed as though it were one problem awaiting one solution. It is several problems arranged in sequence. A nitrogen atom fixed into fertiliser can volatilise as ammonia and form secondary particulate in the air; deposit onto a heathland and shift its plant community; leach as nitrate into groundwater and then a river; feed an algal bloom whose decay strips oxygen from a coastal seabed; and finally leave as nitrous oxide, a long-lived greenhouse gas. Each stop is a distinct receptor with a distinct regulator, and the atom does not stop causing effects when the first agency loses interest.

That sequence is what the literature calls the nitrogen cascade. The underlying biogeochemistry — fixation, nitrification, denitrification and the rest — is set out in the existing article on [the nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained). What follows is what the cascade does to pollution policy, which is a different and in some ways harder question, and one that stress-tests the single-substance logic described in [the source, pathway and receptor framework](/en/ecology/pollution/environmental-pollution-explained).

## The cascade, with numbers on it

A Royal Society synthesis of [the global nitrogen cycle in the twenty-first century](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3682748/) puts total global nitrogen fixation at 413 Tg of reactive nitrogen delivered to terrestrial and marine ecosystems each year, with human activity responsible for half of that, 210 Tg N. Most of the anthropogenic share is processed on land — 240 Tg N a year moving through soils and vegetation, dominated by fertiliser inputs.

The leaks are large in absolute terms. Ammonia emissions from land, together with combustion-derived nitrogen oxides, put roughly 100 Tg N a year into the atmosphere. Leaching and riverine transport deliver 40 to 70 Tg N a year to coastal waters and the open ocean, on top of about 30 Tg deposited there directly from the air; marine biological fixation adds a further 140 Tg N a year, so ocean processing of reactive nitrogen is comparable in magnitude to the terrestrial flux while remaining more natural in origin.

These totals are inventory-dependent, and different assessments count different things — whether combustion is treated as fixation, whether marine fixation is included — so the ratios quoted in popular accounts vary more than the underlying science does.

## Different receptors, different clocks

The same synthesis gives the residence times, and they are the reason a single instrument cannot manage the cascade. Reactive nitrogen other than nitrous oxide persists in the atmosphere for only a few weeks. In terrestrial ecosystems it persists for a few decades, and in peatlands for 10² to 10³ years. In the ocean the lifetime is less well constrained but appears longer still, and the paper flags marine reactive nitrogen as a potentially important long-term source of nitrous oxide that "will respond very slowly to control measures on the sources of Nr from which it is produced".

The policy consequence is uncomfortable but not mysterious. Cutting emissions today changes the atmospheric term within a season; it changes the soil term over decades, because the accumulated pool has to be worked down; and it changes the marine nitrous oxide term over a period nobody can put a firm number on. Lags between an emissions reduction and an ecological response are therefore expected. Treating an absence of quick recovery as evidence that a measure failed is a category error — although so is assuming recovery will eventually arrive on its own.

## Ammonia is the term that has not moved

Europe provides the cleanest test, because the same directive framework has covered several nitrogen-bearing pollutants over the same period. Between 2005 and 2023, EU-27 emissions of sulphur oxides fell 84%, nitrogen oxides 53%, fine particulate matter 38% and non-methane volatile organic compounds 35%. Ammonia fell 17%.

The European Environment Agency attributes the gap to limited progress in agriculture, which is responsible for more than 94% of EU ammonia emissions, and reports that as of 2023 only twelve member states had met their 2030 ammonia reduction commitments. The structural reason is worth stating plainly: nitrogen oxides and sulphur oxides come from a countable number of large combustion sources amenable to engineering controls, while ammonia comes from millions of dispersed field applications and livestock housings where abatement is a change in management practice rather than a device bolted onto a stack.

Ammonia is also an air quality pollutant in its own right, because it neutralises atmospheric acids to form ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate — a large secondary contribution to the fine particulate mass discussed in [what the monitors and the index report](/en/ecology/pollution/air-quality-measurement-and-standards). A nutrient policy is therefore also a respiratory-health policy, which is not how either is usually budgeted.

## A measurement that missed, and why the target is an average

At the coastal end of the cascade, the mechanism is well understood. Nutrients delivered by a river stimulate algal growth; the algae die, sink, and consume oxygen as they decompose; stratification prevents resupply from the surface. Water with 2 mg/L of dissolved oxygen or less at the seabed is classed as hypoxic, and mobile animals leave.

The northern Gulf survey for 2026 illustrates why single measurements make poor policy tests. In June the forecast, driven mainly by river discharge and nutrient-runoff data, projected an above-average hypoxic zone of 7,027 square miles. The July survey measured about 1,332 square miles — the second smallest in forty years of measurement, and outside the forecast's stated uncertainty range. The reason was meteorological rather than nutritional: a tropical storm crossed the survey area on the eve of the cruise and mixed oxygen-rich surface water down to the seabed.

This is precisely why the management target is not framed as a single-year number. The Hypoxia Task Force goal is to bring the five-year average extent below 1,900 square miles by 2035; the current five-year average stands at 3,745 square miles, close to twice the goal. An indicator that responds strongly to weather has to be averaged before it can measure a nutrient-reduction programme, and the same reasoning applies to lake and shelf systems covered in [lake eutrophication](/en/ecology/freshwater/lake-ecology-and-eutrophication) and [ocean deoxygenation](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-deoxygenation-and-dead-zones).

## Critical loads, and a dose–response that is genuinely contested

On land the regulatory instrument is the critical load: the deposition rate an ecosystem can absorb before eutrophication effects appear. The European indicator shows movement in the right direction and short of the target. The area of EU ecosystems receiving nitrogen deposition above the eutrophication critical load fell from 1,249,000 km² in 2005 to 1,068,000 km² in 2023, a reduction of about 14%. The Zero Pollution Action Plan calls for a 25% reduction by 2030 against the 2005 baseline; the baseline projection reaches 19%.

Underneath that indicator sits an empirical dose–response, and it is not settled. A transect of 68 acid grasslands spanning the lower end of the industrialised world's deposition range, 5 to 35 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, reported that [species richness declined linearly with deposition](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1094678) — one species lost per 4 m² quadrat for every 2.5 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, and a 23% reduction in richness at the central European mean of 17 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. A later [spatial Bayesian reanalysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7195837/) of two British gradient datasets, accounting for spatial autocorrelation, found small and ambiguous effects and argued that space-for-time estimates had probably been over-stated. A [published comment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7810039/) countered that the reanalysis used modelled deposition at 5 × 5 km resolution while significant variation exists at 1 km, so treating the residual structure as spatially correlated error would itself bias the estimate downward, and concluded that the reanalysis was premature.

The calibrated reading is that the direction of effect is not in serious dispute — enrichment favours a smaller set of fast-growing competitors, in experiments as well as gradients — while the magnitude estimated from spatial gradients is method-dependent, and depends particularly on the resolution and error of the deposition covariate. Critical loads inherit that uncertainty, which is worth remembering when an exceedance area is quoted to the nearest thousand square kilometres.

Two practical conclusions follow. Success against sulphur is not evidence that the same instruments will work on ammonia, because the source structure is different. And an element that reappears as a different pollutant at every stage of its journey defeats the assumption, built into most environmental law, that a substance has one receptor and one competent authority. The soil-fertility side of the same trade-off — how much nitrogen a crop can actually use — is taken up in [nutrient availability and soil fertility](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-availability-and-soil-fertility).
