The phrase sounds like a chemical family. It is not one. "Persistent organic pollutant" is an operational definition: a set of measurable properties written into the text of an international treaty, and a compound earns the label by having those properties documented rather than by belonging to any structural class. Organochlorine pesticides, electrical insulating fluids, brominated flame retardants and fluorinated surfactants share a list because they behave alike once released, not because they resemble each other on paper. That is also why the category is a useful entry point into the wider [source, pathway and receptor framework](/en/ecology/pollution/environmental-pollution-explained) — it is one of the few pollutant classes defined by the pathway rather than by the source.

## The four properties a listing proposal has to document

Annex D of the Stockholm Convention sets out what a party must show before its review committee will even consider a chemical. The thresholds are specific.

| Screening criterion | What the proposal must show |
| --- | --- |
| Persistence | Half-life greater than two months in water, or greater than six months in soil, or greater than six months in sediment |
| Bio-accumulation | Bio-concentration or bio-accumulation factor in aquatic species greater than 5,000 — or, absent that measurement, a log Kow greater than 5 |
| Long-range transport | Measured levels far from any source, or monitoring or modelling showing transport by air, water or migratory species; for a chemical that moves mainly through the atmosphere, a half-life in air greater than two days |
| Adverse effects | Evidence of harm to health or the environment, or toxicity data indicating the potential for it |

Two things about that table are easy to miss. The numbers are thresholds for *consideration*, not verdicts, and most of them come with an escape hatch: persistence, for example, can also be established by evidence that a chemical is "otherwise sufficiently persistent to justify its consideration", because a hard cut-off would have excluded compounds that plainly belonged. And the criteria only matter in combination. Persistence on its own describes quartz sand. Toxicity on its own describes a great many substances that are handled safely because they break down. What generates the characteristic problem is the joint condition: a compound that survives long enough to travel, concentrates as it travels through tissue, and does damage when it arrives. Persistence here is a statement about [residence time](/en/glossary/residence-time), and the other three criteria describe what a long residence time is able to do.

## Why the Arctic inherits other regions' chemistry

The two-day air half-life is doing precise physical work. A semi-volatile compound evaporates from warm soil or water, rides an air mass, and condenses out again where temperatures drop. Repeat that cycle and the net displacement is poleward, because evaporation dominates where it is warm and deposition dominates where it is cold. Compounds never used within thousands of kilometres of the Arctic therefore turn up in Arctic air, snow and tissue, and no national prohibition reaches a substance that arrives on the wind.

The consequence at the receiving end is a matter of trophic position rather than proximity. It helps to keep three terms apart: bioconcentration is uptake from the surrounding medium, bioaccumulation is uptake by all routes combined, and biomagnification is the increase in tissue concentration from one feeding level to the next. Annex D screens on the first two; the third is what converts a moderate uptake factor into a large body burden at the top of a long chain, and it is the reason the [trophic structure of a food web](/en/ecology/ecosystems/food-webs-and-trophic-structure) determines who is exposed. The US Environmental Protection Agency, drawing on a 1997 Arctic Monitoring and Assessment Programme report, describes caribou in Canada's Northwest Territories carrying up to ten times the PCB concentration of the lichen they grazed, and wolves feeding on those caribou carrying nearly sixty times the lichen level. That is two steps.

For people the dominant route is the same one. The World Health Organization puts more than 90 per cent of human dioxin exposure in food — mainly meat and dairy, fish and shellfish — and estimates a half-life of 7 to 11 years for dioxins in the body, which is why exposure integrates over decades rather than tracking this year's emissions.

## Whether listing works, and what confuses the answer

The clearest national-scale evidence concerns unintentional byproducts. The EPA reports a decline of more than 85 per cent in total dioxin and furan releases from known US industrial sources after 1987, the combined result of regulation and industry action. Byproducts are an instructive case because there is no product to ban: the control has to operate on combustion and process conditions, which is exactly what Annex C of the Convention addresses, as distinct from Annex A elimination and Annex B restriction.

Long-run environmental trends are harder to read, and the honest account of why is instructive. An assessment associated with the Arctic Monitoring and Assessment Programme, [published in 2022 on climate influences on Arctic contaminant time series](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36196982/), starts from the premise that these time series are the instrument for judging whether chemicals regulation is working — on the assumption that they track changes in primary emissions. That assumption is now imperfect. The authors identify correlations between contaminant trends in freshwater and marine biota and physical variables including oscillation indices, sea-ice cover, temperature and precipitation, while noting that the mechanisms behind those correlations remain poorly understood. Biological data show shifts in diet and trophic level in some seabirds and polar bears, which changes exposure independently of emissions. Including climate variables in the trend analysis produced small deviations in some declining series but generally did not reverse the direction of the trend. The effects appear more pronounced in recent years, which suggests the confounding is growing rather than shrinking — a reason to read Arctic contaminant series alongside [cryosphere indicators](/en/ecology/climate-change/cryosphere-indicators-glaciers-sea-ice) rather than on their own.

## Where the screening triad strains: PFAS

The bioaccumulation test assumes fat. A log Kow above 5 is a proxy for partitioning into lipid, and it works for organochlorines. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances are surfactants — a fluorinated tail with a polar head — and they bind to serum proteins, accumulating in blood, liver and kidney rather than in adipose tissue. The standard lipophilicity proxy under-reads them, so the case for listing has rested on measured levels and monitoring data rather than on the tidy partition-coefficient route.

The Convention has listed them anyway, one family at a time: PFOS in Annex B in 2009, PFOA in Annex A in 2019, PFHxS in 2022, and long-chain perfluorocarboxylic acids in 2025 alongside medium-chain chlorinated paraffins and chlorpyrifos. Exposure limits are a different matter, and they are unresolved. The EPA's 2024 drinking-water regulation set maximum contaminant level goals of zero for PFOA and PFOS — a formal statement that no threshold is assumed — with enforceable limits of 4.0 parts per trillion, 10 ppt each for PFHxS, PFNA and HFPO-DA, and a hazard index of 1 for mixtures, with initial monitoring due by 2027 and treatment by 2029. In May 2026 the agency proposed keeping the PFOA and PFOS limits while allowing systems to request until 2031, and separately proposed rescinding the regulations for the other four. Any account that presents PFAS drinking-water limits as settled is describing a moment, not a rule.

What a listing does not do is worth stating plainly. It does not remove what is already in soil, sediment and tissue; for compounds with half-lives measured in months to years in the environment and years in the body, the exposure that a ban prevents is separated by a long interval from the exposure that people currently carry. The chemicals whose contaminant-carrying role is still argued about — including the [open question of microplastics as vectors](/en/ecology/pollution/microplastics-evidence-and-uncertainty) — sit in a different evidentiary position entirely, and the contrast between the two literatures is a useful calibration for how much a screening threshold can be asked to carry.
