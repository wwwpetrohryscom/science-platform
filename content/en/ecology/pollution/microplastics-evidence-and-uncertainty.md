Two claims about microplastics are routinely made in the same breath and they do not rest on remotely comparable evidence. The first is that the particles are present — in surface water, sediment, soil, air, tap water and bottled water. That claim rests on sampling across thousands of stations and study sites, and it is not seriously disputed. The second is that this presence is causing measurable harm at the concentrations found in the field. That claim is not in the same evidential state, and the gap between the two is mostly a gap in methods rather than a gap in nature. Reading the literature well means keeping the two apart, which is a specific instance of the general problem of separating exposure from effect described in the [source, pathway and receptor account of pollution](/en/ecology/pollution/environmental-pollution-explained).

## Defined by size, not by substance

The working definition is dimensional. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration describes microplastics as plastic pieces smaller than five millimetres, and distinguishes fragments produced by the breakdown of larger debris from particles manufactured small in the first place, such as the microbeads that appeared in cleansers and toothpastes around half a century ago. Nothing in that definition says anything about polymer, density, shape, additive load or surface chemistry.

This matters more than it sounds. A polyethylene fragment, a polyester fibre shed in a wash cycle and a tyre-wear particle are all microplastics, and they differ in buoyancy, in what they adsorb, in how an animal handles them and in whether a given sampling method will catch them at all. Aggregating them into one count is defensible for tracking a waste stream and hazardous for inferring a biological effect. The upper bound of the class is a round number chosen by convention; the lower bound is not set by biology at all but by whatever the instrument can resolve, which is why the sub-micrometre fraction — the one most likely to cross membranes — is the fraction least represented in occurrence data.

## What a particle count actually depends on

Almost every decision in a microplastic survey moves the answer. Net mesh size determines whether thin fibres are retained or pass through. Density separation determines which polymers float into the analysed fraction. Whether particles are confirmed spectroscopically or sorted visually determines how many non-plastic fragments are counted as plastic and how many transparent films are missed. And because the laboratory air, the analyst's clothing and the reagents all shed fibres, results are only interpretable when procedural blanks are run and reported. A review of analytical approaches for microplastics in wastewater treatment concludes that the absence of standardised methods limits both the accuracy of individual assessments and any comparison between studies — the same conclusion NOAA reaches when it notes that researchers are still working towards methods that would allow global comparison of released quantities.

The scale of the resulting uncertainty is visible inside a single well-constructed synthesis. A 2023 study pooling [11,777 sampling stations from 1979 to 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281596) estimated the standing stock of small floating plastics in the ocean surface layer at 82 to 358 trillion particles, weighing 1.1 to 4.9 million tonnes. That is a factor of more than four between the low and high bounds of one estimate built with one method — before any comparison with an older estimate using different stations and a different model. The same analysis found no detectable trend before 1990, a fluctuating but flat period to about 2005, and a rapid rise since; the trend is the more robust output, and the absolute number the more fragile one. The wider marine picture, including the larger debris that feeds this fraction, is taken up in the article on [plastic in the ocean](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-plastic-pollution-evidence).

## The concentration mismatch

The most consequential finding in the effects literature is not about a particular endpoint. It is about a mismatch of scale. A systematic review published in 2018 in *Environmental Toxicology and Chemistry* assessed the weight of evidence for microplastics causing environmental harm and found that fragments and fibres dominate field samples, with beads a small minority, and that measured environmental concentrations sit orders of magnitude below the concentrations at which laboratory studies report effects on biochemistry, feeding, reproduction, growth, tissue inflammation and mortality. The same review found the evidence weak for the idea that microplastics act as an important vector delivering hydrophobic organic compounds into organisms — a hypothesis worth taking seriously because the compounds in question include several [persistent organic pollutants that biomagnify on their own](/en/ecology/pollution/persistent-organic-pollutants), which means a vector effect would have to be shown to add something to a transfer route that already exists through diet.

The regulatory reading of the human-exposure evidence points the same way and is expressed with the same care. When the World Health Organization examined microplastics in drinking water in 2019, it concluded that they do not appear to pose a health risk at current levels, while calling the evidence limited; it noted that particles above 150 micrometres are unlikely to be absorbed, that uptake of smaller particles is expected to be limited but poorly quantified, and that wastewater treatment can remove upwards of 90 per cent of particles. The European Environment Agency, reporting an estimated annual human intake of between 70,000 and over 120,000 particles — mostly inhaled rather than eaten — states in the same passage that exposure through drinking water in Europe is assumed to be low, and that there is not enough evidence to be sure. An assumption flagged as an assumption is the correct way to publish that number.

## What "no evidence of harm" is not

None of the above establishes that microplastics are harmless, and the 2018 review is explicit about why its own conclusion is provisional. The particle types, size ranges and concentrations used in laboratory tests do not match those measured in the field; several environmental compartments have barely been studied; and the fraction that toxicological reasoning would flag as most likely to matter — the smallest particles, which can cross biological barriers rather than pass through a gut — is precisely the fraction that routine spectroscopic identification cannot yet quantify at environmental levels. A detection limit is not a biological threshold, a point that recurs wherever measurement capability sets the shape of the evidence, as it does in [the limits of remote sensing](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty).

The honest summary is therefore asymmetric in a way that resists both available headlines. Current evidence does not support the claim that microplastics at measured environmental concentrations are driving population-level ecological harm. It also does not support the claim that they are safe, because the exposure metric is unstandardised, the most biologically relevant size fraction is under-measured, and the chronic low-dose designs that would settle the question are scarce.

## What would move the argument

Four things would change the evidential position more than another thousand occurrence papers. Certified reference materials and shared spectral libraries, so that two laboratories analysing the same sediment report the same number. Agreed reporting units, since particle counts and mass concentrations are not interconvertible without a size distribution that most studies do not publish. Effects experiments run at environmentally realistic concentrations with field-representative particle shapes and polymers, rather than with spherical beads at doses chosen to produce a response. And routine measurement of the sub-micrometre fraction, which requires methods that are still maturing. Until then, monitoring programmes are measuring a proxy — a fact that applies to any indicator, as the discussion of [what a water-quality measurement represents](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained) sets out in a different context.

Policy is not waiting for that resolution, and does not have to. The EU zero-pollution action plan targets a 30 per cent reduction in microplastics released to the environment and a halving of plastic litter at sea, on the reasoning that reducing an input with a very long environmental residence time is defensible under uncertainty about its effects. That is a precautionary judgement rather than an evidential one, and it is clearer for being described as such.
