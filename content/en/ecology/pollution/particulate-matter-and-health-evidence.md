The exposure metric at the centre of modern air pollution epidemiology is a size class rather than a chemical: PM2.5 is whatever fraction of airborne material behaves, aerodynamically, as though it were 2.5 micrometres across or less. A microgram of it can be sea salt, ammonium nitrate, road dust, diesel soot or condensed wood smoke, and a filter weighing treats all of them identically. That crudeness is precisely why the evidence base is as large as it is — every network in the world can measure the same quantity the same way — and it is also where the remaining questions come from.

Where this fits in the wider machinery of exposure limits is set out in [the source, pathway and receptor framework](/en/ecology/pollution/environmental-pollution-explained); what follows is the epidemiological evidence itself, and what it can and cannot support.

## A definition by behaviour, not chemistry

The US Environmental Protection Agency defines PM10 as "inhalable particles, with diameters that are generally 10 micrometers and smaller" and PM2.5 as "fine inhalable particles, with diameters that are generally 2.5 micrometers and smaller". For scale, a human hair is roughly 70 µm across — about thirty times wider than the largest particle in the fine fraction.

Some of this material is emitted directly, from construction, unpaved roads, fields, stacks and fires. A large share is not emitted at all but forms in the atmosphere when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides react to produce sulfate and nitrate aerosol, usually with ammonia supplying the counter-ion. That secondary route is why agricultural ammonia is an air quality problem as well as a nutrient one, a link followed in [the reactive nitrogen cascade](/en/ecology/pollution/nitrogen-pollution-and-eutrophication).

The operational consequence is that the exposure metric is a mass concentration of a chemically heterogeneous mixture whose composition varies with place, season and source mix. Every epidemiological estimate below is an estimate for whatever that mixture happened to be.

## Why cohorts carry the weight

Exposure cannot be randomised, so the design has to do the work that randomisation would otherwise do. Early comparisons of mortality across metropolitan areas were criticised, fairly, for not controlling smoking at the individual level — a variable that both differs between cities and dwarfs air pollution in its effect on any individual.

The prospective cohort answered that criticism. Following 8,111 adults in [six US cities](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8179653/) for fourteen to sixteen years with individual risk-factor data, mortality was indeed most strongly associated with cigarette smoking; after adjusting for smoking and other individual risks, the mortality rate ratio between the most and least polluted city was 1.26, with a confidence interval of 1.08 to 1.47. The association survived the control that had been demanded of it.

What has accumulated since is scale and independence. An open cohort of every Medicare beneficiary in the continental United States — 60,925,443 people and 460,310,521 person-years over 2000 to 2012 — found that a 10 µg/m³ increase in annual PM2.5 was associated with 7.3% higher all-cause mortality (95% CI 7.1 to 7.5), with ozone contributing a separate 1.1% per 10 ppb in the same two-pollutant model. In Europe, eight cohorts pooled under the ELAPSE project followed 325,367 adults for an average of 19.5 years, recording 47,131 deaths; a 5 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 was associated with 13% more natural-cause deaths (10.6 to 15.5), and a 10 µg/m³ increase in nitrogen dioxide with 8.6% (7.0 to 10.2).

Different continents, different exposure models, different confounder sets, consistent direction and comparable magnitude. That is what makes this the strongest body of evidence in environmental health — not any single study.

## The curve does not flatten at the bottom

Both large studies were designed to look below the prevailing standards, and both found associations there. That much is not in serious dispute. The awkward finding is the shape.

Restricting the Medicare analysis to person-years below 12 µg/m³ of PM2.5 and 50 ppb of ozone did not weaken the particle coefficient: it rose, to 13.6% per 10 µg/m³ (13.1 to 14.1), while ozone stayed at 1.0%. Restricting the European pooled analysis to participants below the same 12 µg/m³ raised the estimate from 13% to 29.6% per 5 µg/m³ — but with a confidence interval running from 14.0 to 47.4, an interval wide enough that the point estimate should not be over-read.

A threshold model predicts the opposite: the slope should approach zero as concentrations fall towards the threshold. Steeper-at-the-bottom is compatible with at least four explanations, and current evidence does not cleanly separate them.

- The concentration–response relationship may genuinely be supralinear, with each additional microgram doing more harm in clean air than in dirty air.
- Exposure misclassification usually biases estimates towards the null, and it may be smaller in low-concentration areas where spatial gradients are gentler and prediction models perform better, so the low-exposure slope may simply be less attenuated.
- Residual confounding by area characteristics that correlate with clean air — income, housing, healthcare access — is harder to rule out in a restricted subset than in the full range.
- The low-exposure subsets are not random samples of the cohorts; they are particular places and particular people.

What the literature supports is a clear statement about the absence of an identified threshold, and a much weaker one about functional form. The distinction matters for policy, because burden estimates are far more sensitive to the assumed shape at low concentrations than to the well-measured slope in the middle of the range.

## What an attributable-death figure actually is

The World Health Organization estimates that ambient air pollution caused 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide in 2019, and that in the same year 99% of the world's population lived where its guideline levels were not met. Those numbers are widely quoted and rarely unpacked.

An attributable-burden estimate is a product of four modelled quantities: a global exposure surface built from satellite retrievals, chemical transport models and ground monitors; a concentration–response function transferred from cohorts to populations that were never studied; baseline disease rates by cause, age and country; and gridded population. Each factor carries its own error, and the errors compound multiplicatively rather than cancelling.

More importantly, the output is a counterfactual. It is the number of deaths that would not have occurred had exposure been at a specified alternative level — not a count of deaths certified as caused by air pollution. Change the counterfactual and the total changes. WHO notes separately that achieving its first interim target of 35 µg/m³ would save around 300,000 deaths a year worldwide — far below the headline total, because that calculation prices a partial improvement rather than full attainment of the guideline. A burden number without its counterfactual is uninterpretable, which is a specific case of the general problem examined in the insight on [uncertainty lost between dataset and headline](/en/insight/uncertainty-lost-between-dataset-and-headline).

## Ultrafine particles, and the honest gap

Particles below about 0.1 µm dominate particle number in most urban air and contribute almost nothing to particle mass. A mass-based metric is therefore close to blind to them, and the monitoring networks described in [what the air quality index reports](/en/ecology/pollution/air-quality-measurement-and-standards) do not routinely count them.

The World Health Organization's 2021 revision addressed this by not setting a number. Ultrafine particles and black carbon were included through good-practice statements rather than quantitative guideline levels, because the evidence base was not judged sufficient to support a value. That is a statement about the state of the evidence, not a finding that these fractions are benign — and the two are constantly conflated in reporting.

Two further questions stay open. Whether composition modifies toxicity per unit mass is not resolved: it is not established that a microgram of wildfire smoke and a microgram of traffic-derived particulate carry the same risk, and the standards implicitly assume they do. And separating the effect of particles from that of co-varying pollutants — nitrogen dioxide in particular, since both track combustion — remains difficult in observational designs, which is why multi-pollutant models and the comprehensive evidence synthesis collected in the EPA's Integrated Science Assessment carry more interpretive weight than any individual coefficient.
