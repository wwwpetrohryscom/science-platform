A portion of seafood can contain more arsenic, measured as total arsenic, than a glass of water that would be considered dangerous to drink. Both numbers are correct and they are not comparable, because the arsenic is in different chemical forms. The World Health Organization draws the line explicitly: inorganic arsenic compounds, the kind dissolved in groundwater, are highly toxic, while the organic arsenic that dominates in fish and shellfish is far less harmful. This is the general rule for the elements usually grouped as heavy metals, and it is the reason a monitoring number reported as milligrams of element per kilogram of matrix answers a narrower question than it appears to. What that number belongs to — the source, the pathway or the receptor — is worked out in the [framework this cluster is built around](/en/ecology/pollution/environmental-pollution-explained); what follows is about why the element alone is insufficient.

The grouping itself is loose. It is organised by density and by a shared history of industrial use rather than by chemistry, and arsenic, its most-studied member in drinking water, is a metalloid rather than a metal at all.

## Arsenic: mostly geological, and unevenly distributed

Arsenic in drinking water is largely a natural-source problem. Where aquifers pass through arsenic-bearing sediments under the right geochemical conditions, the element dissolves into groundwater that is otherwise clean, which is why the affected populations are so specific to particular hydrogeological settings — the WHO lists Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Chile, China, India, Mexico, Pakistan, the United States and Vietnam among the significantly affected. The connection between abstraction depth and water chemistry is one reason [groundwater as a resource](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion) cannot be assessed on quantity alone.

The guideline value is 10 micrograms per litre, and the WHO labels it provisional — an unusual and honest piece of drafting, acknowledging that the number reflects what treatment can practically achieve as well as what toxicology suggests. An estimated 140 million people in at least 70 countries drink water above it, with statistical modelling of groundwater putting the exposed population somewhere between 94 and 220 million. The clearest documented health effects come from regions where concentrations exceed 50 to 100 µg/L, and characteristic skin lesions typically appear only after roughly five years of exposure — a latency that makes early detection of a contaminated supply a laboratory problem rather than a clinical one.

## Mercury: the toxic form is manufactured on site

Mercury enters the environment as elemental metal from combustion and mining, and as inorganic compounds from industry. Neither of those is the form that causes the classic exposures. Methylmercury is produced in the environment by anaerobic bacteria, and the genetics of that conversion are now specific: a [two-gene cluster, hgcA and hgcB](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1230667), is required for methylation in the bacteria where it has been tested, and deleting either gene abolishes it. Orthologues of those genes are present in confirmed methylating bacteria and archaea and absent in non-methylators, which makes the capacity a discrete microbial trait rather than a general property of sediment.

The consequence for exposure is severe because methylmercury then behaves as a biomagnifying compound. The US Geological Survey puts methylmercury concentrations in predatory fish at typically more than a million times the concentration in the water those fish live in. The transfer works the same way as it does for the [organochlorines that accumulate through food chains](/en/ecology/pollution/persistent-organic-pollutants), and it depends on the same property of a [biogeochemical cycle](/en/glossary/biogeochemical-cycle): a step that concentrates rather than dilutes. Among subsistence fishing populations the WHO reports between 1.5 and 17 children per thousand showing cognitive effects attributed to mercury in fish, a range wide enough to be informative mainly about how much the answer depends on which population is studied.

The Minamata Convention, adopted in 2013, targets supply and products — an amendment agreed in 2023 prohibits the manufacture, import or export of certain mercury-added products after 2025, and the WHO holds a target of 90 per cent of countries implementing measures to phase down dental amalgam by 2030. It is worth being precise about what that chain of control implies. The treaty regulates commerce; methylation happens in anoxic sediments and wetlands well downstream of any product decision; and the exposure that matters arrives through fish. Each link has to be verified by its own monitoring, and a reduction at the first does not license an assumption about the last.

## Lead: what happened when the threshold was looked for

Lead is the case where a standard regulatory assumption was abandoned under evidence. Toxicology conventionally seeks a threshold — a dose below which no effect is observed — and for lead, successive studies kept finding effects at lower concentrations than the previous ones. The WHO's current position states that no level of exposure to lead is known to be without harmful effects, and that blood lead concentrations as low as 3.5 µg/dL may be associated with decreased intelligence in children. The EPA describes the same figure as the US reference value, defined as the level above which a child has more lead in their blood than most children — a statistical marker, explicitly not a safety threshold, and a fair description of what is left when the threshold model is discarded.

The scale attached to that is large. The WHO attributes more than 3.5 million deaths globally to lead exposure in 2023, chiefly through cardiovascular effects, along with more than 71.5 million years lost to disability. Children absorb up to four to five times as much lead as adults from an ingested dose. As of January 2024, 48 per cent of countries had legally binding controls on lead paint. And because lead does not degrade, historical deposition in soil and dust remains a live pathway long after the emitting activity ends, which is the sense in which a persistent metal differs from a persistent organic compound: the organic molecule can eventually be broken apart, and the element cannot be.

## Cadmium: slow, dietary, and hard to see

Cadmium accumulates over years rather than acting acutely. The WHO identifies the kidneys, the skeletal system and the respiratory system as the principal targets, classifies it as a human carcinogen, and puts contaminated food as the main route of exposure for the general population, with molluscs and crustaceans accumulating notably more than vegetables, cereals and starchy roots. Tobacco smoke is a further significant route, and cadmium travels long distances from its emission source by atmospheric transfer, so agricultural soil far from any smelter is not automatically clean. Because the portion of soil cadmium available for uptake is not the same quantity as the total present, cadmium is the clearest case where the pollution question and the [soil fertility question](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-availability-and-soil-fertility) turn out to be one question asked from two directions.

## What a total-metal measurement cannot resolve

| Element | Form that dominates the risk | Principal route to people |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arsenic | Inorganic arsenic in solution; organic forms in seafood are much less harmful | Drinking water from affected aquifers |
| Mercury | Methylmercury, generated by anaerobic bacteria in the environment | Fish, concentrated by biomagnification |
| Lead | No form treated as having a safe threshold | Dust, soil, paint, water and contaminated products |
| Cadmium | Bioavailable cadmium taken up into crops and tissue | Food, and inhalation from tobacco smoke |

Routine environmental monitoring rarely produces the column in the middle. A standard analysis digests a sample completely and reports total element, because that measurement is cheap, reproducible and comparable across laboratories. Speciation analysis — separating inorganic from organic arsenic, or methylmercury from inorganic mercury — requires different chemistry, costs more, and is applied selectively. The intermediate techniques used to estimate how much of a soil metal is actually available to organisms are operationally defined: a sequential extraction reports what a particular reagent sequence released, which is a category created by the method rather than one found in the soil.

The practical consequence is that guideline values expressed in milligrams per kilogram of soil, or micrograms per litre of water, encode an assumption about form and availability that the measurement itself does not test. That assumption is usually conservative and often reasonable. It is still an assumption, and where it fails — an inert mineral phase treated as bioavailable, or a highly available form averaged into a low total — it fails in whichever direction the local chemistry happens to push it.
