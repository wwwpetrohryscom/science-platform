A single number has organised the discussion of how hot [a changing climate](/en/ecology/climate-change/what-is-climate-change) can make the world before parts of it become uninhabitable: a wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C. It is worth knowing where that number came from, because its origin explains both why it was useful and why it has moved.

## Where 35 came from

The threshold was proposed in 2010 by Sherwood and Huber as an upper adaptability limit, derived from biophysical principles rather than from human testing. The argument is clean. A body at 35 °C skin temperature cannot lose heat to air whose wet-bulb temperature is also 35 °C, because evaporation — the only remaining route once conduction, convection and radiation have failed — requires a gradient. Above that point, metabolic heat has nowhere to go and core temperature rises regardless of behaviour.

That is a limit on physics, and as a limit on physics it is correct. What it is not is a measurement of people.

## What the experiments found

A 2024 paper in *Temperature* reviews what happened when the experiment was done. The Penn State HEAT project exposed participants to controlled combinations of temperature and humidity and identified the conditions at which core temperature began to rise uncontrollably. The paper reports that researchers "found… wet-bulb temperature (Twb) thresholds for heat stress compensability were closer to 30.6°C in warm-humid conditions", with "a linear decrease across hotter and drier environments."

Thirty point six is more than four degrees below thirty-five, and in an exponential humidity context that is a large difference in how much of the world is affected and how soon.

The reasons the empirical limit is lower are specific, and each is a place where the idealisation was optimistic:

**Skin is warmer than the model assumed.** Measured skin temperatures during the trials were 36–38.5 °C rather than the theoretical 35 °C, which reduces the gradient available for heat transfer.

**Sweating is regulated, not unlimited.** The biophysical argument assumes evaporation proceeds at whatever rate the gradient allows. Real sweat rates are physiologically controlled and lower.

**Clothing impedes evaporation.** Any barrier between skin and air reduces the effective evaporative surface.

**Participants were unacclimatised.** Acclimatisation raises tolerance, so the measured threshold is not the highest achievable — but neither is the theoretical one representative of an ordinary population.

## What follows for projections

A 2023 study in *Science Advances* took the empirical threshold rather than the theoretical one and ran it through observations and model projections. Its abstract sets out the shift: "Past modeling efforts have investigated the 35°C wet-bulb threshold, proposed as a theoretical upper limit to survivability taking into account physiological and behavioral adaptation. Here, we conduct an extreme value theory analysis of weather station observations and climate model projections to investigate the emergence of an empirically supported heat compensability limit."

The result is that under 1–3.5 °C of warming above preindustrial levels, parts of every continent except Antarctica see rapid increases in both the frequency and the geographic extent of noncompensable heat, including in populations with no prior exposure to such conditions.

Lowering a threshold by four degrees does not lower the projected impact proportionally. Because the distribution of extreme wet-bulb temperatures falls off steeply, a modest reduction in the threshold moves a large amount of area and population across it.

## What this case shows about thresholds

Three points generalise beyond heat.

**A physical bound is not a biological one.** The 35 °C figure is the correct answer to the question "at what point is heat loss thermodynamically impossible for an idealised body". Human tolerance fails earlier, for reasons that are entirely mundane.

**Simple thresholds get load-bearing fast.** A number that is easy to compute from standard meteorological variables spreads through the literature and into policy, and the assumptions behind it do not travel with it.

**Counting the consequence is its own problem.** How many people heat has already killed depends on which method is used to count, and the two available methods differ by a large factor — the subject of [heat mortality and what the records count](/en/ecology/climate-change/heat-mortality-and-what-the-records-count).

**Empirical work can move a number without overturning the framework.** Nothing in the revision undermines the wet-bulb approach, and [why heat and humidity combine](/en/physics/climate-physics/humidity-and-why-heat-and-moisture-combine) is unchanged. What changed is a parameter, and the change is large.
