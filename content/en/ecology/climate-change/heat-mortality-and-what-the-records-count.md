Among the impacts attributed to [climate change](/en/ecology/climate-change/what-is-climate-change), heat mortality has the most concrete-looking figures. The EPA's heat-island materials record "an average of 702 heat deaths per year" in the United States over 2004 to 2018. It is a real number, produced by a defined procedure, and it is not an estimate of how many people heat kills. The gap between those two statements is the subject here, and it is a good case study in what a count measures.

## What a certificate records

A death certificate names an immediate cause and contributing conditions. Heat appears when a clinician identifies it — most reliably in a case of heat stroke, where the presentation is unambiguous and the history is known.

Most heat-related deaths do not look like that. Heat precipitates cardiovascular and respiratory events in people who already have those conditions, and the certificate records the cardiac or respiratory event. It also acts through dehydration, renal injury, and medication interactions, none of which is coded as heat. A person who dies of a heart attack during a heatwave is recorded as dying of a heart attack, and that record is correct.

So a certificate-based count is not undercounting through error. It is counting a narrower thing than the phrase "heat deaths" suggests, and it does so consistently.

## The alternative, and its own limits

The method that captures the wider effect is statistical rather than clinical. Excess mortality analysis compares deaths during a hot period against a baseline expectation built from the same population in comparable periods, and attributes the difference to the heat.

This recovers the deaths a certificate-based count misses, and it introduces problems of its own.

**The baseline is a model.** Expected mortality depends on how seasonality, long-term trend and epidemic activity are handled, and different reasonable choices give different excesses.

**Displacement is hard to separate.** Some of the excess is mortality brought forward by days or weeks in people who were already near death. Whether that is counted depends on the window used, and the answer changes with the window.

**It is a population estimate, not a list.** Excess mortality cannot say which deaths were heat deaths, only how many more occurred than expected. That is the right tool for a policy question and the wrong one for a coroner.

The two methods therefore answer different questions and produce numbers that differ by a large factor, and neither is wrong.

## Why this matters for the trend, not just the level

An undercount that is stable does not distort a trend. This one is not obviously stable: awareness of heat as a cause has increased, coding practice has changed, and the population at risk has aged. A rising certificate-based count could reflect rising heat, rising recognition, or an ageing population, and separating those requires the same care as any other [attribution problem](/en/ecology/climate-change/extreme-weather-attribution-basics).

The tolerance side compounds it. The threshold beyond which the body cannot compensate is not a single number and varies with age, medication and acclimatisation, as the [physiology of heat stress](/en/biology/physiology/heat-stress-and-thermoregulatory-limits) sets out. A population whose composition is shifting toward the vulnerable tail experiences a given heat event differently from the same population twenty years earlier.

## How to read a heat mortality figure

Three questions settle most of the ambiguity.

**Which method?** Certificate-based counts and excess mortality estimates are not comparable and should never be presented together without saying so.

**Which population and period?** Heat mortality is concentrated in specific ages, specific health conditions and specific housing, and a national annual figure averages over all of them.

**What baseline?** For an excess estimate, the baseline is where most of the methodological choice lives, and a figure quoted without it cannot be checked.
