Nearly every quantitative statement about future warming inherits its uncertainty from a small set of assessed parameters, and two of them carry most of the load. They are routinely used as though they were the same quantity. They are not: the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report puts one at 3°C and the other at 1.8°C, and the difference is not a matter of rounding but of which experiment is being described. Anyone working from the [mechanism and evidence behind modern warming](/en/ecology/climate-change/what-is-climate-change) will meet both figures within a page or two of each other, usually without being told that they answer different questions.

## Two numbers, two idealised experiments

**Equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS)** is the change in global surface air temperature at equilibrium under a sustained doubling of atmospheric CO₂ from a pre-industrial reference state. AR6 Chapter 7 defines equilibrium strictly: a steady state in which the top-of-atmosphere imbalance averages to zero over a multi-century period. It also fences the definition — the ECS used in AR6 excludes the long-term response of the ice sheets, which needs millennia, while including every other feedback. Because ECS is defined against a concentration change rather than an emission, nothing that alters how much CO₂ stays airborne can change its value.

**Transient climate response (TCR)** is the warming reached at the moment of doubling in an experiment where CO₂ rises at 1% per year — year 70. TCR is always smaller than ECS, because at that moment the ocean is still absorbing energy that would eventually have gone into surface warming. It is also better constrained: the ocean heat uptake term that dominates the ECS denominator drops out.

A third quantity sits between them and causes most of the confusion in secondary summaries. **Effective ECS** is the equilibrium warming that would occur *if the present-day strength of the feedbacks persisted all the way to equilibrium*. It is what an energy-budget calculation on the observational record actually returns, and it is systematically lower than ECS.

| Quantity | The experiment it describes | AR6 best estimate | AR6 likely range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ECS | sustained 2×CO₂, run to a multi-century steady state | 3°C | 2.5–4°C |
| TCR | CO₂ rising 1% per year, measured at doubling | 1.8°C | 1.4–2.2°C |
| Effective ECS | equilibrium implied if today's feedback strength held | 2.5°C from the historical energy budget | 1.6–4.8°C, a calculated interval rather than one of AR6's assessed ranges |

## Where 3°C comes from: the feedbacks, added up

Sensitivity is a ratio. The numerator is the [radiative forcing](/en/glossary/radiative-forcing) from doubled CO₂, assessed in AR6 at 3.93 ± 0.47 W/m² once tropospheric adjustments are included. The denominator is the net feedback parameter α — how strongly the planet increases its radiation to space per degree of surface warming.

AR6 assesses α by summing the individual terms. The Planck response, the base cooling of a warmer body, contributes −3.22 W/m² per °C. Working against it: the combined [water-vapour and lapse-rate feedback](/en/ecology/earth-systems/water-vapor-feedback-explained) at +1.30, surface albedo at +0.35, and clouds at +0.42. Biogeophysical and non-CO₂ biogeochemical processes contribute −0.01, effectively nothing at the central estimate but not nothing in the uncertainty. The sum is −1.16 W/m² per °C, with a very likely range of −1.81 to −0.51.

Divide 3.93 by 1.16 and the process-based central estimate is 3.4°C. That arithmetic also explains the shape of the uncertainty. Because α sits in the denominator and its range reaches to −0.51, small changes at the weak-feedback end produce very large changes in sensitivity, which is why every assessed range for ECS has a long upper tail and a firm floor. The cloud term is the largest contributor to that spread — 0.42 with a very likely range of −0.10 to +0.94 — and the reason [cloud response remains the dominant open problem](/en/physics/climate-physics/convection-and-cloud-formation) in this part of climate physics. AR6 nonetheless assesses a net negative cloud feedback as very unlikely, a firmer statement than AR5 could make. The wider mechanics of how such terms are defined are set out in the article on [climate feedback mechanisms](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-feedback-mechanisms).

## Four lines of evidence, and why combining them narrowed the range

AR5 assessed a likely ECS range of 1.5°C to 4.5°C and declined to give a best estimate. AR6 gives 2.5°C to 4°C with a best estimate of 3°C. Nothing about the physical system changed between the two reports; the method did. AR6 assessed four largely independent lines of evidence and then combined them, and — a genuine break with earlier reports — excluded direct estimates from Earth system models from the assessed ranges altogether.

| Line of evidence | Central value | Very likely range or limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Process understanding (feedbacks summed) | 3.4°C | 2.1–7.7°C |
| Warming over the instrumental record | 2.5–3.5°C | greater than 1.8°C |
| Paleoclimates | 3.3–3.4°C | greater than 1.5°C |
| Emergent constraints | 2.4–3.3°C | 1.5–5.0°C |
| Combined assessment | 3°C | 2.0–5.0°C |

The instrumental and paleoclimate rows are one-sided limits rather than two-tailed ranges, which is why they look oddly permissive on their own. The combination logic is straightforward: if two independent lines each assign low probability to ECS below 2°C, the joint probability is lower than either. AR6 notes that removing emergent constraints entirely and combining only the other three still puts the 95th percentile near 5°C — the upper bound does not rest on the newest and least established method.

## Why the thermometer record on its own reads low

The historical energy budget is the most intuitive constraint and the most frequently misused. It needs three observed quantities: the warming since pre-industrial, the change in radiative forcing that caused it, and the energy still accumulating. AR6 uses a warming of 1.03 ± 0.20°C between 1850–1900 and 2006–2019, a forcing change of 2.20 W/m² with a range of 1.53 to 2.91, and an energy imbalance change of 0.59 ± 0.35 W/m². Put through the standard energy-balance relation, those give a TCR of 1.9°C (1.3 to 2.7) and an *effective* ECS of 2.5°C (1.6 to 4.8).

That 2.5°C is the number often quoted as evidence that sensitivity is lower than the assessment claims. It is not a competing estimate of ECS; it is a different quantity. The traditional framework assumes the feedback strength stays constant, and there is now strong evidence it does not. Measured against the pattern projected for the near-equilibrium response to CO₂, warming so far has run relatively hot in key damping regions — the western tropical Pacific above all — and relatively cool in key amplifying ones, notably the eastern tropical Pacific and the Southern Ocean. As the warming pattern evolves towards the equilibrium response to CO₂, the net feedback becomes less negative. This is the **pattern effect**, and AR6 assesses the associated change in the feedback parameter at somewhere between 0.0 and 1.0 W/m² per °C.

The size of the correction depends entirely on which value is used. Taking the midpoint of that range moves the estimate to 3.5°C, with a range from 1.7 all the way to 13.8. Using values derived from coupled model simulations of idealised CO₂ forcing instead gives 2.7°C, with a range of 1.7 to 5.9. The lower bound barely moves in either case, which is the substantive result: the historical record constrains the bottom of the range robustly — ECS is extremely unlikely to be below 1.6°C — and says almost nothing useful about the top.

## The model ensemble is not the assessment

The CMIP6 generation of Earth system models has, on average, higher sensitivity than CMIP5 and a wider spread, extending above the assessed very likely range. The cause has been traced largely to the cloud feedback, which is roughly 20% larger in CMIP6, driven by stronger positive low-cloud responses. Some of that change came from fixing genuine biases against satellite observations; some of the resulting high-sensitivity models are harder to reconcile with observed warming and with paleoclimate proxies.

AR6 therefore did not take the raw ensemble mean, and [a 2022 comment in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-022-01192-2) argued that the rest of the research community should follow the same practice rather than treating every [multi-model projection](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-models-projections-uncertainty) as equally weighted. The models remain indispensable — they supply the feedback decompositions and the emergent constraints — but in AR6 they are inputs to the assessment rather than the assessment itself.

## What a narrower range still does not settle

Three things are worth keeping in view. The upper bound of 5°C carries only medium confidence, because no line of evidence rules out higher values; the lower bounds carry high confidence. Feedback strength is state-dependent — evidence from models and from the glacial–interglacial proxy record agrees that α becomes less negative as the planet warms — so sensitivity is not a fixed constant of the system but a property of the climate state, and AR6 explicitly says there is insufficient evidence to quantify a likely range for how much future feedbacks will change. And because the assessed definition stops short of the ice sheets, the multi-millennial response to a doubling is larger than any ECS figure implies.

The most common misreading is more basic than any of these. ECS is not a forecast. It is the response to a specified, idealised forcing, and how much warming actually occurs depends on cumulative emissions — a separate assessed relationship, and the subject of the companion article on [remaining carbon budgets](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-budgets-and-remaining-emissions).
