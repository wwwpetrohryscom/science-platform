Every published verdict on restoration is a verdict about a comparison, and there are two available. Compare a restored site with the degraded state it came from and restoration looks strongly effective. Compare the same site with an undisturbed reference and it looks incomplete. Both comparisons are legitimate, both appear in the literature, and headline claims about restoration usually turn on which one was reported. This is the first thing to establish before any number means anything; the wider framework for judging intervention evidence is set out in the cluster's guide to [how conservation effectiveness is tested](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained).

## Three syntheses, one consistent shape

The largest synthesis efforts cover different biomes with different methods, and they converge on the same pattern: a substantial gain over degradation, and a persistent shortfall against reference conditions.

| Synthesis | Evidence base | Gain over degraded sites | Remaining gap to reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wetlands (PLOS Biology 2012) | 621 restored and created wetlands against 556 reference wetlands | Not reported on this axis | Biological structure 26 % lower, biogeochemical function 23 % lower |
| Forests (Nature Communications 2016) | 221 study landscapes worldwide | Biodiversity 15–84 % higher, vegetation structure 36–77 % higher | Biodiversity 10–26 % lower, structure 16–42 % lower |
| Terrestrial systems (Ecology Letters 2022) | 83 restoration studies | Biodiversity 20 % higher on average | 13 % below reference, with 20 % higher variability |

The variability result is the least intuitive and possibly the most useful. Restoration reduced the spread of biodiversity outcomes relative to unrestored degraded sites by about 14 per cent, yet restored sites remained more variable than reference sites, and that difference did not shrink as projects aged. Prior land use and the particular practices used appear to leave a durable signature. An average recovery ratio therefore conceals a distribution wide enough that the average is a poor predictor for any single project.

## The reference state decides the answer

Choosing a reference is a scientific decision with a policy consequence, and it is contested. Assessment work on land degradation has been explicit that the absence of consensus on baselines — and on what kinds of change even count as degradation — produces inconsistent estimates of how much land is degraded and how much has been recovered. A reference anchored to the natural state of the ecosystem is harder to define but comparable across countries at different stages of development; a reference anchored to recent condition is easier to measure and quietly forgives whatever was lost before the clock started. Neither is neutral. The general problem of choosing a [baseline condition](/en/glossary/baseline-condition) runs through every long-run ecological comparison.

The choice also interacts with the landscape. Forest restoration success in the global synthesis was driven by three things: time elapsed since restoration began, the type of prior disturbance, and landscape context. Selectively logged forests recovered readily, because they had not travelled far from the reference in the first place. Restoration in less fragmented landscapes did better than restoration in heavily fragmented ones, which places restoration outcomes downstream of [fragmentation and connectivity](/en/ecology/conservation/habitat-fragmentation-and-connectivity) rather than independent of them.

## Structure returns before function

Vegetation cover, canopy height and stem density are the first things to come back and the easiest things to measure. Nutrient cycling, hydrological behaviour and soil biological processes lag, and they are measured less often. The wetland synthesis found plant assemblages converging statistically on reference conditions after roughly three decades while absolute values stayed below reference even a century after restoration — a result compatible either with very slow recovery or with a shift to an alternative stable state that will not converge at all. The data cannot distinguish those two explanations, and that ambiguity is central rather than marginal.

Intergovernmental assessment reaches the same conclusion in plainer language: recovering vegetation cover after degradation is possible and often succeeds, but it seldom attains, within decades, pre-degradation levels of ecosystem function or of compositional biological diversity.

## Restoration and rehabilitation make different claims

The two words are frequently used as synonyms and should not be. In the assessment vocabulary, restoration is any intentional activity that initiates or accelerates recovery of an ecosystem from a degraded state, while rehabilitation refers to restoration activities that may fall short of fully returning the biotic community to its pre-degradation composition. Rehabilitation is therefore a weaker claim, not a lesser activity — recreating a functioning wetland that filters nutrients and holds water is a real result even when the plant assemblage never matches what was there before.

Reporting problems begin when a rehabilitation outcome is described using restoration language. The measurements that distinguish them are precisely the ones that get dropped when monitoring budgets tighten: composition, and function beyond the first few years.

## The recovery debt

There is a further deficit that no snapshot comparison captures. A meta-analysis drawing on 3,035 sampling plots worldwide quantified the shortfall accumulated *during* recovery, not merely the endpoint. Against reference levels, recovering systems ran annual deficits of 46–51 per cent for organism abundance, 27–33 per cent for species diversity, 32–42 per cent for carbon cycling and 31–41 per cent for nitrogen cycling. The pattern held across biomes, though not across degrading factors.

The implication is arithmetical and awkward. Even a project that eventually reaches full recovery will have run a deficit for the whole intervening period, so a hectare restored is not equivalent to a hectare never damaged, and the two cannot be traded at par. That conclusion bears directly on offsetting schemes and on the accounting behind [carbon offset markets](/en/insight/carbon-offset-outsourcing-science), where the exchange rate between damage now and repair later is the entire product.

## What a restoration target commits to

The Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits parties to ensuring that by 2030 at least 30 per cent of areas of degraded terrestrial, inland water, and marine and coastal ecosystems are *under effective restoration*. The wording is doing careful work: an area under effective restoration is one where the process has begun and is being managed, not one that has recovered. Given that the syntheses above measure residual gaps decades after intervention, a 2030 target could be fully met with essentially none of the treated area having reached reference condition. That is not a criticism of the target's design, which is honest about the timescale; it is a warning about how the indicator will be reported.

The economic case is separately robust. Assessment work across biomes estimates that the benefits of restoration exceed the costs by an average margin of about ten to one, with the caveat that this is an established-but-incomplete finding, and that in several Asian and African countries the cost of inaction on land degradation has been estimated at 3.8 to 5 times the cost of avoiding it. Degradation of the land surface is assessed as negatively affecting the well-being of at least 3.2 billion people. Prevention still dominates repair on cost grounds, which is why restoration budgets are usually argued about alongside protection budgets rather than instead of them — the allocation problem taken up in [conservation prioritisation](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-trade-offs-and-prioritisation).

## The measurements are shorter than the process

The honest limitation runs through everything above. Most of what is known about long-run recovery comes from chronosequences — sets of sites restored at different times, read as though they were one site observed repeatedly. That substitution assumes the older sites resemble what the younger sites will become, which is exactly what fails if starting conditions, prior land use or restoration technique differed systematically across decades. Genuine long time series exist for a small number of sites.

Monitoring also tends to stop when funding does, and it concentrates on the variables that are cheap to record. The result is a literature that is well populated on vegetation structure in the first few years and thin on function after ten, which is the interval in which most of the interesting divergence happens. Where restoration is combined with removal of an established invasive species, the recovery signal is further entangled with the removal's own effects, a difficulty examined in the treatment of [invasive species management](/en/ecology/conservation/invasive-species-management).
