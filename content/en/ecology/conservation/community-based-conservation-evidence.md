The hardest problem in this literature is not measuring forest cover. Satellites do that well. It is that no territory was ever assigned at random: community-held and Indigenous-held lands sit where they sit for historical, political and topographic reasons, and every one of those reasons also affects how likely the land was to be cleared. Any comparison that ignores this measures location as much as management.

That makes the field a useful stress test for [how conservation claims are evaluated generally](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained), because the same non-random placement affects state protected areas — and the size of the resulting bias has been estimated.

## How large the naive error is

The reference result comes from Costa Rica's protected-area system, evaluated with statistical matching over 1960 to 1997. Matching pairs each protected parcel with unprotected parcels that resemble it on the observable characteristics correlated with both protection and clearing, then compares only within those pairs. The matched estimate was that roughly 10 per cent of the protected forest would have been cleared had it not been protected.

The comparison with the conventional method is the point. Analyses that do not control for observable covariates correlated with both protection and clearing overestimated avoided deforestation by more than 65 per cent. The same study also tested for displacement of clearing into neighbouring unprotected forest and found spillovers negligible in that setting, which is a separate assumption that cannot be taken for granted elsewhere.

Anyone reading a headline about how much forest a designation "saved" should therefore ask which of three quantities is being reported: gross forest loss inside the boundary, the difference between inside and outside, or the difference between inside and a matched counterfactual. Only the third is an effect estimate. The same distinction determines what a forest-carbon credit is actually certifying, an issue examined in the insight on [carbon offsets and the science behind them](/en/insight/carbon-offset-outsourcing-science).

## What matched studies find on Indigenous lands

A pan-tropical analysis applied that logic at scale, estimating deforestation and degradation from 2010 to 2018 across 3.4 million km² of Indigenous Lands, 2 million km² of protected areas and 1.7 million km² of overlapping protected Indigenous areas, each against matched non-protected counterfactuals. Deforestation was reduced on Indigenous Lands relative to non-protected areas across the tropics, avoiding clearing comparably to protected areas and to the overlapping category — except in Africa, where Indigenous Lands avoided more. Degradation showed the same broad pattern.

A companion analysis using a forest landscape integrity index and land-use intensity, again after statistical matching and regression, reached a more differentiated result. Areas that were both protected and Indigenous had the highest protective effect on forest integrity and the lowest land-use intensity. But the protective effect of Indigenous Lands on integrity was lower than that of protected areas and of non-protected areas in the Americas and in Asia. A single regional average conceals that; the honest reading is that the combination of state designation and Indigenous presence performs best, and that Indigenous Lands alone perform differently in different regions.

Land titling supplies a closer approximation to an intervention with a date. In the Peruvian Amazon, where more than 1,200 Indigenous communities covering some 11 million hectares have been titled since the mid-1970s, community-level satellite data showed that titling reduced forest clearing by more than three-quarters and disturbance by roughly two-thirds — measured in a two-year window spanning the year of award and the year after. That window is the caveat as well as the finding. It is short, and it cannot say whether the effect persists once the immediate boundary-marking phase ends.

Note also that all three studies measure forest cover, extent and integrity. Forest cover is a proxy for biodiversity, not a measure of it. The distinction between clearance and thinning is developed in [degradation versus deforestation](/en/ecology/forests/forest-degradation-vs-deforestation), and a stand can retain canopy while losing most of what lived under it.

## Social outcomes and ecological outcomes move together

A global meta-analysis of 165 protected areas, drawing on 171 published studies, asked whether the social and conservation records point in the same direction. They did. Protected areas associated with positive socioeconomic outcomes were more likely to report positive conservation outcomes, and both were more likely where the area used co-management, empowered local people, reduced economic inequality, and maintained cultural and livelihood benefits.

That is an association across a heterogeneous sample of published studies, not an experiment, and the authors do not claim strict protection is never warranted. It is still the most useful available summary of a question that is usually argued from single cases in both directions, and it reframes governance from a fairness constraint into a variable that predicts ecological outcomes. Whether governance type is recorded at all is a different matter, taken up below and in the discussion of [what makes a protected area effective](/en/ecology/biodiversity/protected-area-effectiveness).

## Where the evidence is genuinely thin

Community forest management, the institutional form most often proposed as the practical vehicle for all of this, has a weaker record than its prominence suggests. A systematic review of the effectiveness literature found evidence associating it with greater tree density and basal area, but not with other indicators of global environmental benefit — and no data on local human welfare in a form amenable to meta-analysis. The reviewers named poor study design, variable reporting of methodology and context, and the absence of common indicators as the reasons synthesis was so difficult, which makes this a gap in the evidence rather than a demonstrated absence of effect. Donors had by then invested billions of dollars in sustainable forest management, of which community forest management is a major mechanism.

The recording problem compounds it. Only 3.95 per cent of terrestrial protected and conserved area coverage was reported as governed by Indigenous Peoples and local communities, with a further 11.84 per cent under shared governance, and governance assessments had been reported for just 0.22 per cent of terrestrial coverage and 0.001 per cent of marine coverage. Equitable governance is a condition written into international targets and almost entirely unmonitored in the database that reports on them.

## Two numbers that look contradictory and are not

Global figures for the extent of Indigenous lands differ by roughly a factor of two, and the discrepancy is instructive rather than embarrassing.

An aggregation of publicly available geospatial resources concluded that Indigenous Peoples manage or hold tenure rights over at least about 38 million km² across 87 countries or politically distinct areas — over a quarter of the world's land surface — intersecting about 40 per cent of terrestrial protected areas and ecologically intact landscapes. The Protected Planet Report 2024, working from what states and other authorities have reported into a protected-area database, records Indigenous and traditional territories as covering at least 13.6 per cent of global terrestrial area beyond protected and conserved areas.

The two are measuring different things: mapped management and tenure rights in one case, territories recognised and reported into a specific database in the other. A reader who treats either as the total will misread it. The gap between them is itself a finding about how much of the world's conserved land is invisible to the official accounting, and it has direct consequences for the [allocation decisions that use those datasets](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-trade-offs-and-prioritisation).

The international framework has moved ahead of the measurement. Target 22 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits parties to full, equitable, inclusive and effective representation and participation in biodiversity decision-making by Indigenous peoples and local communities, respecting their rights over lands, territories, resources and traditional knowledge. Its component indicator is the proportion of the adult population with secure tenure rights to land, measured both by legally recognised documentation and by perceived security. That indicator is a reasonable proxy for a condition the ecological studies keep identifying as decisive, and it is not yet reported at the resolution the target implies.
