The binding constraint on conservation is rarely a shortage of information about which species are in trouble. It is money, land and the number of people available to manage either — which makes the operative question not what is threatened but what to buy with the next unit of budget. Those two questions have different answers often enough that the second deserves its own methods.

Those methods form one of the more mature parts of the field's [applied evidence base](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained), largely because the problem is formally tractable: a set of sites or projects, a budget, and an objective to maximise.

## Complementarity, not the highest score

The framing paper for systematic conservation planning, published in *Nature* in 2000, assigned reserves two jobs: to represent the biodiversity of each region, and to separate that biodiversity from the processes threatening its persistence. It also recorded what existing reserve systems had actually done — contain a biased sample of biodiversity, "usually that of remote places and other areas that are unsuitable for commercial activities". Representation is a property of a set, not of a site. Two equally rich reserves holding the same species add less than a rich reserve plus a poorer one holding different species, which is why the highest-scoring sites on any single-site metric rarely form the best network.

The hotspots approach published in the same journal and year answers a different question: where, globally, are exceptional concentrations of endemic species undergoing exceptional habitat loss? Its authors reported that 44 per cent of vascular plant species and 35 per cent of species in four vertebrate groups are confined to 25 such regions covering 1.4 per cent of Earth's land surface. That is a global triage heuristic, not a site-selection algorithm, and the two have often been argued about as though they were rival answers to one question.

| Approach | What it maximises | What it needs | What it cannot see |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Threat-status ranking | Attention on the most endangered | Red List categories alone | Cost, feasibility, redundancy between sites |
| Endemism-and-loss hotspots | Coverage of irreplaceable, fast-disappearing regions | Endemism and habitat-loss data at regional scale | Within-region allocation; anything outside the filter |
| Complementarity-based planning | Features represented per unit of area or cost | Feature-by-site data and a stated target | Probability that the action actually succeeds |
| Project prioritisation with cost and success | Expected number of species secured per unit spent | Cost, benefit and success estimates per project | Values that resist being written as a benefit score |

## Cost changes the answer more than value does

A project prioritisation protocol developed for New Zealand's threatened-species work makes the trade-off explicit rather than implicit by combining cost, benefit and likelihood of management success in one ranking. Its original *Conservation Biology* analysis reported that weighting projects by species value allowed 11 of 32 projects to be funded, against 16 when species were weighted equally — the same budget, a different objective, five more or fewer species managed. The same protocol was later applied at scale in New South Wales, where management prescriptions for 368 threatened species were elicited and then ranked on cost-efficiency. The contribution is not that this removes the value judgement. It is that the judgement becomes visible and its consequences countable, which is also the argument made for treating [extinction-risk assessment](/en/ecology/conservation/species-extinction-risk-assessment) as an input to allocation rather than as the allocation itself.

Where budgets are examined rather than modelled, a different leak appears. An analysis of management plans for 2,328 listed species across three countries found that 50 per cent of proposed recovery-plan budgets were allocated to research and monitoring rather than to action, and that species with a higher proportion of their budget going to information gathering had poorer recovery outcomes. The authors are careful about direction: hard cases attract more study. But some plans had been allocating the majority of funds to information gathering for decades, which is a pattern no amount of reverse causation explains away. Information has value only when it changes a decision.

## Where protected areas actually went

The strongest empirical test of prioritisation is retrospective: given that roughly a sixth of the land surface now carries some form of protection, was it placed where it was needed?

A global analysis of protected-area location tested exactly the pattern the 2000 planning paper had described, and found it entrenched rather than corrected: neither older nor newer protected areas targeted places with high concentrations of threatened vertebrates. They were established where they conflicted least with agriculturally suitable land. The counterfactual the authors compute is the striking part: had expansion between 2004 and 2014 targeted unrepresented threatened vertebrates, more than 30 times as many species could have been represented for the same area or the same cost — 3,086 or 2,553 species depending on the criterion, against 85 actually gained.

The current picture from the World Database on Protected Areas is consistent with that history. As of August 2024, protected and conserved areas covered 17.58 per cent of terrestrial and inland waters and 8.44 per cent of marine and coastal areas. Ecological representativeness lagged well behind raw extent, with mean protection of ecologically distinct environments at 10.00 per cent, and about 8.52 per cent of land was both protected and connected. Just under a third of Key Biodiversity Areas — 32.03 per cent — had no protection at all. Management effectiveness had been assessed for 4.78 per cent of terrestrial coverage and 1.26 per cent of marine coverage. Whether a designated area functions as [an effective protected area](/en/ecology/biodiversity/protected-area-effectiveness) is therefore unknown for most of the estate, and the marine gap is the sharper of the two — a point developed in the review of [what marine protected areas have been shown to deliver](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-protected-areas-evidence).

## What 30×30 specifies, and what it does not

The most common misreading of the global area target is that it is an area target. Target 3 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework asks parties to ensure that by 2030 at least 30 per cent of terrestrial, inland water, coastal and marine areas — "especially areas of particular importance for biodiversity and ecosystem functions and services" — are effectively conserved through "ecologically representative, well-connected and equitably governed" systems of protected areas and other effective area-based conservation measures.

Four qualifiers there are conditions, not decoration: importance for biodiversity, effectiveness, representativeness, connectivity. A fifth, equitable governance, is examined in the evidence on [community-managed and Indigenous-managed areas](/en/ecology/conservation/community-based-conservation-evidence). The headline indicator for the target, however, is coverage. When a target carries several conditions and one measurable headline number, reporting drifts towards the number, and the target can be satisfied on paper by designating cheap, remote, low-conflict land — precisely the pattern the location analysis documented over the preceding decades. Reaching 30 per cent and improving outcomes are not the same achievement, and the framework's own wording is the best evidence for that.

## Triage, and the objection to it

The word triage attracts more heat than the practice. The objection is that it normalises abandoning species and that public support depends on refusing to. The reply is arithmetic: with a fixed budget, every funded project already implies unfunded ones, so the choice is not whether to rank but whether the ranking is written down and defensible.

The magnitudes make the argument concrete. Reducing the extinction risk of all globally threatened bird species by at least one Red List category was estimated at US$0.875 to $1.23 billion a year, of which about 12 per cent was then funded; extending that to non-avian species raised it to $3.41 to $4.76 billion annually, and protecting and effectively managing all sites of global conservation significance to $76.1 billion a year. At those ratios, an unstated ranking is still a ranking — it is just one nobody has to defend.

None of this decides the objective. Whether to maximise species secured, evolutionary history retained, ecosystem function, or the wellbeing of people living alongside a reserve is a value choice that no algorithm supplies, and different objectives produce different maps from identical data. Prioritisation methods are good at showing what a stated objective costs. They are silent on which objective is right, and the honest versions say so.
