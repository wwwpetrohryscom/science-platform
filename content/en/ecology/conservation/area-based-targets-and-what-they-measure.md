A conservation goal stated as a percentage of area is an unusual instrument. It names no species, no threat and no place, and it can be met by a country that has altered nothing about how the enclosed land is used. Area became the unit of global ambition because it is the one quantity that can be compiled the same way in every country, every year, from records governments already keep — which is also why the number carries more weight in reporting than the conditions attached to it. The wider frame is set out in the overview of [conservation science](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained).

The instrument has been through three revisions. Following what happens to the qualifiers across them, and what the resulting figure is actually computed from, is more informative than arguing about whether 30 per cent is the right number.

## Ten per cent, seventeen, thirty

The first quantified global target came out of the Convention on Biological Diversity in 2004. Decision VII/30 set, under goal 1.1 of its framework for evaluating progress, a target of "at least 10% of each of the world's ecological regions effectively conserved". The phrasing is worth noticing: the percentage applied to each ecological region rather than to the planet, which builds representation into the arithmetic. A country could not satisfy it by protecting more of what it had already protected.

The 2010 Aichi Biodiversity Targets dropped that structure. Target 11 asked that by 2020, "at least 17 per cent of terrestrial and inland water areas and 10 per cent of coastal and marine areas, especially areas of particular importance for biodiversity and ecosystem services, are conserved through effectively and equitably managed, ecologically representative and well-connected systems of protected areas and other effective area-based conservation measures, and integrated into the wider landscape and seascape". Representativeness survived as an adjective rather than as a denominator. The technical rationale published with the target recorded the starting point as roughly 13 per cent of land and 5 per cent of coastal areas.

Target 3 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, adopted in 2022, raised the figure to 30 per cent by 2030 and lengthened the qualifier list again: areas of particular importance for biodiversity and ecosystem functions and services, "effectively conserved and managed", "ecologically representative, well-connected and equitably governed", recognising Indigenous and traditional territories, integrated into wider landscapes and seascapes, with any sustainable use "fully consistent with conservation outcomes". Every one of those is a condition. None of them is the number in the headline.

## What the percentage is computed from

Coverage figures come from the World Database on Protected Areas and its OECM counterpart, compiled by UNEP-WCMC and IUCN from what governments report about their own law. The published method is specific about what that involves. Overlapping designations are removed by dissolving the layer before measurement, so a site carrying three overlapping labels counts once. Proposed sites, sites without a designation status, and point records with no reported area are excluded, as are UNESCO Man and the Biosphere reserves as whole sites. Remaining point records are converted to circles sized by their reported area, a step the compilers note "can underestimate or overestimate protected area coverage". The terrestrial denominator excludes Antarctica; the marine denominator is national exclusive economic zones, with areas beyond national jurisdiction reported separately; and from 2024 areas are computed geodesically rather than on a Mollweide projection.

None of that is a criticism; it is a careful accounting of designations. But it fixes what the number can mean. The percentage is a dissolved, buffered, filtered sum of legal boundaries submitted by states. It contains no information about staffing, enforcement, condition, or what the land would have looked like without the designation, which is the distinction developed at length in the article on [protected-area effectiveness](/en/ecology/biodiversity/protected-area-effectiveness).

## What the Aichi period demonstrated

The terrestrial element of Target 11 was met, though the confirmation arrived late: Protected Planet records the 17 per cent threshold as first reflected in official data with the March 2023 database release, at 17.08 per cent, once the lag between designation and reporting had worked through. The marine element was not met; coastal and marine coverage stood at 8.44 per cent in the 2024 assessment, still short of the 10 per cent asked for by 2020.

The retrospective published alongside that finding is the more useful part. Target 11 carried several conditions, and, in Protected Planet's own summary, "an outsized focus on percentage coverage meant that none were fully implemented". A decade of effort had been organised around the element that was easiest to report, and the elements that specified what the protection should achieve went comparatively unattended. That is a result about targets as instruments, not about protected areas.

## The headline indicator and the ones behind it

The monitoring framework for Target 3 reproduces the same asymmetry with the qualifiers now stronger. The headline indicator is "Coverage of protected areas and OECMs" — one number. Behind it sit component indicators for protected-area coverage of Key Biodiversity Areas, protected-area management effectiveness, ProtConn and PARC-Connectedness, a Red List of Ecosystems connectivity indicator still in development, counts of sites completing a site-level assessment of governance and equity, and the Species Protection Index.

The 2024 global assessment shows how unevenly those are populated. Coverage stood at 17.58 per cent of terrestrial and inland waters and 8.44 per cent of marine and coastal areas, with a further 16.71 million square kilometres of land (12.42 percentage points) and 78.26 million square kilometres of sea (21.56 points) required to reach 30 per cent. Against that, the PARC-representativeness score — the mean share of ecologically distinct environments protected — was 10.00 per cent, well under the headline figure, and 8.52 per cent of land was both protected and connected. Just under a third of Key Biodiversity Areas, 32.03 per cent, fell entirely outside any protected or conserved area. Management effectiveness had been assessed across 4.78 per cent of the world's terrestrial area and 1.26 per cent of the marine realm.

The qualifier indicators are not merely lower than the headline. For effectiveness they describe a small minority of the estate, so "effectively conserved" is unverified for most of what is counted rather than known to be failing — a distinction worth keeping, because the evidence there is absent rather than adverse.

## What OECMs add, and what is not yet known about them

The 2018 addition of other effective area-based conservation measures widened the accounting to geographically defined areas outside the protected-area system that are governed and managed in ways producing sustained positive outcomes for in-situ biodiversity, whether or not conservation is the primary objective. The logic is outcome-oriented: a place is counted by what it delivers rather than by its legal category, which makes room for regimes that were never going to be gazetted as reserves — including many arrangements examined in the evidence on [community-managed conservation](/en/ecology/conservation/community-based-conservation-evidence).

Uptake so far is too narrow to evaluate. The 2024 assessment records 6,464 OECMs reported in total, from 15 countries and territories, contributing 1.18 percentage points of terrestrial coverage, and notes that no effectiveness data are currently reported for OECMs at all. Recognition is described as an evolving process. The category could improve the correspondence between the headline number and reality, by admitting places that work; it could also degrade it, by relabelling existing land uses as conservation without changing them. Which of those is happening cannot be read from a coverage figure, and the reporting base is currently a dozen-odd countries.

## Whether coverage tracks outcome

For the one pressure protected areas are best equipped to resist — conversion of habitat — the answer is a qualified yes, with wide variance. A 2024 analysis in *Nature Communications* of more than 160,000 protected areas, using satellite habitat data at 30-metre resolution with propensity-score matching and a synthetic difference-in-differences design, found average habitat loss rates inside protected areas 4.20 percentage points lower than in comparable unprotected land, a relative reduction of about 33.08 per cent, with cropland conversion 2.54 points lower and forest loss 1.20 points lower. The same study classed 15.69 per cent of protected areas as ineffective, and found that 1.14 million square kilometres of habitat had been altered inside 73 per cent of protected areas between 2003 and 2019.

An average of that shape is compatible with a large fraction of sites doing nothing, which is why the aggregate is a poor guide to any particular designation. The harder question also resists the same treatment. As a 2020 review in the same journal sets out, evaluating whether a protected area abated a threat requires contrasting observed conditions with "what would have happened in the absence of protection", and for threats such as overexploitation and invasive species the data must come from field surveys, so large-scale evaluations of effectiveness "have not yet been possible". That review also records the resourcing gap behind the effectiveness question: less than a quarter of protected areas reporting on the point had adequate staffing and budget. Its warning about targets is direct — focusing on extent "can have perverse outcomes if it encourages nations to expand protection towards areas of little conservation value, or without the necessary subsequent investment in management" — and it notes that when protected areas covered 15.0 per cent of land and 7.4 per cent of the ocean, more than one in two terrestrial ecoregions were still below the 17 per cent mark and 39 per cent of Key Biodiversity Areas had no protection at all. In the sea the divergence between designation and effect is sharper still, because a single label spans regimes from no-take to trawled, a point developed in the review of [what marine protected areas deliver](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-protected-areas-evidence).

## What the instrument is for

An area target does one thing well. It creates a common, auditable unit on which parties can be compared, and gives domestic advocates something a finance ministry cannot argue with. Coverage rose over both the Aichi and post-2020 periods, and it is not credible that this happened at the same rate without a number to miss.

The failure mode is the other side of the same property. A scalar that is cheap to satisfy attracts effort from the conditions that are expensive to satisfy, and the cheapest hectares are the ones nobody wanted — which is how a target can be met while the ecoregions, the Key Biodiversity Areas and the [places prioritisation would have selected](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-trade-offs-and-prioritisation) stay outside the network. Nothing in the Target 3 text endorses that outcome — it asks for representativeness, connectivity, effectiveness and equity in so many words. The gap is in measurement, not ambition. Until the component indicators are reported at anything like the completeness of the coverage indicator, "30 per cent effectively conserved" will continue to be assessed as "30 per cent", and the difference between those two claims is the whole subject.
