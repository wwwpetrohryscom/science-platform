A Red List category is a claim about probability, made under stated rules, from stated evidence. Critically Endangered means a taxon meets at least one of five quantitative criteria at the most severe threshold, and is therefore judged to face an extremely high risk of extinction in the wild. It does not mean the organism is rare, beautiful, ecologically pivotal or a spending priority. Those are separate judgements, and the system was designed to keep them separate so that the risk grading could be audited independently of what anyone wanted to do about it — the general principle behind [evidence-based conservation](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained).

## Five criteria, five different kinds of data

The criteria are alternatives, not a checklist. A taxon qualifies for a category by meeting any one of them, which is what allows an assessment to proceed when only one type of data exists. Thresholds below are from version 3.1 of the categories and criteria; Endangered sits between the two columns shown.

| Criterion | What it demands | Critically Endangered | Vulnerable |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — population reduction | An observed, inferred or projected decline over 10 years or three generations, whichever is longer | ≥ 80 % where the causes may not have ceased or be understood | ≥ 30 % on the same basis |
| B — geographic range | Extent of occurrence or area of occupancy, plus at least two of severe fragmentation, continuing decline, extreme fluctuation | EOO < 100 km² or AOO < 10 km² | EOO < 20,000 km² or AOO < 2,000 km² |
| C — small declining population | A count of mature individuals combined with a continuing decline | < 250 mature individuals | < 10,000 mature individuals |
| D — very small or restricted population | A count alone, or a very restricted range | < 50 mature individuals | < 1,000, or AOO typically < 20 km² or ≤ 5 locations |
| E — quantitative analysis | An explicit extinction-probability model | ≥ 50 % within 10 years or three generations | ≥ 10 % within 100 years |

Two features of that table deserve more attention than they usually get. Criterion B is the cheapest to apply in practice, because a range map can be assembled from occurrence records without anyone counting an animal; criterion E is the most demanding, requiring a viability model with parameters most taxa do not have. Which criterion an assessment ends up using is therefore shaped at least as much by what data exist for that group as by which measure would best capture its risk.

The second feature is the generation clause. Criterion A measures decline over ten years *or three generations, whichever is longer*. For a long-lived organism — a large tree, a whale, a tortoise — three generations can span a century, so a steep decline within a human working lifetime may not reach the threshold, while the same proportional decline in an insect is caught quickly. This is deliberate: it scales the window to the organism's own dynamics. It also means two taxa with identical decline rates measured in years can land in different categories, and neither assessment is wrong.

## Data Deficient is not a threat category

The rules are explicit that a Data Deficient listing indicates inadequate information to assess risk from distribution or population status, and that it is therefore not a category of threat. A taxon can be well studied and still be Data Deficient if abundance and distribution data are the missing pieces. The listing is a request for information, not a reassurance.

Treating it as a mild category is a common and consequential error. A machine-learning analysis published in Communications Biology generated extinction-risk probabilities for 7,699 Data Deficient taxa — 17 per cent of the entities in the Red List spatial datasets — and found that as a group they may be *more* threatened than the data-sufficient ones. Around 85 per cent of Data Deficient amphibians were predicted to be threatened, with more than half predicted threatened in several other groups including mammals and reptiles. If those predictions hold even approximately, indices derived from the Red List are biased by the exclusion, and the conservation importance of some regions — the analysis points to parts of South America — is understated by up to a fifth.

## Lower, best and upper estimates

The Red List's own summary tables handle this uncertainty in a way that is worth copying. For groups assessed nearly completely, three percentages are published rather than one: a lower estimate assuming no Data Deficient taxon is threatened, a best estimate assuming they are threatened at the same rate as data-sufficient taxa, and an upper estimate assuming all of them are.

For mammals in version 2025-1 of the list, that produces 23 per cent, 27 per cent and 36 per cent. For amphibians, 36, 41 and 47 per cent. For birds, all three figures are 12 per cent, because birds are the only large group with essentially no Data Deficient residue. The spread between lower and upper is a direct readout of how much of a group's risk profile is unresolved, and quoting the best estimate without it discards the most honest part of the table.

## Risk is not rate

Extinction risk and extinction rate are different quantities that share a vocabulary. Risk is forward-looking and per taxon: the probability that this lineage disappears within a stated window. Rate is backward-looking and aggregate: how many extinctions occurred per unit of time, usually expressed as extinctions per million species-years.

The comparison of current with background rates rests on assumptions that should be visible in any statement of it. An analysis in Science Advances built the comparison deliberately conservatively, adopting a background rate of two mammal extinctions per 10,000 species per century — twice the figure previously in common use — and comparing it with documented vertebrate losses. On those assumptions the average rate of vertebrate loss over the last century runs up to a hundred times the background rate, and the extinctions recorded in that century would otherwise have taken between 800 and 10,000 years to accumulate, depending on the group. The conservatism runs in one direction: confirming an extinction requires meeting stringent evidential criteria, so documented totals are floors. Choosing a higher background rate makes the ratio smaller, which is exactly why the assumption should be stated whenever the ratio is quoted.

## The coverage problem

The most under-reported figure in this field is how little of life has been assessed at all. Version 2025-1 of the Red List records 169,420 taxa evaluated against an estimated 2,139,242 described, of which 47,187 are classed as threatened — that is, Critically Endangered, Endangered or Vulnerable. Evaluation therefore covers about 8 per cent of described life.

The shortfall is not evenly spread. Vertebrates are 84 per cent evaluated, with birds complete and mammals and amphibians close to it. Invertebrates are 2 per cent evaluated; insects, over a million described taxa, stand at 1.3 per cent. Among plants, gymnosperms are 95 per cent evaluated while flowering plants are at 20 per cent. Any global statement of the form "one in four assessed organisms is threatened" is a statement about a sample chosen mainly for charisma and tractability, and it cannot be extrapolated to the invertebrate majority without an argument that nobody currently has the data to make.

This is also where risk assessment connects to the drivers. Criterion B's fragmentation clause draws directly on the same landscape measurements discussed under [fragmentation and connectivity](/en/ecology/conservation/habitat-fragmentation-and-connectivity), while introduced predators and competitors appear explicitly in criterion A's list of admissible evidence — the mechanism examined in [invasive species management](/en/ecology/conservation/invasive-species-management). A category is downstream of both, and the aggregate movement of categories over time is what the [Red List Index](/en/ecology/biodiversity/red-list-index-explained) is built to track.

What the system cannot do is rank importance. It says nothing about a taxon's ecological role, its evolutionary distinctness, or whether intervention would succeed. Those questions are the input to [recovery programmes](/en/ecology/conservation/endangered-species-recovery-programmes) and to prioritisation, and reading a category as an answer to them is the most frequent misuse of an otherwise carefully bounded instrument.
