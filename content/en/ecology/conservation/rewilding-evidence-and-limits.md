Three largely separate research programmes publish under the word rewilding, and they disagree about what is being restored, what it is being restored towards, and whether anything should be released at all. Reading their results as though they belonged to one literature is the fastest way to reach a wrong conclusion in either direction — that the idea is unsupported, or that it is established.

That makes rewilding an awkward but instructive case for the broader question of [how conservation interventions are tested and compared](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained): the intervention here is not one treatment with a variable dose, but several treatments that happen to share a label.

## Three programmes that share a name

The strands differ most sharply in what they treat as the target condition, which is also what determines whether an outcome can be scored at all.

| Strand | Core intervention | Target condition | Where the empirical work sits | Principal scientific objection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trophic | Reintroduction or ecological replacement of large herbivores and predators to restore top-down interactions | Functioning trophic interactions rather than a historical species list | Case studies of predator and large-herbivore restoration, plus unplanned recolonisations | Effects are conditional on landscape context and human activity, and are rarely tested against controls |
| Passive | Withdrawal of land use and management, letting succession run | Whatever the site becomes without direction | Land-abandonment accounting and vegetation surveys, mostly European | The outcome is undirected, and open-habitat specialists can be lost as scrub and woodland close in |
| Pleistocene | Introduction of extant proxies for megafauna lost in the late Quaternary | Late-Pleistocene faunal function | Largely conceptual and modelled | The target condition is unobservable, and proxies have no local evolutionary history |

A common vocabulary does exist. Ten guiding principles published in *Conservation Biology* were assembled from a survey of 59 rewilding specialists and workshops involving more than 100 participants, and the [guiding principles for rewilding](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/172424/) describe the idea as a continuum of scale, connectivity and human influence aimed at self-sustaining nature. The IUCN Commission on Ecosystem Management condensed those into five guidelines in 2025. Agreement on principles, though, is not agreement on measurement, and the guidelines say so: one of the five turns on evidence and monitoring, and the document notes that standard monitoring guidance is still lacking.

## What the best-known trophic case actually shows

The wolves of Yellowstone are the textbook illustration of a trophic cascade, and the way that illustration was measured is the most useful thing about it. Field crews recording aspen regeneration commonly sampled the five tallest young stems in a stand — a practice that appeared across roughly a dozen peer-reviewed publications over more than a decade.

An analysis in *Ecology Letters* re-examined a long time series and found that this choice [overestimated overstory aspen regeneration by a factor of four to seven](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10377598) relative to random sampling of all young stems, because the tallest stems were already above the preferred browsing height of elk and because stands with no regeneration at all went unrecorded. A second assumption compounded it: stems were treated as safe from browsing above 200 cm, whereas escape from browsing looked uncertain until roughly 400 cm.

The finding is not that the cascade was absent. Random sampling still described one. It described a weaker one. That distinction matters for anyone reading claims about [predator-driven changes in food webs](/en/ecology/ecosystems/food-webs-and-trophic-structure), because the size of the effect, not its existence, is what determines whether restoring a predator is a plausible tool elsewhere.

## Passive rewilding is mostly a land-accounting question

Where the trophic strand argues about species, the passive strand mostly argues about hectares. The European Commission's Joint Research Centre projected net [agricultural land abandonment in the EU](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC113718) of 4.2 million hectares between 2015 and 2030, averaging about 280,000 hectares a year and bringing the total to 5.6 million hectares, or roughly 3 per cent of agricultural land. About 11 per cent of EU farmland — more than 20 million hectares — was classed as at high potential risk of abandonment, with Spain and Poland together accounting for a third of the projected loss.

Something similar happened without being planned. Analysis published in *Science* found that roughly one-third of mainland Europe hosts at least one of brown bear, Eurasian lynx, grey wolf or wolverine, with stable or increasing abundance in most twenty-first-century records, achieved largely in shared, human-dominated landscapes rather than in reserves. Whether either process counts as rewilding depends on the definition being used, and neither is uniformly favourable to biodiversity: semi-natural grasslands and their specialists depend on continued low-intensity use, so abandonment trades one assemblage for another. The comparable question for planted woodland is taken up in the evidence on [reforestation and tree planting](/en/ecology/forests/reforestation-and-tree-planting-evidence).

## The reference-state problem, stated plainly

Restoration usually names a reference ecosystem and measures distance from it — which is why the [restoration meta-analyses can report recovery ratios](/en/ecology/conservation/ecological-restoration-evidence) at all. Rewilding, by design, resists that step. The third guiding principle asks projects to work from an appropriate ecological reference while conceding, in the IUCN text, that any reference point is ultimately arbitrary — it should be self-sustaining and resilient rather than historically exact. The same document lists shifting baseline syndrome in its glossary, the tendency for each generation to treat the conditions it grew up with as normal.

This is a genuine methodological difficulty rather than an evasion, and it is the same difficulty examined in [how baselines are chosen and why they drift](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-baselines-and-shifting-baselines). If the target is a process rather than a state, then progress can be described but not scored against a fixed benchmark, and different observers can look at the same site and disagree about whether it is succeeding. Defining a [baseline condition](/en/glossary/baseline-condition) is the step that decides the verdict, and it is a choice, not a measurement.

## Where "self-sustaining" meets a fence

The Oostvaardersplassen in the Netherlands is the most closely documented enclosed experiment in minimal-intervention grazing, and its record shows what the phrase costs in practice. A second international commission reported in 2010 on the management of large herbivores there. Its assessment was carefully split: the reserve's boundaries and initial foundation are man-made, but most of the internal processes parallel those of natural ecosystems.

The commission also recorded that total herbivore numbers began to approach the area's population-based carrying capacity after 2000, that mortality rose and became more variable across years as winter severity interacted with density, and that average body condition declined over the preceding decade. Its recommendations included early reactive culling from mid-December, additional shelter, and a connecting corridor to neighbouring woodland. In an enclosed area with no emigration and no large predators, non-intervention is itself a management decision with measurable welfare consequences — which is the part of the idea that popular accounts most often drop.

## What the field cannot yet tell you

A scoping review in *Biological Conservation* mapped the peer-reviewed literature that has actually measured outcomes of European rewilding projects. It found real growth in that literature, alongside a geographical concentration in the Netherlands and Scandinavian countries, and it repeated a standing criticism that the science has been more theory-led than evidence-led. It also argued against forcing a single standardised success metric across sites, on the grounds that rewilding outcomes are site-specific and partly self-defined — a defensible position that nevertheless makes synthesis harder. An earlier synthesis of [trophic rewilding](/en/glossary/trophic-rewilding) in *PNAS* reached a similar verdict on the empirical base: rare, fragmented, geographically skewed, and outweighed by essays and opinion pieces.

The evidence that would change this is unglamorous and slow: releases paired with untreated controls, before-and-after designs that survive the departure of the founding staff, random rather than convenient sampling of the vegetation, and monitoring that outlasts a funding cycle. Until more of that exists, the honest summary is that trophic rewilding has demonstrated effects whose magnitude is contested, passive rewilding has well-measured extent and poorly measured consequences, and the Pleistocene variant remains an argument rather than a result.
