A release is an event; a recovery is a trajectory. Almost every unresolved question in this field lives in the distance between the two, and most of the numbers that circulate about reintroduction describe the event rather than the trajectory.

The IUCN's 2013 guidelines cover the full spectrum of conservation translocations and address justification, design and implementation — but they cannot settle what "worked" means, because that phrase changes meaning with the length of the follow-up. Within the broader question of [which conservation interventions have been tested and how](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-science-explained), species recovery is unusual in having a large case literature and a small controlled one.

## Two different things get called success

A global systematic review assembled 514 translocations of terrestrial vertebrates and deliberately used two outcome measures rather than one: a categorical success-or-failure classification, and population growth rate. Those measures answer different questions. The first asks whether animals established; the second asks whether the population is going anywhere.

Its results are worth stating precisely. Both probability of categorical success and population growth rate rose with the total number of individuals released, with [diminishing returns above roughly 20 to 50 animals](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989421001803). Categorical success rose through the twentieth century and appears to have plateaued near 75 per cent since about 1990. Outcomes were better in Europe and North America than elsewhere and worse in Oceania, where invasive predators are the dominant threat and are hard to control at release sites.

Two cautions attach to that 75 per cent. It is a rate across published projects, and projects that failed quietly are less likely to be written up; and establishment measured over a few years is a weaker claim than persistence measured over decades. The review's own recommendation — greater investment per project rather than more projects — follows from the release-size result, not from the headline rate.

## The genetic problem that release alone does not solve

Small isolated populations lose heterozygosity and accumulate the effects of mating among relatives, a process covered from first principles in [genetic drift and population size](/en/biology/evolution/genetic-drift-and-population-size). Adding animals to such a population from a related source — genetic rescue — is one of the few interventions in conservation with a quantitative meta-analytic answer.

That meta-analysis screened 156 cases judged to carry a low risk of outbreeding depression and found beneficial effects in 92.9 per cent of them. The median gain in composite fitness, combining fecundity and survival, was 148 per cent in stressful environments and 45 per cent in benign ones. At the time it was written, only around 20 conservation outcrossings had been published, which the author treated as a small fraction of the populations that might benefit.

The Florida panther is the case most often cited, and the published assessment supports the outline while complicating the ending. Eight female pumas were moved from Texas to southern Florida in 1995; panther numbers subsequently increased roughly threefold, average individual heterozygosity doubled, and physical correlates of inbreeding declined significantly. The same paper lists what did not resolve: continued habitat loss, persistent inbreeding, infectious agents including rising feline immunodeficiency virus prevalence, and possible habitat saturation. Genetic rescue bought demographic room. It did not remove the constraint that created the problem.

## Captivity selects, and it selects quickly

The unglamorous risk in captive breeding is not disease or husbandry but selection. A pedigree analysis of hatchery steelhead released into an Oregon river found that first-generation hatchery fish had nearly double the lifetime reproductive success of wild fish when both were spawned in captivity under identical conditions — a direct demonstration of adaptation to the captive environment in a single generation.

The trade-off showed up in the same pedigree. Captive-born individuals with five or more returning siblings, meaning they came from broodstock that had done well in captivity, averaged 0.62 returning offspring in the wild; those with fewer than five siblings averaged 2.05. Traits rewarded in a hatchery were penalised in a river. This is [evolutionary adaptation](/en/glossary/evolutionary-adaptation) working against the programme's own objective, and it means that time spent in captivity is not a neutral holding period for the population being held.

## What the documented recoveries have in common

The clearest global estimate of what conservation has averted comes from expert elicitation rather than measurement, and the authors present it that way. Assessors concluded that action prevented 21 to 32 bird and 7 to 16 mammal extinctions between 1993 and 2020, and 9 to 18 bird and 2 to 7 mammal extinctions since 2010. Over the same period 10 bird and 5 mammal species were lost or are strongly suspected to have been. On those figures extinction rates would have been 2.9 to 4.2 times greater without intervention. The bounds are wide because the counterfactual is unobservable; the ranges are the finding, not a defect in it.

Individual cases show the same conditional shape. The US Fish and Wildlife Service records that only 23 California condors survived worldwide in 1982 and that the total free-flying and captive population has since been grown to more than 500 — while also stating that lead poisoning from ammunition fragments remains the number one known cause of death. The black-footed ferret was presumed extinct across its range by the late 1950s and rediscovered near Meeteetse, Wyoming, in 1981; the agency identifies sylvatic plague as the most significant challenge to population resiliency, and reports the largest reintroduced population, at Shirley Basin, averaging about 100 breeding adults in 2012.

Neither species is self-sustaining in the sense that a recovered population would be. Both are held above the extinction threshold by a continuing programme, which is a real outcome and a different one from restoration. The [Red List category](/en/ecology/biodiversity/red-list-index-explained) a species carries does not encode that dependence, which is part of why a separate recovery standard was developed alongside it.

## Recovery is not the inverse of extinction risk

The IUCN Green Status of Species was built to measure how far a species sits from a fully recovered state — viable and ecologically functional populations across its pre-impact range — rather than how close it is to disappearing. When the draft framework was tested on 181 species spanning diverse taxa, life histories, biomes and Red List categories, 59 per cent came out as largely or critically depleted, and the relationship between extinction risk and recovery score, while negative, was loose: some species in lower risk categories were assessed as further from recovery than species at higher risk.

The same test quantified how much of that status is attributable to conservation. Only 1.7 per cent of the species examined scored zero across all four impact metrics — conservation legacy, conservation dependence, conservation gain and recovery potential — meaning that for almost all of them, past or projected action was judged to matter. Set against the translocation record, that produces an uncomfortable pairing: intervention is close to ubiquitous among assessed species, while its measured effect sizes remain patchy and its follow-up short.

## The comparisons the record cannot make

Two questions come up constantly and are not answerable from this literature as it stands.

The first is cost per species saved. Recovery budgets are rarely published in comparable units, projects differ in what they include, and the counterfactual is unknown for any single case — so cross-species cost rankings tend to compare accounting conventions. Where costs have been analysed systematically it has been at the level of portfolios rather than individual species, which is the framing used in [allocating a finite conservation budget](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-trade-offs-and-prioritisation).

The second is how long a programme must run before its result is stable. Follow-up in the translocation literature is usually a few years, occasionally a decade, and almost never long enough to observe a population through a full cycle of drought, disease and predator recovery. A project scored as a success at year five and a failure at year twenty-five is not a contradiction in the data. It is the same project, measured twice.
