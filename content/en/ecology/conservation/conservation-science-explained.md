Conservation asks a question that sounds practical and is in fact causal: did this action change the trajectory of this population, this habitat, this landscape, compared with what would have happened without it? Almost every methodological difficulty in the field descends from one uncomfortable property of that question — the comparison state is never observed. It has to be constructed.

That is what makes conservation an applied science with a testable intervention literature rather than a statement of preference. Values decide which outcomes are worth pursuing. Evidence decides whether a given action delivers them. Keeping the two apart is a precondition for making progress on either, and conflating them is the fastest way to defend an intervention that does not work.

## The counterfactual is the whole problem

The foundational critique of conservation evaluation was published in PLOS Biology in 2006, and its argument has not been superseded: without an estimate of what would have happened anyway, spending figures and coverage figures say nothing about impact. The essay pushed the field toward quasi-experimental methods borrowed from programme evaluation in economics, above all matching, which pairs treated sites with untreated sites that resemble them on the variables that drove the treatment decision.

Protected areas show why this matters. An analysis of networks across 147 nations found that protection is systematically biased toward land that is higher, steeper, further from roads and cities, and less suitable for agriculture. Protected land was, on that evidence, land under less conversion pressure to begin with. A naive comparison of protected against unprotected sites therefore measures the siting decision at least as much as it measures protection, and it does so in a direction that flatters the intervention. The same selection problem recurs wherever participation is voluntary, wherever managers choose their sites, and wherever funders prefer places that look tractable — which is to say nearly everywhere. It is particularly acute for [community-managed and Indigenous-managed areas](/en/ecology/conservation/community-based-conservation-evidence), where tenure, remoteness and governance are entangled with the outcome being measured.

## What the design does to the answer

Design choice is not a technical footnote; it changes the reported result. A global analysis in Nature applied a before–after control–intervention design to 1,506 protected areas and 27,055 waterbird populations, then compared the answer with what simpler designs would have concluded from the same data. Before–after alone, or control–intervention alone, misestimated the effect for 37 to 50 per cent of populations, in some cases reversing the sign — classifying a population that benefited as one that was harmed.

The prevalence of the weaker designs is high. Across the environmental and social intervention literature, randomised designs and controlled observational designs with pre-intervention sampling accounted for just 23 per cent of studies in biodiversity conservation, against 36 per cent in social science. Paired within-study comparisons across 49 environmental datasets showed that the stronger designs generally return less biased estimates. The practical consequence is that a body of evidence can be large and still be systematically wrong in the same direction.

## What the syntheses currently support

The most comprehensive synthesis to date, published in Science in 2024, assembled 186 studies covering 665 trials in which biodiversity was measured over time against a counterfactual of no action. In roughly two-thirds of cases, conservation either improved the state of biodiversity or slowed its decline. Interventions aimed at species and ecosystems — invasive species control, reduction of habitat loss, restoration, protected areas, sustainable management — carried the larger effect sizes.

Two readings of that result are wrong in opposite directions. It is not a demonstration that conservation generally works, because the remaining third of cases showed no such benefit, and because the studies analysed are the ones somebody chose to run and publish. Nor is it grounds for pessimism: an effect of that size, measured against a genuine counterfactual rather than against a hopeful assumption, is a substantial finding.

## Reading the cluster

The intervention classes differ enormously in how strong their evidence is, and the difference tracks how cleanly the outcome can be observed. Eradicating an invasive mammal from an island produces a near-census outcome on a bounded system, which is why island work carries the cleanest effectiveness record in [invasive species management](/en/ecology/conservation/invasive-species-management) — and why mainland control, where nothing is bounded, has a far weaker one. [Ecological restoration](/en/ecology/conservation/ecological-restoration-evidence) has abundant meta-analytic evidence of improvement over degraded baselines and consistent evidence of shortfall against reference conditions, with the verdict depending on which reference is chosen. [Species recovery programmes](/en/ecology/conservation/endangered-species-recovery-programmes) can document individual successes in detail but struggle with a definition problem: establishment is not persistence. [Corridors and connectivity](/en/ecology/conservation/habitat-fragmentation-and-connectivity) rest on a small number of long-running experiments whose effects only became visible after a decade or more. [Rewilding](/en/ecology/conservation/rewilding-evidence-and-limits) is the thinnest case, largely because the word covers at least three distinct programmes that should not be pooled.

Two cross-cutting questions sit underneath all of them. How risk is graded — the subject of [extinction risk assessment](/en/ecology/conservation/species-extinction-risk-assessment) — determines which species enter the queue at all. How a finite budget is allocated across that queue is the subject of [conservation prioritisation](/en/ecology/conservation/conservation-trade-offs-and-prioritisation), and it is where the evidence question becomes an explicit trade-off rather than an implicit one.

## Where the evidence runs out

Three gaps are structural rather than incidental. Geographically, the assessment literature repeatedly identifies Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, and parts of Asia as under-represented; conclusions drawn from temperate, well-funded systems are being applied to tropical systems with different ecology and different governance. Taxonomically, the bias toward vertebrates in monitoring mirrors the bias in risk assessment itself. Temporally, the mismatch is the sharpest: funding cycles run three to five years, while the effects of connectivity and of restoration accumulate over decades and can be invisible at the point when a project reports.

There is also no way to size that gap from the published record itself. The syntheses summarised above can only pool work that was written up, and none of them estimates how much unsuccessful or abandoned project work never reached print. Curated evidence syntheses such as the Conservation Evidence project have reduced the cost of finding what has been tested, but they can only summarise what was written down.

## Area targets are an input, not an outcome

The World Database on Protected Areas records 17.36 per cent of terrestrial and inland water areas and 9.81 per cent of marine areas within protected areas as of September 2026, rising to 18.45 and 10.03 per cent when other effective area-based conservation measures are included. The Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework commits parties to at least 30 per cent of both realms being effectively conserved and managed by 2030.

Coverage is measurable, cheap to report, and only weakly connected to biodiversity outcomes. The waterbird analysis found the strongest signal not in whether a site was protected but in whether it was actively managed for the species or the habitat, with a weaker signal favouring larger sites. That is consistent with the broader literature on [protected-area effectiveness](/en/ecology/biodiversity/protected-area-effectiveness), which separates designation from management quality, and with the way regional reporting bodies build [suites of biodiversity indicators](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-indicators-explained) rather than leaning on a single headline figure. A percentage of area is an input to conservation, in the same way that spending is an input. Whether it becomes an outcome depends on management, enforcement and location — none of which the coverage statistic records, and all of which the evidence identifies as decisive. That gap between the target and the thing the target is meant to represent is the single most common source of confusion in conservation reporting, and it is not resolved by raising the target.
