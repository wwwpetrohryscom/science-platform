Almost every claim about nature contains a hidden comparison. To say a population has declined, recovered, or remained intact is to measure it against some earlier state taken as normal. That earlier state is the reference point, and choosing it is one of the quietest yet most consequential decisions in biodiversity monitoring.

## What a baseline is and why it matters

A [baseline condition](/en/glossary/baseline-condition) is the reference state against which biodiversity change is judged. Words such as "loss," "recovery," and "intact" have no meaning on their own; each is defined relative to a chosen point of comparison. Move the reference, and the same set of observations can support different conclusions about whether a system is improving or deteriorating.

This dependence is easy to overlook because baselines are often left implicit. A survey may report that a forest, reef, or fishery is stable without stating which past condition that stability is measured from. Yet the reference does the heavy lifting: it determines what counts as a healthy state and what counts as a deviation. Making the comparison visible is the first step toward reading any biodiversity figure carefully.

## Shifting-baseline syndrome

The phenomenon known as shifting-baseline syndrome began in fisheries science and has since spread across ecology. The core observation is that each generation of scientists and managers tends to treat the state of nature they first encountered as the natural baseline, even when that starting point is already degraded relative to earlier conditions. The reference quietly inherits whatever decline preceded it.

Over successive generations the perceived normal ratchets downward. What one cohort experiences as a depleted system, the next may experience as ordinary, because they never knew the richer state that came before. The cumulative effect is that long-run loss is systematically underestimated: each new reference absorbs part of the prior decline and treats it as the background against which only recent change is measured. Foundational thinking on this pattern emerged from [fisheries and biodiversity](https://www.fao.org/biodiversity/en/) work, where memories of once-abundant stocks faded across professional generations.

## How a degraded system can look healthy

The practical consequence is striking. A system can be judged stable, or even healthy, against a recent reference while having lost most of its historical abundance or geographic extent. The verdict reflects the chosen comparison, not the full trajectory.

A widely cited example is the starting point of [the Living Planet Index](/en/ecology/biodiversity/living-planet-index-explained), which is indexed to 1970. That date is a sensible and practical anchor for a global dataset, but in many systems it already follows substantial earlier losses. Measuring change from 1970 therefore describes the path since then rather than the distance from a less disturbed state. The same caution applies when interpreting [protected-area effectiveness](/en/ecology/biodiversity/protected-area-effectiveness): a reserve that holds its current condition steady may be conserving an already-thinned version of what the area once supported. None of this makes recent baselines wrong; it makes them partial. They answer a narrower question than they often appear to.

## Reconstructing deeper baselines

Because recent references can mask older change, researchers try to reconstruct deeper baselines from several independent lines of evidence. Long-term monitoring records, where they exist, extend the comparison backward in time. Museum and herbarium specimens document which species occurred where, and when. Historical surveys, archives, and written accounts add further context from periods before systematic monitoring.

Where biological remains survive, paleoecological proxies such as pollen, sediment layers, and subfossils can reach further still, sketching conditions long predating any modern record. Aggregated occurrence records, including the specimen and observation data compiled through [occurrence records](https://www.gbif.org/), help assemble distributions across time and space. Where direct evidence is missing altogether, scientists sometimes turn to modelled "potential" reference states that estimate what a system might support under defined conditions. International syntheses such as the [Global Assessment Report](https://www.ipbes.net/global-assessment) draw these strands together to place present conditions within a longer view of change, an approach that resonates with the broader aims of [biodiversity monitoring and ecosystem health](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-monitoring-and-ecosystem-health).

## Methodology: making the reference explicit

Good practice treats the choice of baseline as a decision to be stated openly rather than assumed. The first principle is to make the reference period explicit: to say plainly which past condition the analysis compares against, so readers can judge what the resulting figure does and does not capture. A trend reported without its reference is difficult to interpret and easy to misread.

The second principle is to test sensitivity to that choice. Because conclusions can hinge on the reference, careful analyses re-examine how the apparent direction and size of change shift if a different baseline is adopted. This is conceptually similar to the way [ecosystem resilience indicators](/en/ecology/biodiversity/ecosystem-resilience-indicators) are read against a clearly defined pre-disturbance state, and it connects to the wider idea of [ecosystem resilience](/en/glossary/ecosystem-resilience), where the meaning of a stable system depends on the condition it is compared to. Peer-reviewed historical-ecology research, published in outlets such as [Science](https://www.science.org/), has refined these methods for combining sparse historical sources into defensible reference conditions.

## Uncertainty and limitations

Deeper baselines carry real and unavoidable uncertainty. Historical evidence grows sparse the further back one looks; specimens, records, and proxies are uneven in coverage and quality, and gaps are common. Reconstructed reference states are therefore estimates with wide margins, not precise descriptions of the past.

A further difficulty is that the choice of baseline can change the story itself. Different references can alter, and sometimes reverse, the apparent direction or magnitude of biodiversity change for the same data. There is also rarely a single correct reference state to settle on, because which past condition is most relevant depends on the question being asked and the management goal in view. None of this means baselines should be abandoned. It means they are best treated as explicit, testable choices, reported transparently and revisited as evidence improves, so that statements about loss and recovery can be understood for exactly what they measure.
