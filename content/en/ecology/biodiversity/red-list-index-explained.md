The [IUCN Red List](https://www.iucnredlist.org/) sorts species into categories of extinction risk, from those of least concern to those that have disappeared. On its own, a single assessment is a snapshot. The Red List Index turns repeated assessments of the same groups into a trend, and this piece explains how that conversion works, what the categories and criteria measure, and where the resulting signal can and cannot be trusted.

## From categories to a trend

Each species evaluated for the Red List is placed in one of several categories. In rising order of concern these run from Least Concern through Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, and Critically Endangered, then Extinct in the Wild and Extinct. A separate label, Data Deficient, applies to species for which there is not yet enough information to judge. The three middle categories — Vulnerable, Endangered, and Critically Endangered — are grouped together as "threatened."

A category captures status at one moment. To follow how a group of species fares over years, the assessments need to be combined into something that moves. That is the role of the [Red List Index](/en/glossary/red-list-index), which aggregates the categories of all species in a defined group into a single value that can be tracked across successive assessment rounds.

The index is scaled so that a value of 1 means every species in the group sits in Least Concern, while a value of 0 means every species has gone Extinct. A figure between the two summarises where the group lies along that spectrum. A falling value indicates that the group's overall extinction risk is climbing; a stable or rising value points to the opposite.

## How species are assessed

Placement in a category is not a matter of expert impression alone. It follows a set of standard quantitative criteria, labelled A through E, applied under standards maintained by [IUCN](https://www.iucn.org/). These criteria examine different facets of risk: the rate of population reduction, the size and fragmentation of a species' geographic range, whether a population is small or declining, and, where the data allow, a direct quantitative analysis of extinction probability.

A species can qualify for a threatened category under any one of these criteria. A sharp population decline can be enough on its own, as can a very restricted range, even when the other measures look more reassuring. This structure means that the assessment is designed to catch risk wherever it appears, rather than requiring every line of evidence to agree before a species is flagged.

Because the criteria are explicit and shared, two assessors working from the same data should reach the same category. That consistency is what makes assessments comparable across species and across time, and it is the foundation on which any trend can be built.

## Correcting for changing knowledge

A subtle problem sits at the heart of any long-running index. If a species moves from Data Deficient to Endangered simply because researchers finally gathered enough information, its situation has not worsened — only the records have improved. Counting that move as a deterioration would let the index measure the growth of scientific effort rather than the fate of wildlife.

The index is built to avoid this. As a [biodiversity indicator](/en/glossary/biodiversity-indicator) meant to track real change, it counts only genuine status shifts — those driven by real improvement or real decline in a species' prospects. Changes that arise purely from better data or from revised taxonomy are identified and corrected for, so they do not register in the trend. The aim is a measure that reflects what is happening to species, not what is happening to the catalogue that describes them.

This correction is part of what distinguishes the index from a simple tally of how many species fall in each category in a given year. It is the reason the tool can be read as a measure of conservation status over time rather than a measure of survey activity.

## Where the index is used and what it covers

The Red List Index serves as a headline indicator under the [Convention on Biological Diversity](https://www.cbd.int/), which means governments draw on it when reporting progress toward shared biodiversity goals. In that reporting role it sits alongside other [biodiversity indicators](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-indicators-explained) within the wider field of [biodiversity monitoring and ecosystem health](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-monitoring-and-ecosystem-health), each capturing a different dimension of change. A complementary measure such as [the Living Planet Index](/en/ecology/biodiversity/living-planet-index-explained), for instance, tracks population abundance rather than extinction-risk category.

The index can be calculated only for groups that have been assessed comprehensively and then reassessed, so that the same set of species is followed through more than one round. The standard examples are birds, mammals, amphibians, corals, and cycads. For these groups, repeated and reasonably complete coverage makes a meaningful trend possible.

## Limits and uncertainty

The coverage that the index depends on is also its main constraint. Most described species have never been assessed at all, and the gap is largest among invertebrates, fungi, and plants. Trends drawn only from the well-studied vertebrate and plant groups may not represent the breadth of life, a caution that echoes the broader point about [why species counts mislead conservation](/en/ecology/biodiversity/why-species-counts-mislead-conservation) when one figure stands in for many different patterns. Findings from intergovernmental work such as the [IPBES Global Assessment](https://www.ipbes.net/global-assessment) describe both the patterns of extinction risk and the monitoring gaps that remain.

Two further limits deserve mention. Data Deficient species complicate any trend, because their true status is unknown and their eventual classification can pull the picture in either direction. And reassessment does not happen continuously; it occurs at multi-year intervals, so the index updates in steps rather than smoothly, and recent shifts may not yet be visible. Read with these qualifications in mind, the Red List Index remains a careful and widely used way to follow extinction risk, while reminding its users that it speaks for the groups it covers and not for every species on Earth.
