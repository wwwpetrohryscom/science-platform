Biodiversity varies at several levels at once — the genes within a population, the abundance and range of a species, the makeup of a community, the structure of an ecosystem — and no single measurement captures all of them. To make that variation trackable, monitoring distils each dimension into an indicator. This article maps the main indicators used in international reporting, explains how each is built, and sets out why they are read side by side rather than merged into one headline figure. It sits within the wider EcoScienceHub guide to [biodiversity monitoring and ecosystem health](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-monitoring-and-ecosystem-health).

## What an indicator is, and why several are needed

A [biodiversity indicator](/en/glossary/biodiversity-indicator) is a measurement that compresses one dimension of biodiversity change into a signal that can be tracked over time. An indicator earns its place by meeting two conditions: it has a clear interpretation, so readers know what a rise or fall means, and it can be measured consistently across years and places, so a trend reflects nature rather than a change in method. This is the same logic that underlies climate indicators such as surface temperature or sea level.

The reason monitoring relies on several indicators rather than one is that each answers a different question. Extinction risk, population abundance, and habitat area are related but not equivalent: they can move at different rates, and in a given region they may even move in opposite directions for a time. A landscape can lose individuals while keeping its species list, or shift in composition while its overall extent holds steady. Collapsing these into a single number would hide exactly the trade-offs that conservation decisions turn on, which is why the [IPBES Global Assessment](https://www.ipbes.net/global-assessment) frames the state of biodiversity through a set of complementary measures.

## The main headline indicators

The Convention on Biological Diversity's Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, adopted in 2022, asks countries to report against a set of headline indicators drawn from this multi-dimensional reality. A few recur across most assessments.

- **Extinction risk.** [The Red List Index](/en/ecology/biodiversity/red-list-index-explained) aggregates the extinction-risk categories assigned by the [IUCN Red List](https://www.iucnredlist.org/) into a single trend for a group of species. As more species slide toward higher-risk categories, the index falls; the [Red List Index](/en/glossary/red-list-index) therefore reports the direction in which a group's overall threat status is heading.
- **Population abundance.** [The Living Planet Index](/en/ecology/biodiversity/living-planet-index-explained), compiled by the Zoological Society of London with WWF, summarises the average trend in monitored vertebrate populations. The [Living Planet Index](/en/glossary/living-planet-index) tracks how the abundance of populations is changing on average, which is a distinct question from how many species are at risk of disappearing.
- **Habitat and ecosystem structure.** Habitat-extent and forest-extent metrics measure the area and structure of the places species live, a dimension satellites can observe consistently at large scales.
- **Ecosystem condition.** The Biodiversity Intactness Index is a further composite, used to estimate how much of an area's original biological community remains relative to an undisturbed baseline.

Read together, these indicators describe different facets of the same system: whether species are slipping toward extinction, whether populations are thinning, whether habitat is shrinking, and whether communities still resemble their less-disturbed state.

## The measurements indicators rest on

Indicators are not raw observations; they are summaries built on underlying data. To keep those inputs comparable across countries and programmes, [Essential Biodiversity Variables](/en/ecology/biodiversity/essential-biodiversity-variables-monitoring) organise biodiversity measurements into six classes — genetic composition, species populations, species traits, community composition, ecosystem function, and ecosystem structure. This framework, coordinated by [GEO BON](https://geobon.org/), gives indicators a common foundation: a population-abundance indicator draws on the species-populations class, while a habitat-extent metric draws on ecosystem structure.

The practical value is traceability. When an indicator is anchored to a defined measurement class, it is clear which slice of biodiversity it represents and which it does not. That makes it possible to see where coverage is thin rather than to assume that an indicator speaks for biodiversity as a whole.

## How an indicator is constructed

The defining operation of any indicator is aggregation: many underlying measurements are combined into one trackable value. The Red List Index aggregates risk categories across the species in a group; the Living Planet Index averages population trends across many monitored populations; habitat metrics sum or compare areas of land cover.

Because aggregation is where information is both gained and lost, good practice is to be explicit about three things — what is being aggregated, which taxonomic group it covers, and which region it represents. An indicator labelled for birds in one region should not be read as a statement about all life everywhere. The reporting standards used under the [Convention on Biological Diversity](https://www.cbd.int/) and the methodological synthesis in the IPBES assessment both stress this kind of transparency, so that a published trend can be interpreted against its actual scope.

## Uncertainty and limits

Aggregation, the strength of an indicator, is also its main limitation. Combining many measurements into one value necessarily hides variation: a stable headline figure can sit on top of sharp declines in some groups offset by gains in others, and taxonomic and regional differences disappear into the average. An indicator is therefore most informative when its scope is stated and least informative when it is read as a verdict on biodiversity in general.

Coverage is uneven in a consistent direction. Most indicators lean toward well-studied groups — vertebrates in particular — and toward well-monitored regions such as Europe and North America, where long-running survey programmes are concentrated. Groups and places with thinner baselines are underrepresented in the data that indicators draw on, so a global figure carries more confidence for some parts of the world than others. This is also why independent indicators can disagree on the rate, or even the direction, of change in a region: they aggregate different inputs with different gaps. Read in parallel, that disagreement is itself useful information, flagging where the underlying monitoring is incomplete rather than implying that the trend is unknowable.
