Counting the species present at a site tells you something, but not whether the place still works the way an undisturbed example of its type would. The idea of [ecological integrity](/en/glossary/ecological-integrity) addresses that gap: it asks whether an ecosystem's composition, structure, and function fall within the natural range of variation expected for its kind. This piece walks through the composite indicators used to estimate integrity, how they are built, and why the choice of a reference state is the difficult part.

## What integrity means

Integrity is a condition statement rather than a single quantity. An ecosystem is described as having high integrity when its species composition, physical structure, and ecological processes remain close to what would be expected without substantial human disturbance — loosely, how intact it is relative to a minimally disturbed reference. That framing connects integrity to but distinguishes it from related concepts. Two sites with similar species lists can differ sharply in how their food webs, nutrient cycles, and disturbance regimes operate, and integrity is meant to capture that operational dimension alongside who is present.

The concept now sits inside formal monitoring architecture. Integrity maps onto the ecosystem-structure and ecosystem-function classes of the [Essential Biodiversity Variables](/en/ecology/biodiversity/essential-biodiversity-variables-monitoring) framework, the measurement scheme described by [GEO BON](https://geobon.org/). Ecosystem integrity also appears as an explicit goal in the Kunming-Montreal [Global Biodiversity Framework](https://www.cbd.int/), adopted in 2022 under the Convention on Biological Diversity, which is part of why standardized indicators have drawn renewed attention.

## The composite indicators

Because integrity bundles several properties together, it is estimated with composite indicators rather than measured directly. Several are in common use.

- **Biodiversity Intactness Index (BII)** estimates the average abundance of originally present species that remains at a location, expressed relative to an intact baseline. A high value indicates that most of the native assemblage persists; a low one indicates that abundances have shifted away from the reference.
- **Mean Species Abundance (MSA)** is a related measure built on the same logic of comparing present-day abundance to an undisturbed condition.
- **Indices of biotic integrity (IBI)**, widely used in freshwater monitoring, score a site from the composition of its fish or invertebrate assemblages, judged against the assemblage expected at a reference site of the same type.

Each compresses many observations into a single comparable score, which is what makes these indices useful for reporting under the [Global Assessment Report](https://www.ipbes.net/global-assessment) and similar synthesis efforts. The trade-off, discussed below, is that compression hides detail.

## How integrity is measured

The shared method behind these indicators is comparison. An integrity index takes the current state of a site, sets it against a reference or undisturbed condition for that ecosystem type, and expresses the gap between the two as one number. The reference is the anchor; everything else is measured as distance from it.

The inputs are usually layered. Field surveys supply direct records of which species are present and in what numbers. Species-distribution models extend those point observations across areas that were never visited, estimating what the assemblage would look like under current and reference conditions. Remote sensing adds a view of habitat condition — vegetation structure, extent, and change — at scales no field campaign can cover. Peer-reviewed work, including studies indexed in venues such as [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/), has refined how these strands are combined and how their respective errors propagate into the final score. For readers tracing how landscape pattern feeds these models, the related work on [habitat fragmentation metrics](/en/ecology/biodiversity/habitat-fragmentation-metrics) covers the spatial side in more depth.

## Why the reference state is the hard part

The central difficulty is not the arithmetic but the baseline. Defining "intact" requires choosing a reference condition, and that choice is partly a value judgement: how far back, which disturbances count as natural, and what counts as the undisturbed state for a given ecosystem are not settled by data alone. Different defensible choices can move a site's score in different directions.

This makes integrity indices vulnerable to the shifting-baseline problem, in which each generation of observers treats the conditions it inherited as normal. If the reference quietly tracks a progressively altered state, an ecosystem can appear to hold steady even as its longer-term condition declines. Being explicit about which baseline an index uses is therefore part of reading the number honestly. The same baseline sensitivity surfaces in [ecosystem resilience indicators](/en/ecology/biodiversity/ecosystem-resilience-indicators), where the reference against which recovery is judged matters as much as the measurement itself.

## Limitations and uncertainty

Several caveats travel with any integrity score. First, a composite index averages over many species and processes, so it can hide trade-offs: a stable headline value may conceal gains in some components offset by losses in others. Reading the underlying layers, not only the summary, is necessary to see what changed. Second, the modelled reference state carries its own error, since the undisturbed condition is itself estimated rather than observed, and that uncertainty flows through to the result. Third, the word "integrity" has several definitions in active use, so indicators that share a label may not be measuring quite the same thing, which complicates comparison across studies and regions.

None of this makes the indicators uninformative. It means they are best read as structured estimates with stated assumptions rather than fixed readings, and that the relationship between an intact assemblage and an ecosystem's capacity to keep functioning — its [ecosystem resilience](/en/glossary/ecosystem-resilience) — remains an area of continuing research. For the broader context of how these tools fit together, see the overview of [biodiversity monitoring and ecosystem health](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-monitoring-and-ecosystem-health).
