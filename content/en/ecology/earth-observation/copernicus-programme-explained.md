Copernicus is the European Union's [Earth-observation](https://www.copernicus.eu/en) programme, and it is best understood as two halves working together: a fleet of satellites that measure the planet, and a set of services that turn those measurements into information people can use. It is coordinated by the European Commission, with the European Space Agency responsible for the space component, and it runs on a "full, free and open" data policy. This piece sets out how the programme is organised, who operates each part, and what it produces.

## How Copernicus is organised

At the top sits the European Commission, which coordinates the programme as a whole. The space measurements come from a dedicated satellite fleet, built and operated on the EU's behalf, while the work of converting those signals into usable products is divided among six thematic services, each with its own operator. The result is a single programme of [earth observation](/en/glossary/earth-observation) with a clear separation between the instruments that observe and the services that interpret.

That structure is deliberate. A satellite reports a calibrated measurement; whether that measurement becomes an air-quality forecast, a land-cover map, or a flood extent depends on a downstream processing chain run by a specialised institution. Keeping those roles distinct makes it easier to trace where a given product came from and how it was produced.

## The space component

The space component is the set of satellites that supply the raw observations. Its centrepiece is the family of dedicated Sentinel missions, which between them provide radar, optical, ocean, atmospheric, and altimetry measurements. The European Space Agency [builds and operates](https://www.esa.int/Applications/Observing_the_Earth) these satellites for the EU. Copernicus also draws on data from other "contributing missions" — satellites not built for the programme but whose measurements extend or complement it.

Because each [Sentinel satellite](/en/glossary/sentinel-satellite) is tuned to a specific kind of measurement rather than acting as a general-purpose camera, the fleet as a whole covers land, ocean, ice, and the atmosphere. The companion article on [the Sentinel satellites](/en/ecology/earth-observation/sentinel-satellites-explained) describes what each family observes in more detail; here the point is simply that the space component is the common source feeding every service below it.

## The six thematic services

Six services translate the observations into thematic information. The Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) and the Climate Change Service (C3S) are both operated by the [ECMWF](https://www.ecmwf.int/); CAMS addresses air quality and atmospheric composition, while C3S supports climate work, including reanalysis. The Marine Environment Monitoring Service, run by Mercator Ocean International, describes the state of the sea.

On land and in crisis response, the [Land Monitoring Service](https://land.copernicus.eu/) — which involves the European Environment Agency and the Joint Research Centre — produces information on land cover and vegetation, and the Emergency Management Service, run by the Joint Research Centre, supplies flood and fire mapping when events occur. A sixth service covers security applications. Each service is the authoritative operator for its own domain, which is why the same satellite measurement can appear, processed differently, in several of them.

## What the programme delivers

In practice, Copernicus provides operational monitoring across a range of themes. CAMS underpins air-quality information; C3S supports climate reanalysis, of which ERA5 is a widely used example, a consistent reconstruction of past atmospheric conditions. The marine service reports on the ocean state, while the land service tracks land cover and vegetation and supports applications such as [land-cover change detection](/en/ecology/earth-observation/land-cover-change-detection) and [drought monitoring systems](/en/ecology/earth-observation/drought-monitoring-systems). The emergency service contributes flood and fire mapping during incidents.

Much of this output is free for anyone to use, in keeping with the open data policy. That openness is part of why Copernicus products turn up well beyond the programme's own walls — in national agencies, research, and the broader work of building [climate indicators and Earth-system monitoring](/en/ecology/climate-change/climate-indicators-earth-system-monitoring) from long, consistent records.

## Limits and uncertainty

The services are only as reliable as what feeds them. Each rests on underlying satellite and in-situ observations and on models, and every one of those carries its own error; a product is a combination of measurements and assumptions rather than a direct reading of the world. As with all remote sensing, the optical components are constrained by cloud, so persistently overcast regions yield fewer usable optical observations no matter how the data are later processed.

There is also a question of continuity. Because the programme depends on a working satellite fleet, sustaining its records over time depends on continued launches to replace and extend the missions in orbit. None of this diminishes the value of the products, but it does mean they are best read as estimates with stated uncertainty, firmest where independent observations agree. Seen together, the satellites, services, and open-data policy form one coordinated part of the wider [earth observation and remote sensing](/en/ecology/earth-observation/earth-observation-and-remote-sensing-explained) landscape, designed to keep monitoring running year after year.
