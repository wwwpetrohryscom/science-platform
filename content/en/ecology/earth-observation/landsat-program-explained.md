Among the instruments that watch the planet from orbit, one stands apart for its sheer length of service. [Landsat](/en/glossary/landsat) is a joint program of NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey, and since its first satellite reached orbit in 1972 it has produced an unbroken record of Earth's land surface — the longest such record in existence. This article traces how the program began, how its sensors work, why opening the archive changed scientific practice, and where the limits of the data lie.

## A mission that has outlasted its satellites

The program began in 1972 with a satellite originally named ERTS-1, and no single spacecraft has carried the record since. Instead, a succession of satellites has been launched so that one mission overlaps the next, keeping the observations continuous as older instruments age and fail. The continuity is a property of the series, not of any one machine.

Several missions illustrate the pattern. Landsat 5, launched in 1984, returned data for decades, far beyond its planned life. Landsat 7, launched in 1999, remains part of the story for a less fortunate reason discussed below. The more recent satellites carry updated instruments: Landsat 8, launched in 2013, flies the Operational Land Imager (OLI) and the Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS), and Landsat 9 followed in 2021. Each handover is planned so that the long view of the land is never interrupted, which is the program's central scientific purpose. NASA describes the science side of this partnership in its [Landsat science](https://science.nasa.gov/) materials.

## How Landsat sees the surface

Landsat is an optical and thermal Earth-observation system, and understanding it begins with [what remote sensing is](/en/ecology/earth-observation/what-is-remote-sensing): the instruments do not photograph features directly but record the radiation that surfaces reflect and emit, in defined wavelength bands, as a grid of pixels.

The spatial detail of that grid is what defines Landsat's role. Its multispectral bands image at about 30-metre resolution, a moderate scale that resolves fields, forest stands, and patches of development without aiming for individual objects. Later satellites add a sharper 15-metre panchromatic band, useful for visual sharpening, while the thermal-infrared bands record surface heat at a coarser resolution. Each satellite passes over a given location every 16 days; when two satellites operate together, the time between usable views of the same place falls to roughly eight days. That cadence sets both the strength and the constraint of the system — frequent enough to track seasonal change across a year, but not a continuous watch. The USGS documents these instruments and their bands in its [Landsat missions](https://www.usgs.gov/landsat-missions) overview.

## The 2008 decision that reshaped how the data is used

For much of the program's history, Landsat scenes were sold individually, which limited how many an analyst could practically assemble. That changed in 2008, when the USGS opened the entire Landsat archive for free public download.

The effect was less about any single image than about what became possible with many of them. Free access let researchers stack hundreds of scenes of the same place over many years and analyse the time series directly, rather than buy a handful of dates and reason from them. Long records of [land cover](/en/glossary/land-cover) — the physical material covering each pixel, such as forest, water, or cropland — could now be built and compared across decades. Studies of change that had been impractical at scale became routine, and the archive's value as a multi-decade resource grew accordingly. NASA Earthdata describes how the [Landsat data](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/) are accessed and documented today.

## What the record is used for

Because Landsat combines moderate resolution with a long, consistent archive, it supports a broad set of land applications. Analysts use it for land-cover mapping, for monitoring agriculture and water, and for tracking forests and deforestation over time.

A recurring use is [land-cover change detection](/en/ecology/earth-observation/land-cover-change-detection): classifying the surface at different dates and comparing the results to see where forest has become cleared ground, where water bodies have shifted, or where built land has expanded. Detecting change reliably depends on having comparable images across years, which is exactly what a continuous, cross-calibrated series is built to provide. These land-surface measurements also feed wider Earth-system questions, including the vegetation and disturbance records that inform research on [carbon-cycle feedbacks](/en/ecology/climate-change/carbon-cycle-feedbacks). Within the satellite fleet, Landsat's moderate-resolution land record is complementary to faster-revisiting systems such as [the Sentinel satellites](/en/ecology/earth-observation/sentinel-satellites-explained), and the two are often combined. The peer-reviewed methods behind these applications appear in journals such as [Remote Sensing](https://www.mdpi.com/journal/remotesensing).

## Where the data falls short

A long, valuable record is not a flawless one, and using Landsat well means knowing what the images cannot deliver. Three limitations recur.

The first is coverage in time. With a 16-day revisit, and with clouds frequently blocking the optical view, any given location has gaps — sometimes long ones in persistently cloudy or tropical regions — where no clear image exists for a date of interest.

The second is a specific instrument fault. In 2003, Landsat 7's Scan Line Corrector failed, and imagery acquired after that date carries wedge-shaped gaps, often described as striping, where parts of each scene are missing. The data remain usable with care, but the defect has to be accounted for rather than ignored.

The third is consistency across the series. Because the record is stitched from successive satellites with differing instruments, joining them into one coherent archive requires careful cross-calibration. Without it, a change of sensor could masquerade as a change on the ground, so the apparent signal would be an artefact rather than a measurement. Maintaining that calibration is part of what makes the continuous record trustworthy.

## Reading Landsat in context

Landsat occupies a particular niche within [earth observation and remote sensing](/en/ecology/earth-observation/earth-observation-and-remote-sensing-explained): a moderate-resolution, openly available, decades-long view of the land, sustained by a deliberately overlapping series of satellites. Its strength is the depth of its archive; its constraints are revisit gaps, the post-2003 Landsat 7 striping, and the cross-calibration needed to hold the record together. Read with those caveats in mind, the program offers something no shorter mission can — a consistent way to ask how the land has changed across half a century, and to keep asking as new satellites extend the record.
