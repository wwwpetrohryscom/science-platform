A trophic level is a property of a feeding relationship, not a label that belongs to an organism. The number counts how many transfers separate a unit of energy from the sunlight that fixed it: photosynthesisers sit at level 1, something that eats only photosynthesisers sits at level 2, and so on. Nothing in that definition obliges an animal to stay in one place. A trout eating insects that grazed algae and also insects that ate other insects is feeding at levels 3 and 4 simultaneously, and the value ecologists assign it will be an average of the two, weighted by diet.

That is the first thing to hold on to, because most of the confusion downstream comes from treating the levels as compartments with organisms inside them. The broader case for reading an ecosystem as a set of flows rather than a set of occupants is made in the [flow-based definition of an ecosystem](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem); what follows is about the arithmetic of the transfers, and about how little of that arithmetic is measured rather than assumed. The energy entering at the base is a separate problem, treated in [how primary production is estimated](/en/ecology/ecosystems/primary-production-and-energy-flow).

## The ten per cent rule and the width of its error bars

The idea that roughly a tenth of the energy at one level reaches the next descends from the trophic-dynamic framework Raymond Lindeman published in 1942, which recast a community as a sequence of energy transfers with a loss at each step. The rule survives in the current literature as a stated rule of thumb: a [seven-year warming experiment in artificial ponds](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03352-2) opens by noting that, as a general rule, about 10 per cent of the energy produced at one level makes it to the next.

The same experiment is a good illustration of how soft that general rule is. Relative to ambient conditions, 4 °C of warming decreased trophic transfer efficiency by up to 56 per cent, and the biomass of both phytoplankton and zooplankton was lower in the warmed ponds. A [review of transfer efficiency in marine systems](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33097289/) is blunter still: efficiency is an emergent, unitless property that is difficult to measure, responds dynamically to environmental and ecosystem change, is affected by fishing, and is expected to decline under warming.

Three losses sit behind any efficiency figure: production that is never eaten, ingested material that is not assimilated, and assimilated energy respired away to stay alive. Their proportions differ between an insect and a mammal, which is why one planetary constant was never plausible.

Why that matters is a matter of exponents. Apply 10 per cent four times and a fourth-order consumer receives one ten-thousandth of what the producers fixed. Apply 5 per cent instead and the same consumer receives about one part in 160,000 — a sixteen-fold difference in what the system can support, produced by a parameter that nobody measures directly at ecosystem scale. Two things follow. Real webs have few levels, because there is nothing left to support another one. And any estimate of what a top predator population can sustain is far more sensitive to the assumed efficiency than to almost anything else in the calculation.

## Fractional levels are a result, not a fudge

Because diets mix, published trophic levels are almost never integers. An [analysis of seven marine ecosystem models](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0043966) put the mean trophic level of marine mammal consumption at 2.88 against 3.42 for fisheries catches in the same systems. Neither value names a rung. Each is a diet-weighted average: 3.42 says that, averaged over what was landed, the catch sits about 2.42 transfers above the photosynthesisers.

The web is also not only a chain of things eaten alive. A large share of what plants and algae fix is never grazed at all; it dies, falls, and enters as detritus, where decomposers and their consumers run a parallel set of transfers with their own efficiencies. In terrestrial systems that channel is often the larger of the two, which is why [the soil food web](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web) is not a footnote to the aboveground one. A diagram with arrows pointing steadily upwards misrepresents where most of the energy actually goes.

## An indicator that came loose from the thing it indexes

The clearest case of a trophic number being over-read is the mean trophic level of fisheries catches. A falling value has been widely interpreted as evidence that fisheries are working their way down the web after depleting predators. A [global comparison of catch, survey and stock-assessment data](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature09528) found that catch-based mean trophic level does not reliably predict changes in marine ecosystems, and that its trends often diverge from ecosystem mean trophic level derived from trawl surveys and assessments. Contrary to the earlier picture of rapid decline, that work reported recent increases in catch, survey and assessment values alike.

The right model is not that the indicator lies but that it measures the fishery, not the ecosystem. Catch composition responds to markets, gear, quota and where boats can afford to go. The same analysis noted that catches were rising across most trophic levels, a pattern that can intensify collapses even while the mean sits still or climbs. Where the underlying question is abundance, abundance is what has to be measured; the productivity side of that argument is developed in [marine food webs and productivity](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity).

## Yellowstone as evidence rather than parable

The reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone in the mid-1990s is the standard classroom illustration of a trophic cascade: predators suppress elk, elk browse less, willow and aspen recover. The published record supports something in that direction and is considerably less tidy about its size.

A [2022 analysis in Ecology Letters](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34748261/) showed that the long-standing practice of sampling only the tallest young plants in a stand overestimated overstory aspen regeneration by a factor of four to seven compared with random sampling, because it favoured plants already taller than the height elk prefer to browse and skipped stands that were not regenerating at all. Random sampling still described a cascade — it was weaker than the one the older method described. The subsequent exchange of comment and reply in the same journal argued over the magnitude and the sampling design, not over whether any effect exists.

Two other lines complicate the behavioural half of the story. [Fecal analysis of elk diet in the Gallatin Canyon](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/08-2017.1) found that willow consumption went up, not down, when wolves were present, and that snow conditions affected it more strongly than predators did — pointing to the fall in elk numbers rather than a change in individual behaviour as the operative mechanism. And a [decade-long experiment on riparian recovery](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2012.2977) found that moderating browsing alone did not restore willow along small streams; recovery also required the hydrological conditions that prevailed before predator removal, because seventy years without wolves had altered the disturbance regime in ways the reintroduction did not reverse. Predator restoration, on that evidence, does not simply run the removal backwards — a caution that carries directly into [the evidence base for rewilding](/en/ecology/conservation/rewilding-evidence-and-limits).

## Why these models stay under-determined

Quantitative food-web models make the accounting explicit, and in doing so expose how much of it is supplied by assumption. A mass-balance model needs, for every group it represents, a biomass, a production rate, a consumption rate and a diet composition. Field data rarely constrain all four, so values are borrowed from comparable systems or tuned until the budget closes — and many different diet matrices can close the same budget equally well. NOAA Fisheries, which runs such models operationally, describes using them [alongside other resource management models for multiple-model inference](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/ecosystems/ecosystem-modeling), specifically to handle uncertainty and bias rather than to resolve them.

The interaction data themselves are the binding constraint. Work on predicting network structure across space notes that knowledge of how ecological networks vary at global scales [remains limited by the difficulty of acquiring repeated spatial data on species interactions](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2023.0166), which is why current approaches build a regional pool of possible interactions and then downscale it probabilistically rather than observing local webs directly. Aggregating species into functional groups eases the data problem and imports a strong assumption about substitutability — precisely the assumption examined in [foundation species and the myth of redundancy](/en/ecology/ecosystems/foundation-species-and-the-myth-of-redundancy).

None of this makes trophic structure a bad idea. Levels remain the cheapest way to ask whether a system can plausibly support what is being taken from it, and stable-isotope work has made position estimates far more empirical than diet tables alone allowed. The honest use is comparative and directional: this system transfers energy less efficiently than that one, this population sits higher than that one. Read as a fixed ladder with fixed rungs and a fixed ten per cent between them, the framework gives back numbers with a precision the underlying measurements never had.
