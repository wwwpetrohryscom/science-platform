A city is [an ecosystem by the flow-based definition](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem), with distinctive energy and water budgets, and the [heat island](/en/ecology/ecosystems/urban-ecology-explained) is the clearest expression of both. It is not a small effect and it is not evenly distributed in time.

## The size of it

The EPA states the magnitude for the United States: "the heat island effect results in daytime temperatures in urban areas about 1–7°F higher than temperatures in outlying areas and nighttime temperatures about 2–5°F higher."

The daytime range is wider and the nighttime range is narrower and higher at its floor, and it is the nighttime figure that carries most of the health consequence. Recovery from heat stress happens overnight; a city that does not cool below a threshold denies its residents that recovery, and the physiological effect of consecutive hot nights is larger than that of a single hot afternoon.

## Where it comes from

The EPA identifies the contributing factors, and every one of them is a modification of the surface energy balance rather than an addition of heat.

**Reduced vegetation.** Trees and vegetated surfaces cool by shading and by evapotranspiration — water evaporating from leaves and soil converts absorbed solar energy into latent heat rather than into sensible heating of the air. Replacing vegetation with impervious surface removes that pathway, which is why the effect is fundamentally about [where the absorbed energy goes](/en/physics/climate-physics/humidity-and-why-heat-and-moisture-combine) rather than about how much arrives.

**Material properties.** Human-made surfaces absorb more solar radiation and store more heat, releasing it after sunset. This is the direct mechanism of the nighttime component.

**Urban geometry.** Building spacing changes wind flow and traps outgoing longwave radiation between surfaces that see each other rather than the sky.

**Anthropogenic heat.** Vehicles, industry and air conditioning add heat directly. Air conditioning is a feedback: cooling indoor spaces rejects heat outdoors, warming the city that made cooling necessary.

**Weather.** Clear, calm conditions intensify the effect; wind and cloud suppress it, which is why heat islands are strongest during exactly the heatwave conditions that make them most dangerous.

## The consequences the EPA quantifies

**Energy.** Electricity demand for air conditioning rises "1–9% for each 2°F increase in temperature" — a substantial elasticity, and one that makes [cooling demand a grid problem](/en/physics/energy/cooling-demand-and-electricity) as much as a comfort one.

**Mortality.** The EPA records "an average of 702 heat deaths per year" in the United States from 2004 to 2018. That is a count from death certificates and, like every [heat mortality](/en/ecology/climate-change/heat-mortality-and-what-the-records-count) figure derived that way, it is a lower bound rather than an estimate.

**Water.** Urban streams experienced "temperature surges as much as 18°F higher" than forested streams — runoff heated by pavement entering a channel over a short interval, which is a shock rather than a shift.

## Why it is tractable

Unlike most [climate impacts](/en/ecology/climate-change/heat-limits-and-the-wet-bulb-threshold), the mechanisms here are local and the interventions act on the same terms in the energy balance that produce the problem: shade, evapotranspiration, and surface reflectivity. Each has been measured, each works, and each has limits — reflective surfaces do less at night, and trees need water that a hot dry city may not have.

That last point is the interesting constraint. Evaporative cooling is the mechanism, and it costs water at the moment when water is scarcest, which makes the intervention and its limitation the same physical process.
