Almost everything a plant builds is eventually taken apart. Herbivory removes a minority of primary production in most terrestrial ecosystems; the rest enters the decomposer pathway as dead leaves, roots, wood and exudates, and it is that pathway — not grazing — which returns carbon to the atmosphere and nitrogen and phosphorus to a form roots can use again. It is the closing leg of the cycle that makes [an ecosystem](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem) a system rather than a one-way flow. The measured quantity in that sentence is production, and it is covered in [primary production and energy flow](/en/ecology/ecosystems/primary-production-and-energy-flow). Decomposition is the return leg, and it is measured much less well.

The reason is structural. Production has an instrument — a tower measuring gas exchange above a canopy — that integrates a whole ecosystem continuously. Decomposition has no such instrument. What it has instead is a family of field manipulations: put a known mass of plant material in a known place, come back later, weigh what is left. Everything the literature says about global controls on decomposition rests on that design, and the design's assumptions are worth being explicit about before the results are.

## What sets the rate

Two variables dominate. The first is climate, principally temperature and moisture, which govern the metabolic rate of the organisms doing the work. The second is the chemistry of the material itself: litter high in nitrogen and low in structural compounds is dismantled quickly, while litter dominated by lignified cell walls resists. Neither is a surprise, and neither is controversial.

What has been contested is the third factor — the contribution of soil animals, as distinct from microbes. Microorganisms carry out the enzymatic breakdown; the animal fauna fragments material, moves it, and alters the microbial community that colonises it. Whether that animal contribution is large enough to matter at the scale of a biome, and whether it matters equally everywhere, was a hypothesis for three decades before it was tested at global scale.

The test came from a coordinated litter-bag experiment run across 30 sites on six continents, published in *Global Change Biology*. Its finding was conditional rather than universal: animals in the mesofaunal size range "increase decomposition rates in temperate and wet tropical climates, but have neutral effects where temperature or moisture constrain biological activity." That is a direct test of a prediction made by Swift and colleagues in 1979, that the relative contribution of soil fauna to decomposition varies by climatic region — and the first time it had been examined experimentally at that scale.

A later meta-analysis put a magnitude on the same effect. Across studies at both global and biome scales, soil fauna "consistently enhanced litter decomposition," with an average increment of about 27 per cent, and — this is the part that matters for modelling — climate and litter quality modulated that fauna effect *differently between biomes*, ranging from biomes where climate drove the effect directly to biomes where the climate effect ran through changes in litter quality. A single global coefficient for the fauna contribution would therefore be wrong nearly everywhere.

## Where the animals matter most

A field experiment in tropical rainforest sharpened the picture further. Excluding soil fauna reduced decomposition, with fauna enhancing the rate of litter decomposition by 22.6 per cent, and that contribution "consistently increased as nutrient availability in the microenvironment declined." The authors' interpretation is the interesting one: soil fauna "is able to equalize differences in microbial decomposition potential, thus buffering to a remarkable extent nutrient shortages at an ecosystem level."

That is a functional claim about redundancy with an edge to it. Where nutrients are abundant, microbes alone can do the job and removing the animals changes little. Where nutrients are scarce, the animal community is what keeps the cycle turning at all — which means the fauna contribution is largest exactly where the ecosystem has least margin. The general question of whether ecological function is safely duplicated is taken up in [foundation species and the myth of redundancy](/en/ecology/ecosystems/foundation-species-and-the-myth-of-redundancy); this is a case where the apparent redundancy is real in one context and absent in another.

## The measurement, and what it quietly includes

Litter-bag mass loss is the near-universal method, and its standardised form is the tea-bag approach: bury commercial tea of two contrasting qualities, one labile and one recalcitrant, recover them after a fixed interval, and derive a decay rate and a stabilisation term from the mass lost. The appeal is obvious — the substrate is identical everywhere, so sites become comparable in a way that native-litter studies never are.

The problem is that mass loss is not the same thing as decomposition. Water removes soluble compounds from plant material by simple leaching, with no biology involved. A study in *Ecology and Evolution* measured that directly and found rapid initial leaching from both tea types within the first three to four hours, plateauing quickly, before microbial colonisation could plausibly have begun. Its quantitative conclusion is severe for wet sites: "up to 30–50% of the mass loss of green tea reported as decomposition could be lost through leaching alone in high moisture environments (>90% [soil moisture](/en/ecology/soils/soil-moisture-and-what-a-measurement-covers) and submerged)," and failing to account for it "will lead to a systematic overestimation of the microbial decomposition in wet habitats."

This is worth stating plainly because of what it does to comparisons. A method that overestimates decomposition specifically in wet places will exaggerate the apparent moisture control on decomposition — the very relationship these datasets are most often used to establish. The bias is not random noise that averages out across a global network; it is aligned with one of the two variables under study.

Used carefully, though, the standardised approach does what native-litter studies cannot. A survey across 30 tidal-wetland sites worldwide, spanning a temperature gradient of 10.9 to 28.5 °C, used identical tea litter to separate the decay rate from the stabilisation term — and found the two behaved quite differently. Effects on "decomposition rate per se were minor", while organic-matter stabilisation "was 29 % lower in low, more frequently flooded vs. high, less frequently flooded zones", declined "by ∼ 75 % over the studied temperature gradient", and fell "by > 70 %" under simulated coastal eutrophication. Because it is the stabilised fraction, not the rate, that determines how much carbon a wetland retains, a study reporting only mass loss would have concluded that very little was happening.

## What decomposition returns, and to where

The output of the process is not a single flux. Some of the carbon leaves as CO₂ within days to months. Some is incorporated into microbial biomass and then into the mineral-associated pool, where it may persist for decades or centuries; that fraction, and how badly it is measured, is the subject of [soil carbon measurement and its uncertainty](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty). Nitrogen and phosphorus follow their own paths — immobilised into microbial tissue while the substrate is nutrient-poor, mineralised into plant-available forms once the microbial demand is satisfied — which is why the [availability of soil nutrients](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-availability-and-soil-fertility) is governed by decomposer physiology rather than by total stocks.

The community doing all this is described in [the soil food web](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web). Its relevance here is that it is the least catalogued major community on land, which means the fauna effects above are measured as an aggregate — animals present against animals excluded — rather than attributed to identified species.

## What would change the picture

Three things, in order of tractability. First, routine correction for leaching, or a protocol that separates the abiotic and biotic components of early mass loss; the correction exists and is not universally applied. Second, biome-specific fauna coefficients in decomposition models rather than a global constant, which the meta-analytic result argues for explicitly. Third, and hardest, a method that observes decomposition in place rather than in a bag — because a bag excludes the largest fauna by mesh size, alters the moisture regime of what it contains, and presents a substrate that no longer sits where the plant dropped it.

None of that is a reason to discount the existing results. The direction and rough magnitude of the fauna effect have now been established by a coordinated global experiment and confirmed by independent meta-analysis, which is a stronger evidence base than most ecosystem-scale processes have. It is a reason to treat the absolute rates as method-dependent, and to be sceptical of any global decomposition figure quoted without one.
