A city has a radiation budget, a water balance, a nutrient budget and a species pool. Each of those can be instrumented, and each behaves differently from the landscape the city replaced — which makes an urban area a legitimate object for the same accounting used on [any other ecosystem defined by its flows](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem), rather than a gap in the map where ecology stops. The interesting results in the field are mostly about how much the accounting changes, and how easily the measurement method decides the answer.

## Two quantities, one name

The urban heat island is usually quoted as a single number, but two distinct physical quantities carry the label. A **surface urban heat island** is a radiometric temperature — what a thermal sensor records looking down at roofs, roads and canopy tops. A **canopy-layer heat island** is an air temperature, taken between ground level and roof height, which is the quantity a person standing on a pavement experiences.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency describes the surface effect as forming because paved and roofed surfaces absorb and re-emit more heat than most natural cover; on a warm day conventional roofing can sit as much as 66 °F (about 37 °C) above the surrounding air temperature. The air-temperature difference is far smaller: the agency gives daytime urban air as roughly 1–7 °F (about 0.6–3.9 °C) above outlying areas and night-time air as about 2–5 °F (1.1–2.8 °C) above.

| Quantity | What the instrument records | How it is obtained | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surface heat island | Radiometric temperature of the visible surface | Thermal infrared from satellite or aircraft | Large by day, strongly patterned, sensitive to overpass time |
| Canopy-layer heat island | Air temperature from ground level to roof height | Fixed stations, vehicle traverses, crowdsourced sensors | Small by day, relatively larger at night |
| Modelled temperature field | Neither — a simulation constrained by observations | Urban climate models run with observed inputs | Fills gaps where no field data exist; inherits the model's assumptions |

The gap between the first two is not a rounding difference. An analysis pairing satellite land-surface temperature with more than 50,000 crowdsourced citizen weather stations across 342 European urban clusters found a mean surface heat island of 1.45 °C against a mean canopy heat island of 0.26 °C over July 2019, a month that contained a severe heat wave — roughly a sixfold overestimate if the satellite figure is read as an air temperature. The daytime split was starker still, at 2.00 °C surface against 0.05 °C air, while at night the two converged, at 0.90 °C against 0.47 °C. The surface value exceeded the air value in 96 per cent of those cities by day and 80 per cent at night, with vegetation and evaporative cooling doing most of the work in decoupling them.

That result has a direct editorial consequence. A claim that a given city runs several degrees hotter than its surroundings is often a surface measurement being reported as though it were an air temperature. Both numbers are real; they answer different questions, and only one of them is about what a body outdoors is exposed to.

## Why one city cannot be compared with another casually

Even holding the quantity fixed, a heat island magnitude depends on choices the analyst makes. The intensity is a difference between an urban value and a reference, so it moves with how the urban boundary is drawn, what counts as the rural comparison, which season and hour are sampled, and how cloud-contaminated pixels are screened. A compact city surrounded by irrigated farmland and a sprawling city surrounded by dry scrub will produce different numbers for reasons that have little to do with the cities.

This is the ordinary problem of any satellite-derived index rather than a defect peculiar to urban work, and the general form of it is covered in [the limits of remote-sensing products](/en/ecology/earth-observation/remote-sensing-limitations-and-uncertainty). Its urban version is worse in one respect: the crowdsourced networks that supply the air-temperature side are dense where people buy private weather stations, which is not a random sample of the world's cities. Comparative urban climatology is therefore better read as a set of within-city results than as a global ranking.

## Sealed ground rewrites the water balance

The most complete ecological change a city makes is to the ground itself. Copernicus imperviousness products processed by the European Environment Agency put the sealed area of the EU-27 at 110,702 km² in 2018, or 2.7 per cent of the territory, an increase of 3,606 km² — 3.4 per cent — since 2006, of which 1,383 km² came out of cropland. Getting to a comparable series took work the headline figure hides. The 2018 imperviousness layer was produced at 10 m resolution against 20 m for the earlier ones, and the finer layer resolves so much more built structure that the raw sequence implies, in the agency's own word, unrealistic growth; the published series is a harmonised and bias-corrected reconstruction built to remove that step. Checked against sampled reference data, the imperviousness product still appears to underestimate sealed area across Europe, so the figure is better read as a floor than as a measurement.

Hydrologically, sealing converts a distributed, slow pathway into a concentrated, fast one. Water that would have infiltrated, recharged groundwater and returned to streams through seeps instead reaches storm drains within minutes, which is why the U.S. Geological Survey links watershed development to more frequent and more severe flooding rather than merely to more water. The ecological thresholds are lower than the flooding thresholds. Reviewing effective and total impervious area against stream condition, the EPA's diagnostic framework reports consistent channel instability above about 10 per cent effective impervious area, uniformly low summer baseflow above about 40 per cent total impervious area, sharp declines in macroinvertebrate diversity between 8 and 12 per cent total impervious area, and the absence of brook trout above roughly 4 per cent. Streams are degraded by urbanisation well before a catchment looks urban, and the same [land cover](/en/glossary/land-cover) class that reads as "low-density residential" spans much of that range. The catchment-scale consequences connect directly to [how water moves through the wider system](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained).

## Who actually lives in cities

The species pool changes in a way that is easy to caricature in either direction. A global comparison assembling bird records from 54 cities and plant records from 110 — described by its authors as the largest such dataset for these two taxa — found that urban areas hold about 8 per cent of the native bird species density and about 25 per cent of the native plant species density expected for non-urban land — a statement about species per unit area, not about totals, and not a claim that cities are empty. The same analysis found that current density and the loss in density were better explained by anthropogenic variables, principally land cover and city age, than by geography, climate or topography, and that cities nonetheless retain endemic native species. That combination matters: it means urban biotas are shaped by decisions, and that the decisions are legible in the data.

Interpreting those numbers needs the same care as any count. Density is sensitive to the boundary drawn and the effort spent, in the way set out for [species richness as a measure](/en/ecology/biodiversity/species-richness-explained), and an urban area is a mosaic of fragments whose arrangement — not just their total extent — governs what persists, which is the territory of [habitat fragmentation metrics](/en/ecology/biodiversity/habitat-fragmentation-metrics).

## Convergence in what gets seen, not in what lives there

The standard expectation is that cities converge: shared horticultural palettes, shared commensal animals and shared built structures should make distant cities resemble each other more than each resembles its own hinterland. Tested against 66,209 citizen-science observations of 5,209 species from 14 US metropolitan areas, that expectation received qualified support at best. Only 100 species — 1.9 per cent of the total richness recorded — turned up in eight or more of the cities, though those cosmopolitan species accounted for 21.4 per cent of all observations. Regional context explained community composition better than urbanisation intensity did, and the most intensely developed areas produced the communities least predictable from their region rather than the most similar to each other.

Read carefully, that is two findings, not one. A small set of widespread species dominates what is commonly seen in cities, which is what people notice and what generates the impression of sameness; the underlying species lists remain regionally distinct. Convergence in abundance is not convergence in composition, and studies that measure one and report the other will keep disagreeing.

## What urban ecology still measures badly

Start with what is under the pavement. Urban soils are physically unlike the soils that survey classifications were designed around — truncated, compacted, mixed with construction debris, frequently buried under pavement — and belowground processes in cities are correspondingly less quantified than the aboveground ones. Night-time and seasonal behaviour is under-sampled relative to clear-sky daytime satellite overpasses, even though the canopy heat island is largest at night. And the built-up fraction that ecological work concentrates on excludes most of what a city consumes — cities are supplied by [managed land elsewhere](/en/ecology/ecosystems/agricultural-ecosystems-and-agroecology), and an ecosystem budget drawn at the municipal boundary balances more neatly than the physical system does.

None of this makes cities ecologically uninteresting. It makes them systems whose measurement conventions have to be stated before their numbers mean anything — which is a description of most environmental measurement, arrived at unusually quickly.
