Farmland behaves like an ecosystem that has been stopped early and held there. Tillage, weeding and harvest repeatedly reset the community to a stage dominated by fast-growing, high-light species — the opening move of [any secondary succession](/en/ecology/ecosystems/ecological-succession-explained) — and the reset is paid for from outside the field, in nutrients, in fuel, in water and in labour. Read that way, an agroecosystem is not a degraded natural system but [an ecosystem with an unusual boundary condition](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem): almost everything the community would normally recycle internally is instead imported, and almost all of the accumulated biomass is exported once a year.

Farming is rare among ecological subjects in that most of its flows are already written down somewhere. Fertiliser is bought, harvests are weighed, hectares are registered, so quantities that ecologists elsewhere have to model can here simply be looked up. The awkward terms are the ones nobody invoices: what leaks out of the field, and what arrives in it for free.

## The size of the managed area

FAO's land statistics put world agricultural land at 4,800 million hectares in 2023, more than a third of the global land area, split roughly 1,600 million hectares of cropland against 3,200 million hectares of permanent meadows and pastures. The direction of travel since 2001 is less obvious than the headline: cropland expanded by about 80 million hectares, a 5 per cent increase, while permanent pasture contracted by around 150 million hectares, giving a small net 2 per cent contraction in agricultural land overall.

Meanwhile the area per person fell. Cropland per capita dropped roughly 20 per cent, from 0.24 to 0.19 hectares, over the same period, while land productivity rose by more than 60 per cent. Output per hectare has been doing the work that additional hectares used to do, which is the single fact underlying every argument about how farmland and wild habitat should be arranged.

## Subsidy: what has to be pushed in

A field harvested every year exports nutrients in grain, straw and forage that a natural community would have retained and recycled on site. Where that export outruns what the soil releases by weathering and mineralisation, the deficit is closed with fertiliser. FAO's fertiliser statistics record global agricultural use of nitrogen at 112 million tonnes in 2023, a 32 per cent rise since 2002; phosphorus at 41 million tonnes, up 20 per cent; and potassium at 38 million tonnes, up 62 per cent and the fastest-growing of the three. Across all three nutrients the total moved from 142 million tonnes in 2002 to 190 million tonnes in 2023.

Per unit area, that is 68 kg of nitrogen per hectare of cropland in 2023 against 54 kg in 2002, with phosphorus at 26 kg and potassium at 23 kg. No natural community supplies nitrogen to itself at anything like that rate. A wild system of comparable productivity draws on biological fixation and on tight internal recycling within [the nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained), and the slowness of those routes is part of what sets its growth rate. Industrial fixation removes that constraint and substitutes a supply chain for it.

## Leakage: what does not stay

Nutrients applied are not nutrients taken up. FAO's cropland nutrient balance for 2023 gives a global surplus of 85 million tonnes of nitrogen — the input that crops did not remove — equivalent to 54 kg of nitrogen per hectare of cropland, alongside a phosphorus surplus of 7 million tonnes, or 4 kg per hectare. Surplus is not synonymous with pollution; some of it accumulates in soil organic matter. But the fraction that moves does so into groundwater, watercourses and the atmosphere, and the pathway is set by soil texture, rainfall timing and the state of the soil's biological community, which is one reason [identical practices give different results on different farms](/en/ecology/ecosystems/soil-microbiome-regenerative-agriculture).

The global figure also hides two opposite problems. The same dataset shows Asia carrying the highest nitrogen surplus per hectare since the 1990s and Europe falling below the world average per hectare during the 2000s, while Africa records a low nitrogen surplus together with phosphorus and potassium deficits. A deficit is a soil being mined; a surplus is a watershed being loaded. A single global average sits between two failure modes that require opposite corrections.

## The input nobody buys

One major input arrives without an invoice. FAO's assessment of pollination services reports that three-quarters of the world's most productive crop plants depend at least in part on animal pollinators, that around 35 per cent of global crop production by volume is affected by them, and that over 80 per cent of flowering plant species are animal-pollinated. The pollinator fauna doing this is not small — roughly 200,000 animal species, including more than 25,000 species of bee.

The phrase to hold onto is *at least in part*. Dependence is a gradient, not a switch: the cereal staples are wind- or self-pollinated, while many fruits, nuts, oilseeds and vegetables are strongly pollinator-dependent. That is why the volume share and the crop-species share differ so much, and why claims that pollinator loss threatens a fixed percentage of the food supply usually collapse two distinct measures into one. The 2016 intergovernmental assessment on pollinators, pollination and food production remains the reference treatment, and it is also the clearest statement that the service is delivered by wild communities the farm does not manage — the classic case in the wider [ecosystem services framework](/en/ecology/ecosystems/ecosystem-services-and-human-wellbeing).

## Sparing or sharing, and why the evidence is thinner than the debate

If yield per hectare has risen while area has not, the obvious question is whether biodiversity is better served by farming intensively on less land and protecting the rest, or by farming less intensively over a wider area that retains habitat. The two positions are usually called land sparing and land sharing, and the argument has run for two decades.

A 2025 systematic review in *PNAS Nexus* went looking for studies that actually compare them. It found 57 peer-reviewed articles covering 105 cases, of which only 17 studies and 27 cases permitted a direct comparison of the two strategies. Among those higher-quality cases, context-specific combinations of the two performed best in 52 per cent, land sparing performed best in 41 per cent, and land sharing performed best in 7 per cent. The authors' conclusion is worth stating plainly: the empirical basis is sparse, and it does not support a general claim that either strategy is better.

That is a result about the state of the evidence rather than about farming. Twenty-seven comparable cases is a thin foundation for a global land-use principle, and the outcome also depends on which taxa are counted and at what scale — a point that recurs wherever [conservation priorities are set from species counts](/en/ecology/biodiversity/why-species-counts-mislead-conservation).

## What diversification has and has not been shown to do

Agroecology, as FAO frames it, is a system-redesign approach built on ten interlinked elements — diversity, synergies, recycling, resilience and efficiency among the ecological ones, alongside human and social values, culture and food traditions, co-creation of knowledge, responsible governance and circular economies. The ecological half of that list makes empirically testable predictions, and it has been tested at scale.

A second-order meta-analysis published in *Science Advances* reviewed 98 meta-analyses covering 5,160 original studies and 41,946 comparisons between diversified and simplified cropping practices. Diversification enhanced biodiversity, pollination, pest control, nutrient cycling, soil fertility and water regulation without compromising crop yields overall. The effects sorted by target: practices acting aboveground improved pest control and water regulation, while practices acting belowground improved nutrient cycling, soil fertility and water regulation. Restoring predator and parasitoid guilds is the mechanism behind the pest-control result, which depends on the same [trophic structure](/en/ecology/ecosystems/food-webs-and-trophic-structure) that simplification removes.

Three qualifications belong with that finding. The result is a mean across a heterogeneous set of practices, crops and climates, and the same analysis reports substantial variability in responses and genuine trade-offs, so context-dependency is part of the finding rather than a caveat appended to it. A meta-analysis of meta-analyses also inherits whatever publication bias sits in its inputs, and comparisons are between diversified and simplified versions of a system, not against an absolute standard. And "without compromising yield" is measured per hectare in field trials, which is not the same accounting as output per farm, per unit of labour, or per unit of input cost — the accountings that determine whether a practice is adopted.

So the subject is known to very different depths in its different parts. Nitrogen and land are counted to the tonne and the hectare; pollination is well characterised but supplied by communities no farm manages; and the spatial question — protected land beside intensive fields, or a softer mosaic across the whole landscape — still rests on twenty-seven comparable cases. Diversification is the one intervention with a large evidence base behind it, and even there the mean is carrying weight that a particular farm, crop and climate may not.
