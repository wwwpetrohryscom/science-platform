A burned hillside, a retreating glacier front, an abandoned field: each will be recolonised, and for much of the twentieth century the sequence was thought to be predictable enough to name in advance. That expectation — a site passing through a fixed series of stages toward a stable endpoint set by regional climate — organised a great deal of plant ecology. It did not survive the long-term records. What replaced it is looser, more contingent, and considerably more useful to anyone deciding whether a damaged site is on its way back.

The phenomenon itself is not in doubt. Vegetation and the animal communities that depend on it do change in reasonably ordered ways after a disturbance, driven by the same processes that make [an ecosystem a set of flows rather than a place](/en/ecology/ecosystems/what-is-an-ecosystem): energy capture, nutrient accumulation, and the physical modification of a site by whatever is living on it. What has gone is the claim that those processes have one destination.

## The climax idea and what displaced it

Frederic Clements argued in 1916 that a plant community develops much as an organism does, passing through juvenile stages toward a mature **climax** determined by climate. On that account a region has one climax type, disturbance resets the clock, and succession is repair. It was a strong, testable claim, which is largely why it lost.

Henry Gleason's individualistic alternative, set out in the following decade, held that each species tracks its own physiological tolerances and its own dispersal, so a community is the coincidence of many overlapping distributions rather than an entity with a life cycle. A century of vegetation data has gone Gleason's way. Species shift independently along environmental gradients, the pollen record contains past assemblages with no modern counterpart, and the same climate carries different vegetation depending on parent material, fire history and what happened to arrive first. The same reasoning is why [biome boundaries are genuinely fuzzy](/en/ecology/ecosystems/biomes-explained) rather than merely hard to draw.

## Three mechanisms wearing one label

The most durable replacement came from a 1977 paper in *The American Naturalist* by Joseph Connell and Ralph Slatyer, which separated the net effect that early colonists have on later ones into three mechanisms.

| Mechanism | Effect of early colonists on later arrivals | What sets the sequence | Signature you would look for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Facilitation | Makes the site more habitable — fixes nitrogen, stabilises substrate, adds shade and organic matter | Site modification by the pioneers | Late-successional species cannot establish until pioneers have been present |
| Tolerance | Little either way; later species simply tolerate lower resource levels | Life-history traits and growth rates | Late species are present early but slow, and prevail only as pioneers age out |
| Inhibition | Holds the site against all comers until damaged, killed or displaced | Chance of arrival plus incumbent longevity | Turnover tracks mortality of the residents, not any improvement they made |

The three are not competing theories of succession so much as competing answers to a local question, and a single hillside can be running all three in different patches. A 2011 reassessment in the journal *Ecology* took the scheme up again in the light of later biodiversity research; the framework survives because it asks what the incumbents actually do, rather than assuming they are rungs on a ladder.

## The blank slate that was not blank

Primary succession — colonisation of a surface with no biological inheritance — is the textbook case for facilitation, and Mount St. Helens is the best-observed example of it. The 18 May 1980 eruption began with a magnitude-5.1 earthquake at 8:32 a.m. that released a debris avalanche of about 2.5 km³, the largest in recorded history, which travelled 23 km down the North Fork Toutle valley. The lateral blast removed the upper 300 m of the cone and left a devastated area of 600 km² blanketed in hot debris; pyroclastic flows later that day spread up to 8 km north and created the Pumice Plain.

The blast zone looked sterile, and the assumption at the time was that recolonisation would arrive from outside. It largely did not. The U.S. Geological Survey's account of the monument's ecology records that seeds, spores, fungi and burrowing animals survived beneath the deposits, that survivors were drawn from every trophic level, and that the eruption's early-morning, late-spring timing left nocturnal burrowers underground and snowpack over many plants. These **biological legacies** — survivors and their structures, rather than immigrants — did much of the work.

Facilitation is visible where legacies were thinnest. On the Pumice Plain, the slowest zone to recover, prairie lupine came first; it fixes nitrogen from the air, and the plants caught windblown leaves, dead vegetation and insects, building small pockets of soil on bare volcanic deposits. In the blown-down forests, surviving pocket gophers are credited with the same facilitating role for other species. Landsat imagery from 1979 to 2016 shows greening spreading inward from the far northwestern quadrant of the blast zone, with the Pumice Plain still bare at satellite resolution long after ground surveys found plants on it — a useful caution for anyone reading recovery from [land-cover change detection](/en/ecology/earth-observation/land-cover-change-detection) alone.

## What a chronosequence quietly assumes

Much of what is known about long primary sequences comes from substituting space for time: sampling surfaces of different ages and reading the gradient as a single history. Glacier Bay in Alaska, where a retreating tidewater glacier has exposed ground of progressively greater age, is the classic site, and research published in *Ecological Monographs* worked through the mechanisms operating along it, including whether nitrogen-fixing colonists genuinely ease the way for later dominants.

The assumption the method makes is that every surface passed through the same sequence, differing only in how far along it is. At Glacier Bay that assumption fails: later work in *Ecology* documented multiple pathways of primary succession across the same fjord, with consequences for the ecosystem properties that developed on each. A chronosequence can therefore be internally consistent and still describe a history that no individual plot actually had.

## Different properties, different clocks

Secondary succession — recovery where soil and a seed bank remain — is faster, but "faster" hides how unevenly the parts return. A synthesis of 77 tropical sites tracked twelve forest attributes through secondary succession and found recovery to 90 per cent of old-growth values to be fastest for soil properties, at under one decade, and for plant functioning, at under 2.5 decades; intermediate for structure and species diversity, at 2.5 to 6 decades; and slowest for biomass and species composition, at more than 12 decades. After 20 years, attributes averaged 78 per cent of old-growth values, but the range across attributes ran from 33 to 100 per cent.

That spread is the practical content of the whole idea. A twenty-year-old regrowth stand can be indistinguishable from mature forest on canopy cover and soil carbon while carrying a different species list, which is why single-metric recovery claims are weak and why [ecosystem resilience indicators](/en/ecology/biodiversity/ecosystem-resilience-indicators) are built as suites. Composition lags because it depends on dispersal and on the presence of [foundation species that structure the habitat](/en/ecology/ecosystems/foundation-species-and-the-myth-of-redundancy), neither of which is guaranteed by the return of biomass. Rates of [primary production](/en/ecology/ecosystems/primary-production-and-energy-flow) recover long before the community that once did the producing.

## When the site does not come back

Some systems, once pushed, do not return along the path they left. Where positive feedbacks stabilise more than one configuration under identical external conditions — turbid, algae-dominated and clear, plant-dominated states in a shallow lake are the standard illustration — the threshold for the forward shift and the threshold for the reverse differ, so removing the original pressure is not enough. That asymmetry is **hysteresis**, and it is the reason some restoration projects stall.

How common this is remains genuinely contested. A working group that reviewed 135 freshwater papers using the vocabulary of regime shifts and multiple stable states found little empirical support for it in the sample, despite that sample being biased toward positive claims by the search terms used; the clearest evidence was confined to shallow temperate lakes, and mechanistic studies linking a named pressure to a named response were rare. The concept is well posed theoretically and thinly demonstrated in the field, and the honest position is to treat an apparent regime shift as a hypothesis about feedbacks that has to be tested rather than as an explanation already in hand.

## Choosing a target you can defend

If there is no climax, a restoration project cannot aim at one. What replaces it is a stated reference — a described condition, at a described time, with the disturbance regime that maintained it — and an acknowledgement that the reference may no longer be reachable under a changed climate or a changed species pool. The limiting case is ground whose substrate and hydrology have been rebuilt outright, as on [the sealed and compacted surfaces of cities](/en/ecology/ecosystems/urban-ecology-explained), where no historical reference condition is recoverable and the target has to be defined in terms of function instead. Stating the baseline explicitly is what makes any recovery claim auditable, because it names in advance which attributes count and on what timetable. Given that composition is the slowest attribute to return and the most sensitive to what survived, a project judged on canopy closure after five years is measuring the part of succession that was always going to be quick.
