The World Reference Base draws a line most informal accounts of soil skip. A soil layer, it says, is "a zone in the soil, approximately parallel to the soil surface, with properties different from layers above and/or below it." Then comes the qualifying clause: "If at least one of these properties is the result of soil-forming processes, the layer is called a soil horizon."

A bed of flood silt is a layer; it is not a horizon until something has happened to it in place. Layers are what you see in a pit face; horizons are what you have decided about them. Field description is not an alternative to classification but its input — the FAO's guidelines put description first because it "serves as the basis for soil classification and site evaluation as well as interpretations on the genesis and environmental functions of the soil." The processes behind those interpretations are set out in [how a soil profile forms](/en/ecology/soils/soil-formation-and-classification); this is about the unit they are recorded in.

## Ten letters, and what each one asserts

The FAO's fourth-edition guidelines state that "currently, ten master horizons and layers and seven transitional horizons are recognized," designated H, O, A, E, B, C, R, I, L and W. Three of the ten are set apart by the guidelines as "additional layers associated with some soils": I for ice, L for limnic materials, W for water layers.

An **O** horizon is dominated by organic material — "undecomposed or partially decomposed litter, such as leaves, needles, twigs, moss and lichens, that has accumulated on the surface" — and is not saturated for prolonged periods. **H** is its waterlogged counterpart: all H horizons are saturated for prolonged periods, or were once saturated and are now drained artificially. The WRB's own table glosses H as the layer "generally regarded as peat layer or organic limnic layer."

Both are graded for decomposition by suffixes of their own — a, e and i, for highly, moderately and slightly decomposed material, at under one-sixth, between one-sixth and two-thirds, and more than two-thirds visible plant remains by volume — which is where a field description meets the organisms described in [the soil food web](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web).

An **A** horizon is mineral, with "all or much of the original rock structure" obliterated, and characterised by humified organic matter intimately mixed with the mineral fraction or by properties resulting from cultivation. An **E** horizon is defined by subtraction — its main feature is "loss of silicate clay, iron, aluminium, or some combination of these, leaving a concentration of sand and silt particles." A **B** horizon forms below an A, E, H or O and shows illuvial concentration of clay, iron, aluminium, humus, carbonates, gypsum or silica, residual concentration of sesquioxides, or alteration that forms clay and structure.

**C** covers material "little affected by pedogenetic processes," excluding hard bedrock, and the line between it and **R** is drawn by a field test: air-dry chunks of C commonly slake within 24 hours in water, R does not, and R is "sufficiently coherent when moist to make hand digging with a spade impractical."

Two things are easy to miss. The letters are claims about process, not position: the symbol E "may be used without regard to position in the profile for any horizon that meets the requirements." And the alphabet is not fixed. The WRB's fourth edition of 2022 carries a new field guide that, it says, "replaces the FAO Guidelines (2006)", and a separate annex of layer designations whose master-symbol table lists nine letters rather than ten — H, O, A, E, B, C, R, I and W — with limnic material demoted from a master letter to a suffix. Below the FAO's capitals sit lower-case suffixes running the whole alphabet, a to z, plus @ for cryoturbation.

## A boundary is a measurement with a stated resolution

The FAO's guidelines are explicit that "horizon boundaries provide information on the dominant soil-forming processes that have formed the soil," and they are described by depth, distinctness and topography.

Distinctness is not sharpness in the ordinary sense. It is "the thickness of the zone in which the horizon boundary can be located without being in one of the adjacent horizons" — an interval of positional uncertainty, in four classes: abrupt, 0–2 cm; clear, 2–5 cm; gradual, 5–15 cm; diffuse, more than 15 cm. Topography records the shape of that surface: smooth, wavy (pockets less deep than wide), irregular (pockets more deep than wide), or broken (discontinuous).

Both carry interpretation. The guidelines note that diffuse boundaries point to Nitisols, cryoturbation to a cryic horizon and Cryosols, and tonguing of a mollic or umbric horizon into the layer beneath it to a Glossic qualifier. Boundary shape is itself one of the criteria for a suffix: the FAO's evidence for cryoturbation, the @ symbol, is "irregular or broken boundaries, sorted rock fragments (patterned ground), or organic matter in the lower boundary between the active layer and permafrost layer." Mixing, in that scheme, is read off the shape of a surface rather than measured.

The recording convention is honest about its own precision. Centimetre notation is used where boundaries are abrupt or clear; where they are gradual or diffuse, figures are rounded to the nearest 5 cm, "avoiding the suggestion of spurious levels of accuracy." The exception is a boundary near a diagnostic limit, where rounding is forbidden and the depth is given as the midpoint of the transition zone — 16 to 23 cm becomes 19.5 cm. The depths that trigger the rule are listed: 10, 20, 25, 40, 50, 100 and 120 cm.

## Genetic horizons and diagnostic horizons are different objects

The FAO's guidelines state the distinction directly. Master horizons are mostly "genetic soil horizons, reflecting a qualitative judgement about the kind of changes that have taken place," and "genetic horizons are not equivalent to diagnostic horizons, although they may be identical in soil profiles. Diagnostic horizons are quantitatively defined features used in classification."

The WRB's fourth edition defines its diagnostic horizons as combinations of attributes reflecting "widespread, common results of soil-forming processes," whose features "can be observed or measured in the field or the laboratory" and must reach a minimum or maximum expression to qualify, plus a certain minimum thickness. It carries 40 of them, alongside 17 diagnostic properties and 19 diagnostic materials.

A published US series description shows both layers of naming in one pit. The Trenton series' typical pedon runs Ap 0–8 inches with an abrupt smooth boundary, Btn 8–15 inches with a clear wavy boundary, then Btnk1, Btnk2 and Btnk3 to 60 inches. Its diagnostic features are listed separately: a mollic epipedon from the surface to 15 inches, spanning Ap and Btn, and a natric horizon from 8 to 60 inches. The two overlap between 8 and 15 inches — the same profile drawn twice, with different pencils.

## Why two systems can put the same profile in different classes

Both systems are worked as keys. The WRB tells the user to "go through the Key systematically, starting at the beginning and excluding one by one all RSGs for which the specified requirements are not met"; Trenton's description records that its own class was assigned from *Keys to Soil Taxonomy*. The WRB's general rules show how little slack that leaves. Numerical values "have to be taken as such and must not be rounded when compared with the threshold values in the diagnostic criteria," criteria must hold throughout the specified depth range and, where a diagnostic horizon has subhorizons, in each one separately with no averaging — thickness excepted — and on a slope thicknesses are corrected by the cosine of the slope angle.

Three sources of divergence follow. The first is disagreement about what may enter a definition at all. The WRB states that "climate parameters are not applied in the classification of soils," on the grounds that a soil's name "will not become obsolete due to global or local climate change." Soil Taxonomy does the opposite. Trenton's class, *fine, mixed, superactive, mesic Typic Natrixerolls*, carries a temperature class in *mesic* and a moisture regime in *xer*; the series description gives the criterion behind it — "dry in all parts for 45 consecutive days or longer between July and October. Xeric soil moisture regime."

The second is that the diagnostic list is itself revised. The 2022 WRB deleted the fulvic and melanic horizons, which "belonged to an outdated concept of soil organic matter," deleted aridic and geric properties and sulfidic material, introduced the cohesic, limonic, panpaic and tsitelic horizons, and reinstated the albic horizon with a requirement that soil-forming processes be evidenced. A profile keyed under the third edition and rekeyed under the fourth can change name without anything happening to the soil.

The third is purpose. On the FAO's account, Soil Taxonomy was built to interpret soil surveys, the FAO/UNESCO legend to represent the global distribution of soils, and the WRB "to facilitate correlations between different soil classification systems"; it "is not intended to be a substitute for national soil classification systems, but rather to serve as a common denominator for communication at the international level." Convergence is deliberate and incomplete — the 2022 edition took many field definitions from the USDA Soil Survey Manual and the NRCS field book, which its summary of major changes says "brings WRB and Soil Taxonomy closer together." The European Soil Bureau states the underlying difficulty — unlike plants and animals, "the world's soil cover is a continuum" — and defines the WRB's taxonomic units in terms of measurable and observable diagnostic horizons, "the basic identifiers in soil classification."

## The horizon is the sampling unit; the data products are not

This matters outside a survey office because the horizon governs where a sample comes from. The FAO's guidelines instruct that "samples are never taken across horizon boundaries," that the boundary area itself should not be sampled, and that the recommended method is to sample in equal proportions over the whole horizon — and warn that "horizon symbols should not be used as sample codes because the horizon classifications may be changed later."

Global products do not work in those units. SoilGrids 2.0 predicts on the six GlobalSoilMap intervals — 0–5, 5–15, 15–30, 30–60, 60–100 and 100–200 cm — computed for the midpoint of each interval and treated as constant across it. None of those depths knows where an A horizon ends.

The gap is visible in the largest harmonised profile archive. The WoSIS 2023 snapshot holds standardised data for 228,000 georeferenced profiles from 174 countries, covering more than 900,000 soil layers and over 6 million records. Only 80,849 of those profiles carry horizon designations at all, covering 396,522 layers, and the compilers state that the codes "have not been standardised as they vary widely between different classification systems." Classification is scarcer still: 39,649 profiles carry a WRB name, 38,792 an FAO/UNESCO one and 31,400 a Soil Taxonomy one. Most profiles in the largest such archive arrive as depth intervals with numbers attached and no record of what layer the sampler thought they were in, compounding the depth and bulk-density problems set out in [why soil carbon is a measurement problem](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty).

## What the letters can and cannot carry

A horizon designation is a hypothesis stated in one character, and the guidelines are candid about where it carries less than it appears to. In warm arid climates an undisturbed surface horizon may hold little organic matter and a mineral fraction "unaltered or only slightly altered by weathering"; it is designated A "because it is at the surface." Recent alluvial or aeolian deposits retaining fine stratification are not an A horizon unless cultivated — sedimentation is excluded from the letter by rule, not by observation.

That is the right expectation to hold of a profile description: a disciplined record of layers, boundaries and the interpretation a describer was willing to commit to, in a vocabulary the WRB has revised twice since its 2006 edition. It supports comparison across sites; it does not support the assumption that a horizon boundary is a physical surface at a fixed depth. The [four-phase account of what soil is](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained) turns on the clause that the material is organised in genetic horizons; reading a profile is the operation that decides which ones.
