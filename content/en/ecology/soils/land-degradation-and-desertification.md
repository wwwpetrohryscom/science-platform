The treaty that governs international action on land degradation defines the term in a way most readers of the phrase would not expect. Article 1 of the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification defines land degradation as reduction or loss of biological or economic productivity "in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas" — a definition restricted, by its own text, to drylands. The Sustainable Development Goal indicator that reports national progress on the same subject applies to every land surface on Earth.

The mismatch is a good introduction to the central problem of the field. Land degradation is not one measurable quantity. It is a family of judgements about what land was, what it is, and what it is for, and the wide range across published global estimates follows directly from that. The physical processes underneath those judgements — erosion, carbon loss, salinisation, compaction — are catalogued in the overview of [soil as a slow-forming resource](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained); this article is about what happens when they are aggregated into a single headline percentage.

## Three assessments, three answers

The FAO and its Intergovernmental Technical Panel on Soils concluded in 2015 that 33 per cent of land is moderately to highly degraded. The UNCCD's second Global Land Outlook, surveying a wider set of studies, reports that most assessments place 20 to 40 per cent of the global land area in a degraded or degrading condition. Neither figure is a measurement in the ordinary sense.

The FAO's own history of these exercises is unusually frank about why. Global assessments began more than forty years ago, it notes, and "have until now not achieved a clear answer on where soil degradation takes place, what impact it has on the population, and what the cost to governments and land users would be". Three generations of method sit behind the published numbers:

- **GLASOD** (1991) mapped human-induced soil degradation from structured expert opinion, because at the time little else was available. It has been criticised for reproducibility, and the FAO's assessment defends it on the grounds that later approaches have not clearly done better.
- **GLADA** derived degradation from trends in satellite vegetation indices, which substitutes a measurable proxy for expert judgement and inherits every ambiguity of that proxy.
- **GLADIS** rated six factors from global databases — biomass, water resources, soil health, above-ground biodiversity, and economic and social provision — and was criticised for the reliability of those databases and for downscaling relationships derived at plot scale.

The divergence is visible most sharply at regional level. For Africa, one dryland-focused review estimated 73 per cent of drylands degraded and 51 per cent severely so; GLASOD's expert survey put 65 per cent of agricultural soils, 31 per cent of permanent pasture and 19 per cent of woodland and forest in a degraded state. These are not competing measurements of one quantity. They are answers to differently posed questions.

## Desertification is not deserts advancing

The most persistent misconception about drylands is that desertification means the physical spread of desert margins into productive land — a front moving outward, like a coastline retreating. The Convention's definition says something different: desertification is land degradation occurring in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas, whatever its cause and wherever within those zones it occurs.

The distinction has practical consequences. Degradation in drylands is typically patchy, driven by overgrazing, cultivation of marginal land, irrigation-induced salinisation and wind erosion, and it appears as a mosaic of damaged patches rather than an advancing edge. The Convention treats it as reversible in principle: its definition of "combating desertification" covers prevention and reduction of degradation, rehabilitation of partly degraded land, and reclamation of land already desertified. The UNCCD reports that about 500 million people live in areas that have experienced desertification since the 1980s.

Drylands are not a marginal category: the FAO's assessment puts them at around 40 per cent of the Earth's land surface. Nor are their soils bare in the way the word suggests. Biological soil crusts — assemblages of cyanobacteria, lichens, mosses and associated microfauna binding the surface — cover roughly 25 per cent of dryland soils, equivalent to about 12 per cent of the global land surface, and they fix carbon and nitrogen, stabilise the surface against [wind and water erosion](/en/ecology/soils/soil-erosion-processes-and-rates), and regulate infiltration. Damage to a crust is a form of degradation that an index tuned to green leaf area is poorly placed to detect.

## What the indicator actually computes

Under SDG indicator 15.3.1, the proportion of land that is degraded, reporting rests on three sub-indicators: trends in land cover, trends in land productivity, and trends in carbon stocks, currently represented by soil organic carbon. The UNCCD's technical guidance integrates them with a **one-out-all-out** rule — a significant negative change in any one of the three counts as degradation for the whole land unit — and reports the result as a binary classification, degraded or not degraded, expressed as a percentage of total land area.

Three design choices inside that deserve attention. The baseline period is fixed as the sixteen years from 1 January 2000 to 31 December 2015, chosen because moderate-resolution satellite data became widely available in 2000 and because roughly fifteen years is usually enough to span the variability of natural systems. The output is binary, which means a unit that has lost a tenth of its productivity and a unit that has been paved are recorded identically. And one-out-all-out is asymmetric by construction: it maximises sensitivity to degradation at the cost of scoring some genuine improvements as losses, a consequence the guidance addresses directly.

Land Degradation Neutrality, the policy target these indicators serve, is defined in the same guidance as a state in which the amount and quality of land resources needed to support ecosystem functions and services, and to enhance food security, remain stable or increase within specified temporal and spatial scales. Neutrality is therefore a balance between losses and gains, which makes the accounting rules above load-bearing rather than technical.

## The greening paradox is mostly a definition problem

Satellite records show the planet getting greener. A 2019 analysis in *Nature Sustainability* of [leaf-area trends from 2000 to 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30778399/) found greening concentrated strikingly in China and India and overlapping with croplands worldwide: China alone accounted for 25 per cent of the global net increase in leaf area while holding 6.6 per cent of global vegetated area. The authors attribute over a third of the net global increase to direct human land-use management rather than to climate or CO₂ fertilisation, driven in China by afforestation programmes and in both countries by intensified cropping — food production in the two countries rose by over 35 per cent since 2000, largely through multiple cropping supported by fertiliser and irrigation.

Read carelessly, that looks like a refutation of the degradation literature. It is not, for three reasons that are worth separating.

More leaf area is not more ecosystem function. Irrigated multiple cropping raises measured productivity while drawing down groundwater and, in many settings, soil organic matter. Plantation forestry raises leaf area while replacing species-rich systems. The productivity sub-indicator is explicitly a measure of above-ground productivity, and the UNCCD's guidance says so.

The indicator's own designers anticipated the inverse error too. Version 2 of the guidance adds a procedure for identifying false positives and false negatives where the one-out-all-out process yields a counterintuitive result — its worked example is remediation that removes invasive weeds, which lowers apparent productivity in satellite data and would otherwise be scored as degradation.

And greening and degradation are not mutually exclusive at any scale that matters. They are typically happening in different places, and often on the same hectare through different mechanisms — a cropland can green above ground while losing carbon and structure below it, which is why the soil sub-indicator exists at all and why [measuring that carbon reliably](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty) is more than a bookkeeping matter. The general limits of what a vegetation index can and cannot resolve are treated in [NDVI and its interpretation](/en/ecology/earth-observation/ndvi-explained) and in the insight on [what satellites cannot see](/en/insight/what-satellites-cannot-see).

## What the numbers cannot settle

Three things follow for anyone reading a degradation statistic. The headline percentage depends on a definition that differs between the Convention text and the SDG indicator, so quoting one against the other is comparing different populations. The estimate depends on a baseline, and a baseline set in 2000 treats a landscape already transformed by centuries of use as the reference condition — the [shifting-baseline problem](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-baselines-and-shifting-baselines) in another guise. And the choice of indicator decides which kinds of damage are visible: soil compaction, biodiversity loss and salinisation below the detection threshold of a satellite productivity trend are real, and largely absent from the global totals.

The economic case is similarly framed rather than measured. One initiative cited by the FAO put annual global losses from deforestation and land degradation at EUR 1.5 to 3.4 trillion in 2008, or 3.3 to 7.5 per cent of world GDP — a range spanning more than a factor of two, resting on what the FAO itself called scattered and uncertain information. That such a figure is uncertain is not an argument that the underlying problem is small. It is an argument for reading the number as a statement about method, and for asking which definition, which baseline and which indicator produced it before comparing it with any other.
