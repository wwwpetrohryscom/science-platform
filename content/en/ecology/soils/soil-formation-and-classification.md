A horizon is evidence. Every layer in a profile records something that was added, transformed, moved down or across, or lost from a particular point on the land surface, repeated for long enough to leave a signature that a trained eye can read in a pit face. That is the working premise of pedology, and it is also the premise of the classification systems built on top of it: the World Reference Base states that its diagnostic horizons and properties are characterised by attributes reflecting the common results of the processes of soil formation.

The [four-phase description of what a soil is](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained) sets up the question this article takes on — how the arrangement arises, and what happens when people try to give the result a name.

## Four families of process, and the horizons they leave

Weathering supplies the mineral raw material. Physical breakdown fragments rock without changing its chemistry; chemical weathering by hydrolysis, oxidation, carbonation and dissolution converts primary minerals into clays and oxides and releases cations into solution. Organic accumulation supplies the other half: litter and root exudates enter the surface, are partially decomposed, and leave a dark, aggregated layer distinct from anything below it.

The interesting processes are the ones that move material inside the profile. Percolating water carries clay particles, humus, iron and aluminium compounds, carbonates and soluble salts downward, stripping an upper layer and depositing them lower down. Where evaporation exceeds rainfall the direction reverses and salts accumulate near the surface. Losses close the accounting: leaching out of the base of the profile, gaseous loss, and removal from the top by [the three erosion processes](/en/ecology/soils/soil-erosion-processes-and-rates).

The clearest demonstration that these processes drive naming is the World Reference Base's own arrangement of its Reference Soil Groups, which allocates them according to the dominant soil-forming factor or process. Its eight groupings read as a list of pedogenic outcomes, each holding several groups: thick organic layers, of which Histosols are the case; strong human influence, covering Anthrosols and Technosols; limitations to root growth imposed by permafrost, shallowness, exchangeable sodium, shrink–swell clay or soluble salts; iron and aluminium chemistry, taking in Gleysols, Andosols, Podzols and Ferralsols among others; pronounced organic-matter accumulation in a mineral topsoil, the Chernozem and Kastanozem group; accumulation of moderately soluble salts such as secondary gypsum or carbonate; clay-enriched subsoils, where Luvisols and Acrisols sit; and profiles with little or no differentiation at all, including Cambisols, Arenosols and Regosols. A Podzol is not a colour; it is a name for subsoil accumulation of humus and oxides.

## The rate at which rock becomes soil

Soil production has been measured directly since cosmogenic nuclides made it possible. The 1997 *Nature* paper that established [the soil production function](https://www.nature.com/articles/41056) used in-situ produced ¹⁰Be and ²⁶Al concentrations in bedrock beneath soils of different thickness on hillslopes in northern California, alongside hillslope curvature as an independent proxy. Both methods agreed, and both showed production declining exponentially as the overlying mantle thickens — a relationship that had been assumed for a century without field data behind it.

The global compilation published in *PNAS* in 2007 puts the median soil production rate at 0.017 mm per year across 188 measurements. At a bulk density of 1,200 kg m⁻³ that is close to 0.2 tonnes per hectare per year, and it means a metre of profile represents something on the order of tens of thousands of years of production.

Two limits on that figure deserve stating. It is measured at the soil–bedrock interface, which makes it a poor guide where the profile has developed in a thick mantle of loess, glacial or lacustrine sediment that was never bedrock in place. And mixing by burrowing animals, roots and fungi can construct an organically enriched surface horizon far faster than bedrock disintegrates, which is why the [biological turnover of the profile](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web) belongs in any account of how quickly a soil can be said to form.

## What the state factors explain, and where they stop

The five-factor framework — climate, organisms, relief, parent material and time — is the standard way to reason about why two profiles differ, and it is genuinely productive when four factors are held roughly constant and the fifth is varied. Granite and limestone under the same rainfall produce profiles with different clay mineralogy, different base status and different pH. The same granite under high rainfall and under semi-arid conditions produces, respectively, a strongly leached acid profile and one accumulating secondary carbonate at depth. A floodplain surface a century old and a tropical upland surface stable for a million years will differ in horizon development regardless of everything else.

What the framework does not do is predict. It is a statement of dependence, not an equation you can evaluate: no one can compute a horizon sequence from five inputs, because the factors interact, because relief redistributes water and material laterally, and because most real landscapes are polygenetic — they have experienced more than one climate. Early classification systems nevertheless tried to organise soils by these factors directly. FAO's account of the history describes the Russian and 1938 USDA systems dividing soils into zonal types, where climate and vegetation dominated, and azonal and intrazonal types, where parent material and time did. Modern classification began when that was abandoned: the seventh approximation of USDA Soil Taxonomy replaced inferred genesis with precisely defined, quantified properties used to define diagnostic horizons.

That shift is the important one. Contemporary systems classify what can be measured in a pit and a laboratory, not what the surveyor believes happened.

## Two systems, two jobs

The persistence of competing systems is often read as disciplinary untidiness. FAO's own explanation is more useful: the systems were built for different purposes — Soil Taxonomy to interpret soil surveys, the FAO/UNESCO legend to represent the global distribution and geography of soils, and the World Reference Base to make correlation between systems possible.

| | USDA Soil Taxonomy | World Reference Base | FAO/UNESCO legend |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First published | 1975 | 1998 | after a 1968 request from the International Union of Soil Sciences |
| Purpose | support US soil survey and correlate soil series | correlate national systems; common international denominator | map soils at continental scale |
| Structure | hierarchical categories under 12 orders | two levels: reference group plus ranked qualifiers | broad units, simple |
| Current status | maintained through successive Keys | fourth edition, 2022, endorsed by the International Union of Soil Sciences | replaced by the WRB in 1998; still in legacy maps |

The World Reference Base began with 30 Reference Soil Groups in 1998 and has had 32 since its second edition; its second level attaches principal qualifiers, given in ranked order, and supplementary qualifiers, listed alphabetically, so that a soil with few distinguishing features gets a short name and a complex one gets a long name. It states explicitly that it is not intended to substitute for national systems but to serve as a common denominator. Soil Taxonomy makes the opposite commitment: FAO's description notes that it attempts to classify all the world's soils, but that its main aim has always been to group those of the United States, and that it was designed to correlate soil series and to supply map unit names at varying cartographic detail. Neither system is wrong; they are answering different questions, and the WRB explicitly borrows from Soil Taxonomy in doing so.

## Classification is a decision procedure

Both systems are keys, and a key is a sequence of decisions rather than a measurement. In the WRB the user works through the key from the beginning, excluding groups whose requirements are not met, and the soil belongs to the first group whose criteria it satisfies. The instruction that follows is the one worth noticing: field and laboratory values must be taken as they are and must not be rounded when compared against diagnostic thresholds.

That rule is honest, and it is also where the apparent precision of a soil name comes from. A layer one centimetre short of a thickness requirement, or a base saturation a fraction below a limit, produces a different name for a profile that is physically almost identical to its neighbour. The name is exact; the underlying continuum is not. Anyone comparing soil descriptions across surveys, laboratories or decades is comparing the outputs of a threshold rule applied to measurements that carry their own error, and small analytical differences can move a profile across a boundary.

## What a soil map is actually asserting

The scale problem is the last and most practical one. FAO's own note on the legacy Soil Map of the World is candid: it was intended for mapping at continental scale and not at local scale. The Harmonized World Soil Database, whose version 2.0 was released in 2023 at roughly 1 km resolution, describes the composition of soil units within nearly 30,000 mapping units, drawing on about 21,000 profiles and reporting seven depth layers from 0–20 cm down to 150–200 cm.

Read that structure carefully and the limitation is explicit in the data model. A mapping unit is a statement about the proportions of soil units expected inside a polygon, not an assertion about the profile beneath any particular point in it. A global soil map is a defensible description of a region and a weak prediction for a field, in much the same way that [a biome boundary](/en/ecology/ecosystems/biomes-explained) is real at continental scale and arbitrary at the scale of a hillside. The practical consequence for anyone using gridded soil data is that the uncertainty is not principally in the attribute values; it is in whether the polygon's composition applies where the question is being asked.
