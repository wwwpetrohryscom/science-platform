Most of the nutrient content of a fertile field is unavailable to the crop growing in it, and this is normal rather than a defect. Fertility is better understood as a supply rate — how fast particular ions arrive at a root surface — than as an inventory. The inorganic forms of nitrogen that roots absorb, nitrate and ammonium, usually amount to less than 5 per cent of the nitrogen present. Phosphorus in the soil solution sits between 0.01 and 0.50 mg per litre, against roughly 400 for calcium, 60 for magnesium and 40 for potassium; half or more of the total may be locked in organic compounds. Around 1 to 3 per cent of soil organic matter decomposes in a year, so a fertile profile holding 8,000 kg of organic nitrogen per hectare releases about 160 kg of it, of which a crop might take up half.

The [profile-scale view of what a soil holds](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained) is the necessary background here; this article is about the chemistry that decides which fraction of that holding a plant can actually reach.

## The list of essential elements is shorter, and less fixed, than it looks

Under the criteria set out in 1939 by Arnon and Stout — that a deficiency must prevent the plant completing its life cycle, that the effect must be specific to the element, and that the element must act in nutrition rather than by correcting some other condition — FAO's plant nutrition guide counts 16 essential elements for higher green plants. Three of them, carbon, oxygen and hydrogen, come from carbon dioxide and water. The remaining 13 are the mineral nutrients: six macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulphur, calcium, magnesium) and seven micronutrients (boron, iron, manganese, copper, zinc, molybdenum, chlorine).

The familiar figure of 17 essential nutrients reflects nickel, whose essentiality was established in 1987; FAO's guide records that there is no unanimity on whether nickel is essential or merely beneficial, and adds that the list should not be considered final. A count that looks like a constant is in fact a convention with an open edge.

The quantities differ by orders of magnitude. Carbon, oxygen and hydrogen make up about 95 per cent of plant biomass; of the remaining mineral fraction, nitrogen and potassium account for roughly 80 per cent, phosphorus, sulphur, calcium and magnesium for about 19, and all the micronutrients together for under 1. The ratio of nitrogen to molybdenum in plant tissue is around 10,000 to 1, and a deficiency in the element present at a few parts per million limits yield as effectively as one in the element present at 1.5 per cent.

## The exchange complex is the short-term memory

Nutrient cations are mostly not in solution. They are held in exchangeable form on the negatively charged surfaces of clay minerals and organic matter, and released as the solution is depleted. The capacity to hold them is the **cation exchange capacity**, expressed in centimoles of positive charge per kilogram: below 10 cmol kg⁻¹ for sandy soils, above 30 for clay-rich ones, and higher wherever organic matter is abundant. Because it depends on which clay minerals are present and on how much organic matter has accumulated, the capacity is largely inherited from the [weathering and horizon development](/en/ecology/soils/soil-formation-and-classification) that produced the profile in the first place.

Cations are not held with equal strength. The order runs aluminium, then calcium and magnesium, then potassium, hydrogen and ammonium, with sodium held most loosely — which is why sodic soils behave so badly. Base saturation, the share of the exchange capacity occupied by basic cations, is the conventional summary.

The anions are the asymmetry that governs everything downstream. Phosphate and, to a lesser extent, sulphate are bound on positively charged oxide and humic surfaces. Nitrate and chloride are barely held at all. A soil that stores potassium for years stores nitrate for days.

## pH is the master variable, though not quite for the reason usually given

Below about pH 4.5, aluminium becomes soluble enough to be toxic to roots; almost no plants survive below pH 3. Availability of the metallic micronutrients — iron, manganese, copper, zinc — rises as acidity increases, with molybdenum moving in the opposite direction. Phosphate is constrained at both ends of the range, rendered less available in strongly acid upland soils and precipitated with calcium in alkaline ones. Most crops perform best in the neutral to slightly acid band around pH 6 to 7.

The frequently reproduced diagram of nutrient availability against pH is easy to over-read as a statement about plants. FAO's guide is careful on this: a species' apparent preference for a pH range is often determined by nutrient requirement and efficiency rather than by acidity as such — oats prefer slightly acid conditions because manganese supply is better there — and the ranges shown do not mean a crop cannot be grown outside them.

## Nitrogen: lost because it moves

Nitrogen applied as urea is hydrolysed by the urease enzyme to ammonium, which is then usually nitrified to nitrate rapidly, unless the soil is too acid or too poorly aerated for the nitrifying organisms. Every step opens a loss pathway. Urea left on an alkaline surface loses nitrogen as ammonia gas. Nitrate, held by nothing, moves with percolating water out of the root zone; under anaerobic conditions it is denitrified and leaves as nitrous oxide or dinitrogen. Ammonium can be fixed in non-exchangeable form inside 2:1 clay minerals such as vermiculite and illite. The mineralisation and immobilisation turnover behind all of this is biological, which is why the [soil food web](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web) is not a separate subject from fertility.

First-crop recovery reflects that. FAO's tabulation gives mineral nitrogen fertiliser a utilisation rate of 50 to 70 per cent under good conditions, slurry 30 to 50 per cent and solid manure 20 to 40 per cent; measured recovery in Asian paddy rice in the late 1990s ranged from under 30 per cent to about 70.

Supply is not the constraint. USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries put world ammonia plant production at 156 million tonnes of contained nitrogen in 2024 and an estimated 160 million in 2025, and state that the availability of nitrogen from the atmosphere for fixed-nitrogen production is unlimited. The nitrogen problem is a problem of where the unrecovered fraction ends up, and it connects directly to [the wider nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained).

## Phosphorus: lost because it does not move

Soluble phosphate entering the soil solution is largely converted to adsorbed forms within hours. In acid soils it binds to iron and aluminium hydrous oxides, clay minerals and aluminium–organic complexes; in neutral and alkaline soils, to calcium and magnesium carbonates and their organic complexes. Pedologists describe the result as three pools — solution, labile and non-labile — in slow equilibrium.

The consequence for recovery is severe and easily misread. Mineral phosphate fertiliser has a first-year utilisation rate of 10 to 20 per cent, against 50 to 60 for potassium and 0.5 to 5 for micronutrients. But where the first-year figure is 15 per cent, roughly 1 to 2 per cent follows in year two and about 1 per cent per year after that, reaching some 25 per cent cumulatively over ten years and about 45 per cent over thirty. Fixed phosphorus is mostly slow rather than gone, so the efficiency you report depends on the accounting window you choose.

| | Nitrogen | Phosphorus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ultimate source | atmospheric N₂, industrially fixed | mined sedimentary and igneous rock |
| Behaviour in soil | mobile as nitrate; barely retained | adsorbed within hours; effectively immobile |
| Dominant loss pathways | leaching, denitrification, volatilisation | surface runoff with sediment; retention in soil |
| First-crop recovery | 50–70% (mineral fertiliser) | 10–20% (mineral fertiliser) |
| Long-run supply constraint | none stated by USGS | reserves concentrated; grades declining locally |

The reserve question is where public discussion most often goes wrong. USGS reports world phosphate rock mine production of 239 million tonnes in 2024 and an estimated 250 million in 2025, world reserves of about 73 billion tonnes, and world resources of more than 300 billion tonnes, adding explicitly that there are no imminent shortages. What the same figures show is concentration: Morocco holds about 50 billion tonnes of the reserve total. Depletion is nonetheless real regionally — USGS attributes flat United States output to Florida producers contending with decreasing reserves and lower phosphorus pentoxide content — and the agency notes there is no substitute for phosphorus in agriculture. The defensible framing is access, grade and geopolitics on a decadal horizon, not exhaustion on a human one.

## Where the unrecovered fraction goes

The US Environmental Protection Agency describes surplus nutrients washing from fields during rain and snowmelt, leaching through the profile into groundwater, and leaving as gaseous ammonia and nitrogen oxides. Sediment carries adsorbed phosphorus, which links fertility loss to [erosion rates and the arguments about them](/en/ecology/soils/soil-erosion-processes-and-rates).

The regional accounting is uncomfortable. The European Environment Agency estimates that nutrient imbalances affect 74 per cent of EU agricultural land, with nitrogen surplus increasing. Downstream, NOAA's July survey of the hypoxic zone on the northern Gulf shelf — the area where bottom oxygen falls below 2 mg per litre — measured 4,402 square miles in 2025 and 1,332 square miles in 2026, the second smallest in a record that began in 1985. A more than three-fold swing between consecutive years is a reminder that the zone's area tracks river discharge as much as nutrient loading, so one year's measurement says very little about the trend in [coastal deoxygenation](/en/ecology/oceans/ocean-deoxygenation-and-dead-zones).

## What a use-efficiency figure can actually support

Every recovery percentage quoted above rests on one of two methods, and FAO's guide states the limitation without hedging: no method has been developed to establish true values for the recovery of applied nutrients by crops. The difference method — comparing fertilised and unfertilised plots — is confounded because plants respond to deficiency by changing root growth and uptake capacity, so the unfertilised control is not a clean baseline. Isotope tracers are confounded by the internal mineralisation–immobilisation turnover of nitrogen in the soil, which exchanges labelled and unlabelled atoms.

That leaves nutrient-use efficiency as a useful comparative measure and a poor absolute one. A 2015 *Nature* analysis of [managing nitrogen for sustainable development](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature15743) treats improvement in crop nitrogen-use efficiency as central to reconciling food production with environmental limits, and works with national trajectories rather than a single global constant — the appropriate use of a quantity whose absolute value cannot be pinned down. Comparing this year's efficiency figure against one published a decade ago, computed by a different method over a different accounting window, manufactures a trend the underlying measurements cannot support.
