Fertiliser applied to a field has three destinations: the crop, [the soil](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained), and somewhere else. The third fraction is the subject here, and the reason it matters is that the energy spent making the nutrient is spent whether or not the plant takes it up — around 2 per cent of world final energy for the nitrogen alone, on the IEA's accounting.

The two main nutrients leave by different routes, and confusing them is the most common error in nutrient management.

## Nitrogen leaves in solution and in gas

Nitrate is highly soluble and binds weakly to soil particles, so water moving through soil carries it. It leaves in drainage, in tile flow, and in shallow groundwater, and it does so most readily when there is no crop taking it up — after harvest, over winter, during the interval between application and root development.

Some also leaves as gas. Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate to nitrogen gas and to [nitrous oxide](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained) under low-oxygen conditions, and ammonia volatilises from surface-applied fertiliser and manure. Both routes are invisible without instruments, which is part of why they are routinely underestimated in on-farm accounting.

## Phosphorus leaves with soil

Phosphate binds strongly to mineral surfaces. The USGS puts the practical consequence directly: "Soil erosion is a major contributor of phosphorus to streams. Bank erosion occurring during floods can transport a lot of phosphorous from the river banks and adjacent land into a stream, lake, or other water body."

So phosphorus loss is mostly particulate and mostly episodic — concentrated in the storms that move soil, rather than distributed through the drainage season. It also accumulates: soil that has received phosphorus for decades holds a large legacy pool that continues to supply losses long after applications stop.

## Why the interventions differ

The routes determine what works, and a measure that helps one element can be neutral or counterproductive for the other.

**Timing helps nitrogen.** Applying closer to peak crop demand shortens the window in which nitrate sits in the soil with nothing to take it up. It does much less for phosphorus, whose losses are driven by erosion events rather than by [residence time](/en/ecology/earth-systems/biogeochemical-cycles-explained).

**Erosion control helps phosphorus.** Cover crops, residue retention, buffer strips and reduced tillage all reduce the sediment that carries phosphate. Their effect on nitrate is smaller and can be negative — no-till systems sometimes increase preferential flow through macropores, which moves dissolved nitrate faster.

**Drainage management helps nitrogen.** Controlled drainage and denitrifying bioreactors intercept nitrate in solution. They do nothing for particulate phosphorus, which does not travel that route.

The general point is that "nutrient loss" is not one problem. It is two problems that share a vocabulary.

## What the losses do downstream

The EPA identifies "agriculture and stormwater" as key contributors of excess nutrients, and describes the consequence: excess nitrogen and phosphorus cause "algae and algae-like bacteria to grow faster than ecosystems can handle, forming algal blooms", with toxins that "can make people and animals sick if they touch, drink, or eat contaminated fish or shellfish."

The USGS adds the oxygen sequence: "excessive nutrient enrichment triggers algal overgrowth, leading to decreased dissolved oxygen as the algae decay", and "the decay of a large bloom can leave 'dead zones' — low oxygen areas where fish can't survive."

Because fresh water is more often phosphorus-limited and coastal water more often nitrogen-limited, the same field can contribute to two different problems by two different routes, and reducing one loss does not automatically reduce the other.

## The honest position on efficiency

Use efficiency can be improved and cannot be made complete. A crop takes up nutrients through a root system that must encounter them, and encounter is imperfect — which is the same geometric problem that [mycorrhizal symbiosis](/en/biology/microbiology/mycorrhizal-symbiosis-and-nutrient-uptake) exists to solve, operating at the millimetre scale. Some fraction of applied nutrient will always be in the wrong place at the wrong time.

What can be reduced is the size of that fraction, and the reduction is worth pursuing on two grounds at once: the downstream damage, and the energy embodied in the nutrient itself.
