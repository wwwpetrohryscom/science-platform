A 2019 analysis in *Nature* assembled 6,759 georeferenced samples into a global model of soil nematode abundance and arrived at [4.4 ± 0.64 × 10²⁰ individuals](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31341281/) in the world's surface soils, with a combined biomass of roughly 0.3 gigatonnes. The distribution was not the one most people would guess: sub-Arctic soils hold 38 per cent of the total, against 24 per cent in temperate and 21 per cent in tropical regions.

That result is a fair summary of the state of soil biology as a whole. The numbers are enormous, they are recent, they overturn intuitions built from above-ground ecology, and they come with error bars that are wide for a reason. What lives in the ground beneath the [profile described in the soils overview](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained) is the least catalogued major habitat on the planet.

## A census in orders of magnitude

Soil biology is conventionally organised by body size — microbes, then microfauna, mesofauna and macrofauna — because size determines which pores an organism can occupy and therefore what it can eat.

| Group | Diversity | Abundance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bacteria and archaea | tens of thousands of taxa per gram | up to 1 billion cells per gram |
| Fungi | ~90% of species live in soil | up to 200 m of hyphae per gram |
| Nematodes | all trophic levels represented | 4.4 × 10²⁰ globally, ~0.3 Gt biomass |
| Mites (Acari) | >20,000 described; likely >80,000 undescribed | up to 100,000 per m², ~2 g wet weight per m² |
| Earthworms | ~6,000 species in about 20 families | ingestion up to 1,250 t/ha/yr at one tropical site |

The 2023 review in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* that estimated [soil holds 59 ± 15 per cent of species on Earth](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37549278/) reached that figure by partitioning group by group, and the per-group results are more informative than the headline. Enchytraeidae, the small pale annelids related to earthworms, are 98.6 per cent soil-dwelling, the highest share of any group examined. Fungi come in at 90 per cent, plants at 85.5 per cent — a figure that only makes sense once rooted plants are counted as soil organisms — and termites at 84.2 per cent. The ±15 is not decoration: for most of these groups the total number of species is itself an extrapolation.

Two features of the community matter more than the census. The first is that abundance and diversity are decoupled from dominance. A survey of 237 locations across six continents, published in *Science*, found that [about 2 per cent of bacterial phylotypes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29348236/) — some 500 of them — consistently accounted for almost half of soil bacterial communities worldwide. A soil can be extraordinarily rich and still be functionally dominated by a small, globally recurring cast.

The group that best illustrates how thin the knowledge base still is sits between the microbes and the animals. Protists — amoebae, ciliates, flagellates, and everything eukaryotic that is not a plant, animal or fungus — can exceed hundreds of thousands of individuals in a gram of soil, and the FAO assessment describes them as among the least studied members of the community. Their principal recognised role is predation: the microbial loop, in which protists grazing on bacteria release nitrogen that bacterial biomass had immobilised, making it available to plants. That concept is well established for nitrogen. For phosphorus and other elements the assessment's own verdict is that the work has largely not been done, and that most of what exists dates from an earlier generation of research.

The second feature that matters more than the census is that the food web is fuelled partly from below. Stable-isotope work summarised in the FAO's 2020 soil biodiversity assessment shows soil mites enriched in root-derived carbon and nitrogen, implying that root exudates and fine-root turnover, not only leaf litter falling from above, drive much of the below-ground economy. That is a different topology from the classical grazing web set out in [food webs and trophic structure](/en/ecology/ecosystems/food-webs-and-trophic-structure), and it implies that management altering rooting patterns reaches the soil community through a more direct channel than management altering litter inputs.

## Mycorrhizas are a trade, and the terms vary

The most consequential symbiosis in terrestrial ecology is between plant roots and fungi. A 2018 assessment in *New Phytologist* of [global host plant diversity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29355963/) gives the breakdown: 72 per cent of vascular plants are arbuscular mycorrhizal, 2.0 per cent ectomycorrhizal, 1.5 per cent ericoid and 10 per cent orchid mycorrhizal. Just 8 per cent are entirely non-mycorrhizal, and a further 7 per cent form the association inconsistently. The FAO assessment dates the arbuscular association to at least 500 million years — effectively as old as land plants.

The economics are straightforward and often misstated. Fungal hyphae are far finer than root hairs and explore a larger soil volume, which matters most for nutrients that move by diffusion rather than mass flow — phosphorus above all. In exchange the plant supplies photosynthate. Because it is a trade rather than a gift, its value to the plant depends on what the soil already supplies: where available phosphorus is abundant the exchange buys less, and the association matters correspondingly less to the plant's nutrition. Which nutrients are limiting, and why phosphorus behaves so differently from nitrogen, is taken up in [soil fertility and nutrient availability](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-availability-and-soil-fertility).

Most of the non-mycorrhizal 8 per cent are specialists — carnivorous plants, parasites, hydrophytes, epiphytes — organisms that have either an alternative nutrient supply or a habitat where the association cannot form. The exceptions are informative rather than embarrassing for the model.

## The stable carbon is mostly dead microbes

The largest revision in soil biology this century concerns what stays in the ground. The older model held that stable soil organic matter consisted of intrinsically hard-to-degrade plant compounds — lignin, waxes, so-called humic substances — that resisted decay because of their molecular structure.

A 2011 synthesis in *Nature* [reframed persistence as an ecosystem property](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21979045/) rather than a molecular one, concluding that molecular structure alone does not control stability and that environmental and biological controls predominate. In the account that has replaced the old one, organic matter survives largely because of where it is rather than what it is — protected inside aggregates, adsorbed onto mineral surfaces, or simply out of reach of the organisms that could consume it.

The positive half of that revision followed. A 2019 analysis in *Global Change Biology* pooled amino-sugar biomarker data published between 1996 and 2018 and concluded that [microbial necromass can make up more than half of soil organic carbon](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31365780/) in temperate agricultural, grassland and forest topsoils. In that account, plant inputs are largely a substrate: microbes consume them, build biomass, die, and it is their residues — cell-wall fragments binding to clay and iron oxides — that accumulate.

Two caveats belong with this. Amino sugars are a proxy, not a direct count, and the paper is explicit that separating microbial from non-microbial carbon by molecular signature is genuinely difficult. And "more than half" is a topsoil result from temperate systems; the balance in deep horizons, peats and tropical soils is less well established. But the direction of the change matters for management, because it implies that feeding the microbial community is a more direct route to durable carbon than adding recalcitrant material — with the measurement caveats set out in [soil carbon measurement and its uncertainties](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty).

## What a DNA survey is actually reporting

Almost everything above rests on molecular surveys, and those have a known and quantified pathology.

Extracellular DNA from dead cells persists in soil for weeks to years. A 2016 study in *Nature Microbiology* used a viability assay to separate intact cells from that residue and found that, on average, [40 per cent of both prokaryotic and fungal DNA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27991881/) recovered from soil was extracellular or from cells no longer intact. This "relic DNA" inflated observed richness by up to 55 per cent and skewed the estimated relative abundances of taxa — including taxa central to nutrient cycling. The effect was not uniform: it was more likely in soils with low exchangeable base cations, and its removal changed community structure most in high-pH soils. A survey that does not control for it is reporting a mixture of the living community and its recent history.

A second limitation is the reference database. The FAO assessment notes that metabarcoding and metagenomics for several soil groups are constrained by insufficient reference sequences, so a large share of recovered sequences cannot be assigned to a named organism at all. For soil algae the report observes that molecular phylogenies and morphological taxa do not align, and that photosynthetic microorganisms are routinely omitted from metagenomic soil studies altogether.

The practical consequence is that a soil sequencing result answers a narrower question than it appears to. It reports which sequences were recovered and could be matched, from DNA that may or may not have come from living cells, using primers with known taxonomic blind spots. That is still enormously more than could be known twenty years ago. It is not a species list, and treating it as one is how [the gap between microbial composition and function](/en/insight/microbiome-research-and-the-causal-gap) gets underestimated.

Sequencing costs have fallen far faster than interpretive capacity has grown. The limiting factor in soil biology is no longer generating data about who is present; it is establishing what any of them are doing.
