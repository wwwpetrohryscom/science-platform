Three distinct processes strip material from a hillslope, and they are not variations on a single theme. Water erosion detaches particles by raindrop impact and by the shear of runoff moving across the surface. Wind erosion detaches them by drag, and by the hammering of grains already bouncing downwind — saltation. Tillage erosion detaches nothing: the implement itself carries material downslope, a little further on every pass. The FAO's 2019 review of the subject records that tillage erosion was recognised by soil scientists only in the 1990s and, unlike the other two, is difficult to observe visually while it is happening.

Whether any of this matters at a given site depends entirely on a comparison with the rate at which new material is made, the quantity that makes [soil a stock rather than a flow](/en/ecology/soils/soil-science-explained). That comparison is where the arithmetic gets difficult, and it is the reason published erosion figures disagree by more than most readers expect.

## What each process does to a slope

Water erosion has been the most studied and probably affects the largest area. Detached particles first travel in thin sheets of overland flow, then in concentrated channels: rills where the channel is shallower than 0.3 m, gullies where it is deeper. Where flow slows or meets a vegetative barrier, sediment settles; where it does not, the material reaches a stream and leaves the landscape entirely. That distinction between redistribution and export matters more than it sounds, because a field can lose material from its upper slopes without exporting a gram to the river.

Wind erosion dominates in arid and semi-arid environments — the FAO review identifies it as the principal form in the Near East and North Africa — and grain size largely sets how far material travels, from a few metres by saltation to continental distances in suspension.

Tillage erosion thins upper slopes and over-thickens footslopes, and the material accumulated at the bottom is then available for water to move again. It is not a small term. For European cropland, one modelling study summarised in the FAO review puts tillage erosion at 3.3 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ against 3.9 for water erosion on the same land; a global cropland estimate in the same table gives 12.1 for water and 3.5 for tillage. A conservation programme that addresses runoff and ignores the plough is addressing part of the problem.

## The units carry an assumption

Soil science reports erosion as mass per area per year. Converting that to a depth of lowering — the form most readers find intuitive — requires a value for bulk density. At the 1,200 kg m⁻³ used in the most widely cited global compilation, 1 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ corresponds to 0.08 mm of lowering, so every depth figure quoted below is a mass measurement multiplied by an assumed density. Scale carries a second assumption: rates measured on experimental plots of 10⁻⁴ to 10² m² are systematically unlike those measured over hillslopes and catchments of 10⁴ to 10⁹ m², because the larger the area, the more opportunity eroded material has to be redeposited inside it rather than leaving.

## What a factor model can and cannot deliver

The Universal Soil Loss Equation and its revisions estimate long-term average annual loss as a product of terms for rainfall erosivity, soil erodibility, slope length and steepness, cover management and support practices. Its empirical foundation is concrete and narrow: erosion-runoff plots 22.1 m long and 4.1 m wide, plus rainfall simulators, mostly in the United States.

Applied to Europe with harmonised datasets and a 25 m elevation model, that framework returns a mean of 2.5 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ over the erosion-prone area, with 76 per cent of the European land mass below 2 and permanent crops such as vineyards and olives highest at 9.47. Applied globally at 250 m resolution over 125 million km², or 84.1 per cent of the land surface, it gives 35.9 petagrams of material moved by water in 2012, about 2.5 per cent more than in 2001. Croplands occupy roughly 11 per cent of that modelled area and account for about half the total, at 12.7 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ against 0.16 for forest and 1.84 for other natural vegetation.

What the framework cannot do is equally definite. It estimates detachment on hillslopes, not delivery to a river; it does not represent gully incision or deposition; and the authors of the global assessment state plainly that applying equations developed in the United States elsewhere is a legitimate concern.

## Measured against modelled

The gap between what models return and what field workers find is the central unresolved question in the subject.

| Estimate | Basis | Land covered | Rate (t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Global plot compilation | erosion plots | conventional agriculture | ~49 (mean) |
| Second global plot synthesis | erosion plots | cropland | 12–15 |
| Western Europe | plots plus modelling | cropland | 3.6 |
| Europe, RUSLE-based map | modelling | arable land | 2.7 |
| Seven sites in England | field survey of rills and gullies | cropland | 0.15 |

The FAO review states the pattern directly: plot-derived mean annual losses run from 8 to almost 50 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, while regional and global models return 2 to 4. At the extremes of the table, two estimates of erosion on cultivated land differ by a factor of several hundred.

The European map attracted an exchange in the literature that is unusual for the field. Critics argued that the modelled results were never compared against field assessments of erosion in Britain, that the rainfall-erosivity and erodibility terms are weak, and that averaging cover factors leaves wheat and maize in Germany almost identical, contrary to other evidence. The modelling group replied that each factor had been published separately and was open to comparison. The FAO review presents the disagreement as live, not settled — the same definitional trouble that runs through [estimates of land degradation](/en/ecology/soils/land-degradation-and-desertification).

## Against the rate at which soil is made

A compilation of 1,673 measurements from 201 studies, published in *PNAS* in 2007, is still the reference point. Its medians, in millimetres of lowering per year: conventional agriculture about 1.5; no-till 0.08; native vegetation 0.013; soil production 0.017; long-term geological erosion 0.029. Means run higher — 3.9 mm yr⁻¹ for conventional agriculture — because the distributions are strongly skewed. The paper's own consequence is that an initially decimetre- to metre-thick profile erodes through in a few hundred to a few thousand years.

Set against that, "tolerable" soil loss has two incompatible definitions. Derived from soil production rates, tolerable values fall between 0.2 and 2.2 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. Derived from maintaining crop yield, USDA work put the range at 4.5 to 11.2 and later European Environment Agency work at 1 to 5. The same field can be comfortably sustainable under one definition and failing by an order of magnitude under the other.

Two caveats belong with the production-rate comparison. Soil production functions are measured at the soil–bedrock interface, so they are least relevant where the profile has developed in a thick mantle of loess, glacial or lacustrine sediment — the mechanism is covered in the companion article on [how a profile is built and named](/en/ecology/soils/soil-formation-and-classification). And mixing by soil organisms can build an organic-enriched surface horizon considerably faster than bedrock converts to soil.

There is also a persistent misreading worth naming. Topsoil depth is not a usable indicator of erosion, because the ploughed surface horizon cannot be thinner than the depth of ploughing: it retains a constant thickness while the material beneath it is progressively incorporated and removed. What changes is the horizon's composition, not its depth — which is one reason erosion redistributes organic carbon rather than simply destroying it, and why [soil carbon budgets are difficult to close](/en/ecology/soils/soil-carbon-measurement-and-uncertainty).

## What the control measures actually buy

Effect sizes for conservation practices are better constrained than the global rates, and they are regionally specific rather than universal. A meta-analysis summarised by the FAO found no-till reduced soil loss by 60 per cent in temperate climates but produced no significant difference in subtropical and tropical ones; runoff fell 33 per cent in temperate conditions and was significantly higher in the warmer ones. A separate synthesis put the yield cost of no-till at 5.1 per cent overall, 15.1 per cent in tropical latitudes and 3.4 per cent in temperate.

Mulching illustrates the measurement problem inside the effect size. Across rainfall-simulation studies the average reduction in sediment concentration was 68.9 per cent; across runoff plots, silt fences and sediment traps it was 48.8 per cent. Terracing has been used for about 5,000 years and can bring erosion under rice close to geological rates, but a survey of 60 studies attributes terrace failure to abandonment, poor maintenance and poor design.

The modelled European result is the outlier that should give pause: reduced tillage and cover crops, applied by 2015 on more than a quarter of EU agricultural land, reduced modelled soil loss by roughly 1 per cent each. That is a statement about the model's cover-management factor as much as about the practices, and the difference matters when the same model underpins policy.

## The cost estimates are the clearest signal of what is unresolved

An estimate published in *Science* in 1995 put the annual on-site and off-site cost of wind and water erosion, plus prevention, at about US$44.4 billion for the United States alone. An estimate published in an environmental-economics journal in 2007 put the annual loss in US farm income at $100 million. FAO's own summary calls erosion cost estimates widely variable and controversial, because on-site productivity losses are masked by additional fertiliser use and off-site costs — sedimentation, and the [nutrient enrichment of lakes and reservoirs](/en/ecology/freshwater/lake-ecology-and-eutrophication) — depend on whose valuation is applied.

The regional picture is firmer. The European Environment Agency estimates about 1 billion tonnes of material eroded across the EU each year, with roughly a quarter of EU soils affected by water erosion, mainly cropland, and projections of a 13 to 25 per cent increase by 2050. But a four-hundred-fold spread in the cost of a process whose physics is well understood is not an arithmetic failure. It is what happens when a rate that has to be integrated over decades is inferred from plots that ran for years, using models fitted on one continent, and judged against thresholds that two agencies define differently.
