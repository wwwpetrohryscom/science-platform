Every lake and river channel on Earth holds, between them, about 93,100 cubic kilometres of water. The ocean holds 1,338,000,000. In the global water distribution table maintained by the USGS Water Science School, that surface fraction works out to roughly one one-hundred-and-fiftieth of one per cent of all water; adding the world's swamps leaves the total still under one hundredth of one per cent. The species assessments say those same habitats support more than 10 per cent of all described species, including around a third of vertebrates and about half of all fishes. Freshwater ecology is largely the study of what follows from that ratio.

## Volume is the wrong measure; area and connectivity are the right ones

The distribution table is worth reading closely, because it is quoted more often than it is understood. Of all water, 96.54 per cent is in oceans, seas and bays. Fresh water is 2.5 per cent of the total, and most of it is not available to organisms: 68.7 per cent of it is locked in ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow, and another 30.1 per cent is groundwater. Fresh lakes hold 91,000 cubic kilometres, swamp water 11,470, and the world's rivers just 2,120.

Comparing those three surface stocks with each other is more informative than comparing any of them with the ocean. Of the liquid fresh water standing or flowing at the surface, lakes hold roughly 87 per cent, wetlands about 11 per cent, and rivers about 2 per cent. Rivers are, volumetrically, almost nothing.

Area tells a different story. A satellite inventory of the world's lakes counted about 117 million water bodies larger than 0.002 square kilometres, covering close to 5 million square kilometres, or 3.7 per cent of the non-glaciated land surface. A separate reconstruction from Landsat-derived river widths put the surface area of rivers and streams at mean annual discharge at 773,000 ± 79,000 square kilometres, 0.58 ± 0.06 per cent of non-glaciated land — some 44 per cent more than earlier estimates. Fresh waters are thin films with large perimeters, not reservoirs. Their ecological weight comes from surface, edge and connection, not from volume, and their role in the [global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained) is one of rapid transfer rather than storage.

## Why a drainage basin behaves like an island

A river network is dendritic. Water moves down it, and most strictly aquatic organisms can only move along it. To reach the next basin, a fish, a mussel or a caddisfly has to cross either a watershed divide or salt water, and most cannot do either. Basins are therefore closer to islands than to patches of forest, and they have the biogeography of islands: small ranges, high turnover between neighbouring systems, and a great deal of endemism concentrated in single catchments or single lakes.

This has three consequences that recur across every page in this cluster. Endemic species are common, so local extirpation is often global extinction. Ranges are small, so a single impoundment, diversion or pollution source can affect a species' entire distribution. And because the network is linear, an obstruction anywhere upstream removes everything above it from the system below — a form of [habitat fragmentation](/en/glossary/habitat-fragmentation) with no terrestrial analogue, examined in detail in the article on [dams and river fragmentation](/en/ecology/freshwater/dams-and-river-fragmentation).

## Four compartments, four different clocks

Fresh water is not one habitat. The four principal compartments differ by orders of magnitude in how long a water molecule stays put, and that single property sets what kind of ecology each supports.

| Compartment | Global stock (km³) | Turnover | The variable that organises it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh groundwater | 10,530,000 | Decades to millennia | Recharge rate against abstraction |
| Fresh lakes | 91,000 | Years to centuries | Thermal structure and nutrient loading |
| Wetlands (swamp water) | 11,470 | Seasonal to permanent | Hydroperiod — how long, how deep, how predictably |
| Rivers and streams | 2,120 | Days to weeks | The flow regime |

Stocks are from the USGS distribution table; turnover is characteristic rather than precise, and varies enormously within each class.

Because the clocks differ, so do the failure modes. In rivers, the organising variable is discharge, and the argument for treating it as the master control — along with the measurement chain that produces every discharge number you have ever read — is set out in [rivers and flow regimes](/en/ecology/freshwater/river-ecology-and-flow-regimes). Lakes integrate: they are mixed vertically only once or twice a year in much of the temperate zone, and they accumulate what their catchments deliver, which is why [lake eutrophication and recovery](/en/ecology/freshwater/lake-ecology-and-eutrophication) is a story about legacy rather than about this year's inputs. Wetlands sit at the interface, and their functions and their contested loss statistics are covered in [what wetlands do](/en/ecology/freshwater/wetlands-and-their-functions). Groundwater is the largest liquid store by far and the slowest, which is why abstraction from it is often mining rather than harvesting, as [aquifer depletion and how it is measured](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion) explains. A small but disproportionate share of wetland area is peat: the IUCN puts peatlands at no less than 3 per cent of the global land surface, holding more than 600 gigatonnes of carbon — up to 44 per cent of all soil carbon.

## What the threat data actually say

The largest freshwater assessment yet published covered 23,496 species — 14,628 freshwater fishes, 6,223 dragonflies and damselflies, and 2,645 decapod crustaceans — and found 24 per cent of them threatened with extinction. Decapods were worst affected at 30 per cent, fishes at 26 per cent and odonates at 16 per cent. Eighty-nine assessed species, 0.4 per cent of the total and mostly fishes, are recorded as extinct.

The threat breakdown matters more than the headline. Among threatened species, 54 per cent are affected by pollution, 39 per cent by dams and water extraction, 37 per cent by land-use change and associated effects from agriculture, and 28 per cent by invasive species and disease; close to a fifth are recorded as affected by climate change and severe weather. These categories overlap heavily — most threatened species carry several — so the shares cannot be added, and no single lever addresses the majority.

National monitoring puts flesh on the pollution term. The US EPA's 2022 National Lakes Assessment, a probability survey representing lakes across the conterminous United States, found 50 per cent of lakes in poor condition for phosphorus and 47 per cent for nitrogen, with hypereutrophic conditions in 30 per cent. Its 2018–19 rivers and streams survey found only 28 per cent of river and stream miles in good biological condition for benthic macroinvertebrates. The instruments and thresholds behind judgements like these are unpacked in [how water quality is measured](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained).

## The part the assessments cannot see

Roughly a quarter of the freshwater species assessed — 23 per cent — are classed as Data Deficient, meaning there is not enough information to judge their extinction risk at all. That figure is a statement about the evidence base, not about the species. It also understates the problem, because the three groups were chosen partly for being well enough described to assess at all: the work covered 97 per cent of described odonates, 81 per cent of decapods and 79 per cent of freshwater fishes. Available assessments do not reach comparable coverage for the far larger freshwater invertebrate fauna — molluscs, and most aquatic insect orders — so the 24 per cent threatened figure describes the best-known quarter of the problem.

The uncertainty extends to habitat extent itself. A 2023 reconstruction of inland wetland conversion since 1700 estimated a loss of 3.4 million square kilometres, confidence interval 2.9 to 3.8, or 21 per cent of global wetland area with a confidence interval of 16 to 23 per cent — and the authors were explicit that this is lower than earlier figures, which had been extrapolated from records drawn disproportionately from high-loss regions. Freshwater trend statistics are frequently produced from monitoring networks that are thinnest where endemism is highest, a bias examined in [the freshwater biodiversity record](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-biodiversity-decline).

None of this argues for discounting the numbers. It argues for quoting them with their intervals and their coverage attached, and for treating a confident single figure about global fresh water as the least reliable kind of freshwater statistic.
