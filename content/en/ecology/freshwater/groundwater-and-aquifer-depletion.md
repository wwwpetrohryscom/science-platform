Groundwater accounts for 99 per cent of the liquid fresh water on Earth, according to the UN World Water Development Report for 2022, and it is the only large store of it whose condition cannot be seen. Every published figure — a depletion rate, a remaining lifetime, a recharge estimate — is the end of a measurement chain, and the chains do not all arrive at the same answer. Understanding why they differ is more useful than memorising any one of the numbers, and it is the reason this page spends as much space on instruments as on aquifers. Groundwater's place among the other freshwater compartments — and the reason it turns over so much more slowly than any of them — is set out in the overview of [freshwater ecosystems](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained).

## What an aquifer is, and what the word conceals

The US Geological Survey defines an aquifer as a body of rock or sediment saturated below the water table that readily transmits water to wells and springs. Two properties decide whether a formation qualifies: porosity, the fraction of the material that is void space, and permeability, the degree to which those voids are connected. A rock can be porous and nearly useless, and the distinction matters because storage and yield are not the same quantity.

Where the saturated zone has a free upper surface, the aquifer is **unconfined** and the water table rises and falls with recharge. Where a low-permeability layer caps it, the aquifer is **confined**, the water in it is under pressure, and a well drilled into it may flow at the surface without pumping. The USGS notes that porosity and permeability generally decline with depth as overlying weight closes pores and cracks, though the relationship is loose enough that fresh water has been found below 6,000 feet (about 1,800 m).

The persistent misconception is the underground lake. Groundwater sits in pore space, and the rate at which it moves through that space spans roughly six orders of magnitude: several metres a day in coarse permeable material, a few centimetres a century in clay and shale. That range is why a contaminant plume, a pressure change and a volume change all propagate at different speeds through the same formation — and why the container itself can be damaged by the act of emptying it.

## Recharge, residence time, and the line between harvesting and mining

The volume argument is where most public discussion goes wrong. A 2016 study in *Nature Geoscience* on [the global volume and distribution of modern groundwater](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo2590) put roughly 22.6 million km³ in the upper 2 km of continental crust, but estimated that only 0.1–5.0 million km³ of that — less than 6 per cent — was recharged within the previous 50 years. Spread over the continents, the modern fraction amounts to a layer about 3 m deep.

That fraction is the part with a meaningful [residence time](/en/glossary/residence-time) on human timescales. The rest is a legacy stock. When abstraction exceeds recharge, water is not being harvested from a renewing flux; it is being removed from a store that accumulated over millennia, and the distinction between the two is what the word *depletion* is doing. The scale of the demand is not marginal: the same UN report attributes 69 per cent of global groundwater abstraction to agriculture, 22 per cent to domestic use and 9 per cent to industry, and estimates that groundwater supplies half the volume withdrawn for domestic use worldwide and about a quarter of all irrigation water.

## Three families of measurement, three different quantities

No single instrument observes the thing that policy asks about. The three approaches in general use measure genuinely different variables, and each is blind in a way the others are not.

| Approach | What it directly measures | Scale it resolves | Principal blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monitoring wells | Hydraulic head at one point in one formation | Metres to a few kilometres | Head is not volume without a storage coefficient; networks sit where wells were drilled, not where change matters |
| Satellite gravimetry (GRACE, GRACE-FO) | Change in total terrestrial water mass | Large regions, monthly | Cannot separate groundwater from soil moisture, snow, surface water or ice |
| Hydrological and groundwater models | A simulated water balance | Basin to global | Inherits every error in reported abstraction and in assumed recharge |

NASA flew the original Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment from 2002 to 2017 and launched its successor, GRACE-FO, on 22 May 2018. The missions are exceptionally good at what they do, but the separation problem is intrinsic. When one team assessed the record and published [34 trends in global freshwater availability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0123-1) in 2018, the drivers had to be sorted by hand into natural interannual variability, unsustainable groundwater consumption, climate change, or combinations of the three. A gravity trend is a mass signal; calling it depletion is an attribution step performed afterwards.

## Where the methods disagree, and where they agree

The largest well-based synthesis to date, published in *Nature* in January 2024 as [an analysis of rapid groundwater decline and some cases of recovery](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06879-8), used in-situ levels from 170,000 monitoring wells across 1,693 aquifer systems in countries accounting for roughly 75 per cent of global groundwater withdrawals. It found rapid declines — faster than 0.5 m per year — to be widespread in the twenty-first century, concentrated in dry regions with extensive cropland, and accelerating over the previous four decades in 30 per cent of the world's regional aquifers. It also found reversals: specific systems where depletion trends turned around after policy change, managed [aquifer recharge](/en/glossary/aquifer-recharge) or surface-water diversion.

Both of those findings are stronger than a global total, because both are statements about places where the measurement is dense. Global depletion totals, by contrast, depend heavily on modelled abstraction — a quantity that is metered in some jurisdictions and estimated from crop and energy data in others. Where the record is long and national, the picture sharpens: the USGS estimates cumulative depletion across 40 US aquifers and areas at approximately 1,000 km³ between 1900 and 2008, with the rate rising from a 9.2 km³ per year long-run average to almost 25 km³ per year during 2000–2008.

## The consequences that do not reverse

Two effects make groundwater different from a bank account, which is the metaphor the USGS itself uses and which breaks in exactly this respect.

The first is **[land subsidence](/en/glossary/land-subsidence)**. When fine-grained sediments lose the pore pressure that helped support them, they compact, and the compaction is largely permanent — it lowers the ground surface and it permanently reduces the aquifer system's storage capacity. The USGS reports that more than 17,000 square miles (about 44,000 km²) across 45 states have been directly affected, and that more than 80 per cent of identified subsidence in the country is attributable to groundwater extraction. Around Houston, water-level declines of roughly 400 feet (about 120 m) have been accompanied by up to 10 feet (about 3 m) of subsidence; in south-central Arizona, declines of 300 to 500 feet have been measured alongside as much as 12.5 feet of subsidence. Refilling the aquifer does not restore the pore space.

The second is **saline intrusion**. Saline groundwater is not a marginal presence: the USGS puts it at about 12.9 million km³ against roughly 10.5 million km³ of fresh groundwater. Under natural gradients the boundary between the two is broadly stable; pumping moves it inland and upward. Because the flushing rate is the groundwater flow rate, a contaminated coastal aquifer in fine material recovers on the timescale at which that material transmits water, which can be centuries. The same physics governs how long any contaminant persists once it reaches the saturated zone, a point taken up in the article on [what routine water-quality monitoring measures](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained).

Surface systems feel the drawdown too. Pumping intercepts groundwater that would otherwise discharge to streams, and where the water table falls below the reach of streamside roots the riparian community goes with it. The USGS documents a reach of the Santa Cruz River south of Tucson photographed in 1942 with mesquite and cottonwood stands and re-photographed at the same site in 1989 with the riparian trees largely gone, after a water-table decline exceeding 100 feet in nearby wells. This coupling is why aquifers cannot be managed as a separate resource from the rivers and [wetlands they sustain](/en/ecology/freshwater/wetlands-and-their-functions), and why groundwater belongs inside the accounting of [the global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained) rather than beside it.

## What is still unresolved

Three gaps limit what any of this can support. Storage coefficients — the conversion from a head change to a volume change — are poorly constrained for most aquifers, so even a dense well network yields an uncertain volume. The behaviour of deep and old water under sustained pumping is largely unobserved, because monitoring concentrates in the shallow zone that supplies wells. And the documented recovery cases are few and specific enough that it is not yet clear which of them generalise; they establish that depletion is reversible in some settings, not that it is reversible in most.
