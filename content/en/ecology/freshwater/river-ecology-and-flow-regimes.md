A streamgage does not measure how much water is going past it. It measures stage — the height of the water surface — usually every 15 minutes, and converts that height into discharge using a stage-discharge relation built separately from field measurements of velocity and channel cross-section. The USGS operates more than 13,500 gauges on this principle and has done so since 1889, when the first was installed on the Rio Grande in New Mexico. Nearly every statement about how much water a river carries is therefore a rating curve applied to a height, which is worth remembering before treating a hydrograph as a direct observation.

What that record shows, wherever it is long enough, is that a river's ecology tracks the shape of its flow far more closely than its total. Rivers are volumetrically negligible — the USGS global water table puts every river channel on Earth at 2,120 cubic kilometres, about 2 per cent of the liquid fresh water at the surface — but they are ecologically decisive because of how that small volume behaves in time and how much land it touches. Mapping river widths from Landsat put the global surface area of rivers and streams at mean annual discharge at 773,000 ± 79,000 square kilometres, some 44 per cent more than pre-satellite estimates had assumed. The broader picture of where fresh water sits is set out in the [freshwater ecosystems overview](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained).

## Five properties, not one number

A flow regime is conventionally described by five properties, and each one has a distinct set of organisms and processes attached to it.

**Magnitude** is how much water moves per unit time. It sets channel dimensions over decades, wetted habitat over hours, and the shear stress that decides which grain sizes stay put. **Frequency** is how often a flow of a given size occurs — the difference between a bankfull event every year and one every twenty. **Duration** is how long a condition persists, which is what separates a brief low-water spell from one long enough to strip oxygen from a pool, or a short overbank pulse from one that actually recharges a floodplain. **Timing** is when in the year an event happens, and it is the property with the tightest biological coupling: many fishes cue spawning migrations to a seasonal rise, and riparian trees release seed in a window that historically coincided with receding flood water and exposed wet sediment. **Rate of change** — how fast the water rises and falls — determines whether organisms can track a moving shoreline or are stranded by it.

The practical implication is that a single summary statistic destroys the information. Two rivers can share a mean annual discharge and share almost nothing else. An annual average also conceals the direction of change: a regime can become both flashier and drier at once.

## The longitudinal template, and where it stops working

The classic organising idea for a river system is longitudinal. Headwater streams are narrow and shaded, so most of their energy arrives as leaf litter from outside the channel and the invertebrate community is dominated by animals that shred it. Mid-order reaches are wide enough for light to reach the bed, so in-channel algal production rises and grazers become prominent. Large lower reaches are deep and turbid, production returns to being subsidised from upstream, and fine particulate matter and the animals that filter it dominate. Each reach is, in this reading, adapted to the material the reach above it fails to process.

The template is a starting point rather than a law, and three situations break it. In large floodplain rivers the dominant exchange is lateral rather than longitudinal, and the sequence never establishes. In regulated rivers a dam resets the gradient part-way down: it exports clear, cold, sediment-free water into a reach that is structured for the opposite, an effect covered in [dams and river fragmentation](/en/ecology/freshwater/dams-and-river-fragmentation). And in arid and intermittent systems the continuum is interrupted in time as well as in space, so the relevant question is not what arrives from upstream but whether there is surface water at all.

## The lateral dimension, and a record long enough to see it change

Where floodplains are extensive, the annual rise and fall of water across them is the organising event. The Amazon at Manaus has been gauged at the same site since the early twentieth century, and the annual difference between high and low water there may reach 15 metres. That predictable pulse drives fish migration onto the floodplain, tree reproduction timed to inundation, and the exchange of nutrients between channel and forest.

The Manaus record is also long enough to show that the pulse is not stationary. An analysis of the 1903-2015 series found that severe floods went from roughly one every twenty years in the first half of the twentieth century to about one every four years from the 2000s onward, with a flood in every year from 2009 to the end of the record — a fivefold increase in frequency. Severe droughts over the same period showed no significant long-term change. The authors attributed the flood signal to a strengthening of the Walker circulation driven by tropical Atlantic warming alongside tropical Pacific cooling, connecting a local hydrograph to the kind of large-scale [climate variability and teleconnection](/en/ecology/earth-systems/climate-variability-and-teleconnections) patterns that operate across ocean basins. A change in one tail of the distribution without a matching change in the other is exactly the pattern a mean annual figure would hide.

## Measuring alteration, and setting a flow that is not the natural one

Hydrological alteration is assessed by comparing a river's observed regime against a reference — its own pre-development record where one exists, or a modelled natural regime where it does not — using statistics of the same five properties. A USGS synthesis of streamflow data for 1980-2014, compiled from more than 3,000 gauging sites across the United States, found that low flows are now more frequent than they would be naturally but of shorter duration, while high flows are also more frequent yet lower in magnitude and shorter. USGS work reports flow modification along more than 1.2 million miles of US streams, associated with losses of native fish or invertebrates.

That result is a useful corrective to the intuition that regulation simply smooths a river. Here it made events more common and less pronounced — a compression of the distribution from both ends, which removes the extremes that many life histories are keyed to.

Environmental flow prescriptions attempt to reinstate what matters most within whatever water is left after abstraction. The honest limits are worth stating. A prescription can usually deliver magnitude and timing; it delivers rate of change less reliably and duration only when storage allows. It cannot restore the sediment that a reservoir intercepts, nor the temperature regime of water drawn from a hypolimnion, and both travel with flow in an unregulated river. Where the reference condition itself has to be modelled, the alteration metric inherits every assumption in that model.

## What the receiving end looks like

The EPA's National Rivers and Streams Assessment for 2018-19, a probability survey of perennial rivers and streams in the conterminous United States, found 28 per cent of river and stream miles in good biological condition based on benthic macroinvertebrates and 47 per cent in poor condition; 35 per cent had healthy fish communities. Phosphorus was in poor condition across 42 per cent of miles and nitrogen across 44 per cent, and 64 per cent of miles carried moderate or high riparian disturbance. The survey's own analysis suggested about 20 per cent of the miles in poor biological condition could improve if nutrient condition moved from poor to fair or good — an estimate that also implies most of the deficit is not nutrient-driven. How these condition classes are defined, and what a chemical spot measurement can and cannot integrate, is taken up in [water quality measurement](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained).

## Where the record is weakest

Three limits sit under all of the above. Rating curves are calibrated within the range of flows that have been measured directly, so the flood you most want a number for is often the one being extrapolated. The great majority of the world's river network is ungauged, and the reaches with the longest records are systematically the large, accessible, economically important ones. And a reference condition is a moving target: the Amazon series shows a regime shifting under climatic forcing that no upstream management decision produced, which means that comparing a modern hydrograph to a mid-century baseline conflates two different causes of change. Distinguishing them is the same [event attribution](/en/ecology/climate-change/extreme-weather-attribution-basics) problem that arises for extreme weather, and it is not solved by more gauges alone.
