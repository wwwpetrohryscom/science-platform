Ask how much of the world's wetland has been destroyed and the answer depends on which study is reached for, and the spread between reputable answers is wide enough to change what a reader would conclude from it. The disagreement is not about drainage records. It is about what counts as a wetland, when the clock starts, and which parts of the world the underlying data came from. Getting that straight is the precondition for reading anything else written about these systems.

## Three criteria, two of which are inferred

Under the US Clean Water Act, wetlands are "those areas that are inundated or saturated by surface or ground water at a frequency and duration sufficient to support, and that under normal circumstances do support, a prevalence of vegetation typically adapted for life in saturated soil conditions." Field identification rests on three parameters: wetland hydrology, hydric soils, and hydrophytic vegetation.

Only one of those is a direct observation of water. Hydric soils are a record of past saturation written in colour, mottling and organic accumulation; hydrophytic vegetation is a community that indicates saturation because of what it tolerates. Delineation therefore reads two proxies for a hydrological condition that may be absent on the day of the survey — which is the point, since a system saturated only in spring is still a wetland, but it is also why boundaries are argued over and why a change in indicator criteria can move national wetland area without a spade being lifted.

The consequence for statistics is direct. A definition that requires surface water excludes wet meadows and many peatlands; one that includes seasonally saturated soils includes vast areas of boreal and floodplain landscape. Compare two loss estimates built on different definitions and you are comparing two different objects.

## Two global loss numbers that are not the same measurement

The most systematic reconstruction to date combined national and subnational drainage and conversion records with land-use maps and simulated wetland extents to trace conversion to seven human land uses between 1700 and 2020. It estimated that 3.4 million square kilometres of inland wetlands have been lost since 1700, with a confidence interval of 2.9 to 3.8 million, primarily to cropland. As a share, that is a net loss of 21 per cent of global wetland area, confidence interval 16 to 23 per cent, concentrated in Europe, the United States and China and expanding rapidly during the mid-twentieth century.

The paper is explicit that this share is lower than what had been suggested previously, and about why: the older figures were extrapolations from data drawn disproportionately from high-loss regions. Neither approach is careless, and the difference between them is instructive. Aggregating local and national drainage records is a sound way to establish that loss happened and a poor way to establish what fraction of the world it represents, because the places that generate wetland records are largely the places that drained wetlands. Reconstructing the denominator as well as the numerator lowers the percentage, because it credits the large and still largely unconverted wetland areas of the boreal and tropical zones that the local-record literature under-samples.

The usable conclusion is that the direction and the absolute magnitude are robust — millions of square kilometres, overwhelmingly to cropland, with the sharpest expansion in the middle of the twentieth century — while the percentage is a function of method and should never be quoted without it.

## Functions, ranked by how well they are quantified

Wetlands are credited with water purification, flood attenuation, habitat provision and carbon storage. These are not equally well measured, and the difference is worth being explicit about.

Nutrient processing is the best quantified. Saturated wetland soils are anoxic close to the surface, and anoxia with available organic carbon is the condition under which microbes reduce nitrate to nitrogen gas, removing it from the water rather than moving it downstream. A continental-scale analysis put current nitrogen removal by US wetlands at about 860 ± 160 kilotonnes of nitrogen per year, and modelled a spatially targeted 10 per cent increase in wetland area — 5.1 million hectares — as roughly doubling that removal, with an estimated 54 per cent decrease in nitrogen loading in nitrate-impacted watersheds such as the Mississippi basin. The word doing the work there is "targeted": the same analysis found existing removal capacity is limited by a spatial mismatch between where wetlands are dense and where nitrogen loads are high. This is the upstream end of the loading problem that drives [lake eutrophication](/en/ecology/freshwater/lake-ecology-and-eutrophication), and it connects wetland extent directly to the [nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained).

Flood attenuation is the least well quantified of the headline functions at any scale above a single catchment. The mechanism — temporary storage and increased roughness slowing the passage of a flood wave — is not in doubt, but the effect depends on antecedent saturation, position in the catchment and the size of the event, and it does not aggregate cleanly. Claims about national or global flood protection from wetlands rest on much thinner evidence than claims about nutrient removal.

## Peatlands are a different accounting problem

Peat forms where saturation slows decomposition below the rate of plant production, so carbon accumulates as partly decayed organic matter, sometimes to depths of several metres, over millennia. The IUCN puts peatlands at no less than 3 per cent of the global land surface, holding more than 600 gigatonnes of carbon — up to 44 per cent of all soil carbon, and more than is held in all other vegetation types including the world's forests.

Drainage inverts the system. Once the water table drops, the stored carbon meets oxygen and oxidises, so a peatland switches from a slow sink to a fast source and stays one for as long as it is dry. The IUCN's assessment gives emissions from drained peatlands as about 1.9 gigatonnes of CO₂ equivalent a year, equivalent to 5 per cent of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, while the remaining near-natural peatland — over 3 million square kilometres — sequesters roughly 0.37 gigatonnes of CO₂ a year. The asymmetry between those two figures is the reason peatland condition, not peatland area, is the quantity that matters for the [carbon cycle](/en/glossary/carbon-cycle), and it is why a drained peatland cannot be treated as a degraded version of a wet one. It is an actively different sign.

## Condition, and the fraction that cannot be assessed

Extent statistics say nothing about whether what remains is functioning. The US EPA's National Wetland Condition Assessment is a probability survey designed to answer that, and its 2021 round found 45 per cent of wetland area in good condition on a vegetation indicator, and 48 per cent in good condition on an indicator based on non-native plants, with 24 per cent poor or very poor. Physical alteration was the most widespread stressor: a combined indicator across six alteration types put 82 per cent of wetland area in fair or poor condition, with vegetation removal and vegetation replacement each affecting 56 per cent.

The most instructive number in the survey is a gap rather than a result. Because many wetland sites had no surface water to sample, 40 per cent of wetland area could not be assessed for nitrogen, phosphorus or cyanotoxins at all. Nitrogen was rated poor across 17 per cent of wetland area and phosphorus across 24 per cent, but those percentages describe the assessable 60 per cent. A wetland that is dry at the time of sampling is not an anomaly to be discarded; for many types it is the normal summer state. Any chemistry-based condition statistic for wetlands is thus structurally biased toward the wetter end of the population, in a way that a comparable statistic for lakes is not.

Comparing survey rounds compounds the problem. Between 2016 and 2021 the biological indicators showed little national change, while the share of wetland area in good condition for nitrogen rose 8 percentage points to 29 per cent and microcystin detection rose by almost 22 percentage points to 30 per cent, with exceedances of the health benchmark staying below 1 per cent throughout. Whether the detection increase reflects the wetlands, the weather in the sampling years or the laboratory is not resolvable from two survey rounds. That is the recurring situation across freshwater monitoring, and the reason the [freshwater ecosystems overview](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained) treats interval and method as part of every figure rather than a footnote to it. Where the water table itself is falling for reasons outside the wetland, as it is across many irrigated basins, wetland condition is partly a downstream expression of [groundwater depletion](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion).
