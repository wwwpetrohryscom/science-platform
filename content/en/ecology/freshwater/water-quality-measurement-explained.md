There is no such thing as water quality in the abstract. A sample has a temperature, an oxygen concentration, a suspended-solids load and a bacterial count; whether those values are good or bad is a comparison against a threshold, and the threshold encodes a purpose — drinking, bathing, irrigation, or supporting a particular biological community. The same river reach can pass one test and fail another on the same day. Most confusion about water-quality reporting comes from reading a compliance judgement as if it were a physical measurement. What follows applies across the compartments surveyed in the overview of [freshwater ecosystems](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained), though the sampling problem differs sharply between a river, a lake and an aquifer.

## The parameters taken almost every time

Four measurements travel with nearly every field sample the US Geological Survey collects: temperature, pH, specific conductance and dissolved oxygen. They are cheap, they are made in situ with a multi-parameter sonde, and between them they constrain a surprising amount.

**Dissolved oxygen** is the one most directly tied to whether animals can live in the water. It is inversely related to temperature, so it carries both a seasonal and a daily cycle, and it responds to photosynthesis, to decomposition and to turbulence. The USGS gives more than 8 mg/L as typical for surface water of normal salinity in summer, and defines water below 2 mg/L as hypoxic — the condition that produces the seasonal oxygen-depleted zone off the Mississippi and Atchafalaya outflows, where nutrient-driven algal production is consumed by bacteria in the subsurface layer. A single oxygen reading is therefore almost uninterpretable without the time of day, the temperature and the flow condition attached.

**Turbidity** measures how much light a sample scatters, reported in nephelometric turbidity units. It is a proxy, not a constituent: clay, silt, fine organic matter, algae and dissolved coloured compounds all raise it. USGS notes that many rivers run below 10 NTU at base flow and rise sharply during storms as material is washed in and stirred up from the bed. Its significance is largely about what rides with the particles — metals and bacteria attach to them, and suspended material shelters microorganisms from disinfection.

**Specific conductance** measures the ability of the water to carry current, which scales with the concentration of dissolved ions. It is the cheapest window onto the dissolved-solids load, and it is also the parameter that reveals the slow chemical change discussed at the end of this page.

## Why an invertebrate assemblage records more than a water sample

A chemical sample describes the instant it was drawn. An organism that lived in the reach for a year integrates everything that passed through it — including the pulse that arrived on a weekend between sampling visits, and the brief low-oxygen episode that no one was there to catch.

That is the argument for **biotic indices**: assemblages of benthic macroinvertebrates or fish, scored against the assemblage expected at a reference site of the same type. Sensitivity varies sharply across taxa, so composition carries information that abundance alone does not — the same reasoning that underpins composite [indicators of ecosystem health](/en/ecology/biodiversity/biodiversity-monitoring-and-ecosystem-health). The EPA's National Rivers and Streams Assessment builds its biological condition class this way, from the abundance and diversity of bottom-dwelling invertebrates — stonefly and dragonfly larvae, snails, worms, beetles — and its condition results are discussed alongside the flow record in the companion article on [rivers and their flow regimes](/en/ecology/freshwater/river-ecology-and-flow-regimes).

Biological monitoring has its own weaknesses, and they are the mirror image of the chemical ones. It integrates, so it cannot say when the damage happened or what caused it. It depends on a reference condition that must be defined somewhere, and reference sites are scarce in heavily modified catchments. And it responds to physical habitat and flow as much as to chemistry, which is a feature when you want ecological condition and a confound when you want to attribute a cause.

## How a number becomes a legal threshold

A standard is a chain of decisions, each of which could have gone otherwise, and the chain is usually invisible in the headline figure.

The EPA's 2024 national primary drinking water regulation for PFAS shows every link. It set two kinds of number: a maximum contaminant level goal, which is health-based and non-enforceable, and a maximum contaminant level, which is enforceable and also reflects what can be measured and treated. For PFOA and PFOS the goal was set at zero and the enforceable limit at 4.0 parts per trillion. PFHxS, PFNA and HFPO-DA each received both goal and limit at 10 parts per trillion, and mixtures of two or more of those three plus PFBS were addressed with a unitless hazard index of 1. Compliance is judged on running annual averages at a sampling point, not on any single result.

Standards are also revisable, and revision is not a scientific event alone. In May 2026 the EPA announced two proposed rules on the same regulation: one retaining the PFOA and PFOS limits while offering water systems two additional years, to 2031, to comply, and one that would rescind the regulations for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA and the hazard-index mixture. Reading a limit as a statement of where harm begins mistakes the nature of the object.

The World Health Organization takes a deliberately different approach in its *Guidelines for drinking-water quality*, now in a fourth edition incorporating three addenda. Rather than issuing binding numbers, it promotes locally relevant standards built around health-based targets, catchment-to-consumer risk management through water safety plans, and independent surveillance to confirm those plans are working — an acknowledgement that a threshold without a monitoring and management system behind it does very little.

## Detection is not exceedance, and the floor keeps moving

Analytical chemistry has outrun toxicology for a widening class of compounds, and the results of that mismatch are visible in the same national survey.

In the 2018–19 rivers and streams assessment, the cyanobacterial toxins microcystin and cylindrospermopsin were detected in 9 and 10 per cent of river and stream miles respectively — and exceeded EPA recommended criteria in none of the samples. Detection and exceedance are different claims, and conflating them is one of the more consequential errors in reporting contaminant results.

The reverse mismatch is starker. PFOS was detected in 91 per cent of the 290 fish composite samples analysed for that survey, and EPA states plainly that it is not comparing those concentrations to screening levels, because the toxicity assessment used to derive screening levels is still in draft. There is a measurement and no threshold to judge it against. Where thresholds do exist, they carry assumptions about the person: total PCBs exceeded screening levels across 45 per cent of the sampled river miles for general fish consumers and 74 per cent for high-frequency consumers — the same tissue, two answers, because the exposure assumption changed.

A non-detect, meanwhile, is a statement about the method rather than about the water. Reporting limits fall as instrumentation improves, which means a substance can appear to spread across a monitoring network when what actually spread was the ability to see it. Comparing detection frequencies across decades without reconciling the reporting limits produces trends that are artefacts.

## The two failures the apparatus handles worst

The first is aggregation. Under the EU Water Framework Directive the overall ecological status of a water body is set by the **one out, all out** principle: the class is determined by whichever biological or supporting quality element scores worst. The European Environment Agency reported that in 2021 only 37 per cent of Europe's surface water bodies achieved good or high ecological status and 29 per cent achieved good chemical status — but that if two groups of long-lived, ubiquitous pollutants, mercury and brominated flame retardants, were set aside, 80 per cent would reach good chemical status rather than 29 per cent. Both figures are correct. They answer different questions, and only one of them tells you whether the local pressures on a given river have changed.

Where the reporting boundary is drawn moves the answer as well. The agency's own ecological-status indicator, updated in September 2025, gives 39.6 per cent for the same year on data electronically reported by 25 Member States, against 42.4 per cent for all 27 in the previous planning cycle — a comparison complicated by the fact that the current cycle contains no data from Finland or Hungary, and that excluding those two countries from the earlier analysis would have produced 40.5 per cent. Including Norway lifts the current figure to 47 per cent. The 2024 report, for its part, notes that its directive data came from the 19 Member States that had reported to the agency. None of those numbers is incorrect. Each answers a slightly different question about who was counted.

The second is slow chemical drift, because compliance monitoring is built to catch excursions. Analysis of long-term US records published in 2018 as an account of [freshwater salinization syndrome](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29311318/) found concurrent trends in specific conductance, pH, alkalinity and base cations, with salinization affecting 37 per cent and alkalinization 90 per cent of the drainage area of the contiguous United States over the past century, and rising pH at 66 per cent of 232 USGS monitoring sites — a trend that in many cases began decades before acid-rain regulation. Road salt, irrigation return flow, sewage, mining and the weathering of concrete and agricultural lime all contribute. No individual sample in that record was necessarily out of compliance. The change is in the baseline, and a threshold test cannot see a baseline moving.

Chemistry, biology and toxicology answer different questions and fail in opposite directions, which is why national programmes publish suites of indicators rather than a single water-quality score — the same reasoning that governs [nutrient loading and lake recovery](/en/ecology/freshwater/lake-ecology-and-eutrophication) and the reading of [freshwater population trends](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-biodiversity-decline). In aquifers the timescale problem compounds all of this: the flushing rate is the groundwater flow rate, so a contaminant that reaches the saturated zone stays measurable for far longer than it was released, and a sampling programme designed for a river will be badly calibrated for it, as [how aquifer depletion is measured](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion) sets out.
