Monitored freshwater vertebrate populations declined by an average of 85 per cent between 1970 and 2020, against 73 per cent for the global index that contains them. Those are the figures the Zoological Society of London publishes for the Living Planet Index, where freshwater is the worst-performing of the three realms reported. The freshwater number is also among the most methodologically sensitive headline figures in conservation science — not because it is wrong, but because what it measures is narrower than the sentence it usually appears in, and because a defensible change in the analysis moves it a long way. Both things have to be held at once.

Extinction risk is a separate currency, assessed against different criteria and reported separately; the multi-taxon Red List assessment of freshwater fauna and its coverage gaps are set out in the overview of [freshwater ecosystems](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained). This page is about the abundance record.

## What the index is an average of

The 2024 edition of the index draws on 34,836 population time series covering 5,495 species of mammals, birds, fishes, reptiles and amphibians. It works the way a price index works: each population's trajectory is converted to a rate of change, and the rates are aggregated. The output is an average rate of change in population size, not a count of animals and not a count of species.

ZSL's own documentation is unusually direct about what that does and does not mean. A decline of 85 per cent does not mean 85 per cent of freshwater animals are gone, nor that 85 per cent of populations are declining. About half the species in the index show an average decline; roughly half do not. Because positive and negative trends are close to balanced in number, the average is negative because the magnitude of the declines exceeds the magnitude of the increases — a small number of collapsing populations can move the index a long way. Species enter the index on the basis of whether robust trend data exist, not on whether they are threatened, so the sample is a convenience sample of the monitored world.

## The criticism that landed, and the one that is often overstated

Two published critiques matter, and they cut in opposite directions.

The first says the summary statistic is dominated by outliers. A 2020 *Nature* analysis, [clustered versus catastrophic global vertebrate declines](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2920-6), showed that the reported mean vertebrate decline of more than 50 per cent since 1970 was driven by fewer than 3 per cent of populations; removing those extreme decliners flipped the global trend to an increase. Reanalysing the index as clusters rather than a single mean, the authors found 16 taxonomic–geographic systems containing clusters of extreme decline, comprising about 1 per cent of populations and falling disproportionately on larger-bodied animals, and 7 containing extreme increases at about 0.4 per cent. The remaining 98.6 per cent of populations showed no mean global trend. Three systems — all in the Indo-Pacific — were declining strongly with high certainty.

That is a serious result about the arithmetic, and it is regularly misread as a finding that vertebrate populations are broadly fine. It is not. It says that a single global mean is the wrong summary for a distribution with heavy tails, and that identifying the clusters should inform how conservation effort is prioritised. Whether extreme declines are outliers to be discounted or the signal itself is a question about purpose, not about statistics.

The second critique runs the other way, and it comes from the index's own authors. A 2017 paper in *PLOS ONE* on [controlling for taxonomic bias in a global biodiversity indicator](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0169156) noted that the underlying database over-represents well-studied groups and regions, and rebuilt the index weighting each biogeographic realm by its estimated species richness. Across 14,152 populations of 3,706 species from 3,095 data sources, the global vertebrate decline for 1970–2012 came out at 58 per cent under proportional weighting against 20 per cent without it. For freshwater specifically the figure moved from 46 to 81 per cent; marine went from a 12 per cent increase to a 36 per cent decline, and terrestrial from a 15 per cent increase to a 38 per cent decline. The authors' conclusion is the important one: the groups with the poorest data coverage appear to be declining faster than the well-monitored groups that dominate the raw database.

Put the two critiques together and the honest position is narrow. The freshwater index is sensitive to weighting and to the treatment of extreme values, and both sensitivities have been quantified. Neither analysis supports reading the freshwater realm as stable.

## The largest animals, where the signal is least ambiguous

Body size cuts through some of the ambiguity, because large freshwater animals are conspicuous and their historical ranges comparatively well documented — though the synthesis below is explicit that even for them the record is limited by a lack of monitoring data and by taxonomic and spatial bias.

A 2019 synthesis in *Global Change Biology* on [the global decline of freshwater megafauna](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31393076/) — animals reaching 30 kg or more — compiled population data for 126 species from the Living Planet Database and the literature, together with distribution data for 44 species in Europe and the United States. Globally, those populations fell by 88 per cent between 1970 and 2012, with the Indomalayan and Palearctic realms at −99 and −97 per cent. Mega-fishes declined most, at −94 per cent. Ranges contracted as well: 42 per cent of European freshwater megafauna species had lost more than 40 per cent of their historical distribution.

Those are the taxa most likely to be caught by the outlier critique, since the extreme-decline clusters fall disproportionately on large animals. The two results are consistent rather than contradictory. Large freshwater vertebrates really are collapsing; they are also a small and unrepresentative slice of freshwater biodiversity.

## A counter-signal, and how to hold it

Not every freshwater trend points downward, and the clearest exception deserves stating plainly. A 2020 meta-analysis in *Science* of [terrestrial and freshwater insect abundance trends](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32327596/), compiling 166 long-term surveys across 1,676 sites, reported an average decline of about 9 per cent per decade for terrestrial insects and an average *increase* of about 11 per cent per decade for freshwater insects, with considerable variation even between adjacent sites. Both patterns, the authors note, were driven largely by strong trends in North America and some European regions — which is to say by the places where long-running insect monitoring exists.

That paper carries an editorial expression of concern issued by the journal in January 2026, so it should be read as contested rather than as an established counterweight. Its structure is nonetheless instructive: the freshwater increase and the megafauna collapse were measured in the same realm over overlapping periods, in different taxa, using different data. A realm-level average cannot be true of both.

## Where the evidence is thinnest

The pattern in the gaps is consistent across every dataset discussed here, and it is not random.

Taxonomically, the record is vertebrate-heavy. The abundance index covers mammals, birds, fishes, reptiles and amphibians and no invertebrates at all, which excludes the great majority of freshwater animal species. Geographically, the database over-represents well-studied regions, which is the bias the diversity-weighted reanalysis was constructed to correct. Temporally, coverage is uneven within the database itself: ZSL notes that time series for birds, mammals and fishes are longer while amphibians are represented almost entirely by short series, and that long-term data are more often available for species and groups that are doing comparatively well — a bias that pushes the aggregate towards optimism before any weighting is applied.

The regional breakdown makes the stakes concrete. The 2024 index reports declines of 95 per cent for Latin America and the Caribbean and 76 per cent for Africa, within a range across IPBES regions of 35 to 95 per cent. Set that beside the weighting result: giving each biogeographic realm weight in proportion to its species richness makes the global decline steeper, which means the species-rich realms gaining weight are declining faster than the raw database implies. The regions doing most to set the global figure are, on that evidence, the ones whose contribution is least well sampled.

None of this makes the indices unusable. It makes them indicators of the monitored world that require their coverage stated alongside their value — and it means the mechanisms are often better evidence than the aggregates. Barrier density and sediment starvation are directly measured, as set out in the article on [dams and river fragmentation](/en/ecology/freshwater/dams-and-river-fragmentation); so are nutrient loads, contaminant detections and biotic condition classes, described under [what water-quality monitoring records](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained); so are aquifer drawdown and the loss of baseflow that sustains dry-season refuges, in [how groundwater depletion is measured](/en/ecology/freshwater/groundwater-and-aquifer-depletion). Where a pressure can be quantified directly, it is usually the sounder basis for a claim than a realm-wide average of the species that happened to be counted. The construction of the index itself, and how it compares with other headline measures, is treated separately in [the Living Planet Index explained](/en/ecology/biodiversity/living-planet-index-explained).
