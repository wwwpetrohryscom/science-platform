Begin with the count, because the count is not known. The FAO's geo-referenced dams database holds records for over 14,000 structures and states in its own documentation that the files are "neither complete nor can be considered error-free". A 2020 survey published in *Nature* as [an inventory of more than one million barriers in Europe's rivers](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-3005-2) found at least 1.2 million instream barriers across 36 European countries at a mean density of 0.74 per kilometre — and, crucially, that 68 per cent of them are structures less than two metres high. Standardised walkover surveys along 2,715 km of stream on 147 rivers indicated that existing records underestimate barrier numbers by roughly 61 per cent.

That result reframes the whole subject. The ecological problem is not principally the large, named structures; it is the weirs, fords, culverts, sluices and mill dams that nobody counted, distributed at roughly one per kilometre through the drainage network. This page follows the physical consequences of that density, and then asks what the removal record can actually support. The flow-regime side of the argument — magnitude, timing, rate of change, and how altered hydrographs are measured — belongs to the companion article on [rivers and flow regimes](/en/ecology/freshwater/river-ecology-and-flow-regimes), and the wider context to the overview of [freshwater ecosystems](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained).

## What connectivity means, and how much of it is left

Rivers are connected in four directions, and a barrier does not affect them equally. Longitudinal connectivity is movement up and down the channel; lateral connectivity links the channel to its floodplain; vertical connectivity joins surface water to the sediments and groundwater beneath; and temporal connectivity is the seasonal sequencing that makes the other three usable by organisms whose life histories are timed to it. The patch-and-edge statistics developed for [terrestrial habitat fragmentation](/en/ecology/biodiversity/habitat-fragmentation-metrics) map awkwardly onto a branching network in which every patch is upstream or downstream of every other, which is why [river connectivity](/en/glossary/river-connectivity) is assessed with metrics of its own.

The global assessment of longitudinal connectivity is unambiguous about how much has been lost. Assessing 12 million kilometres of river, the 2019 *Nature* study [mapping the world's free-flowing rivers](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1111-9) found that only 37 per cent of rivers longer than 1,000 km remain free-flowing over their entire length, and 23 per cent flow uninterrupted to the ocean. The very long free-flowing rivers that remain are concentrated in remote parts of the Arctic and the Amazon and Congo basins. An earlier global overview had already found that 172 of 292 large river systems were affected by dams, including the eight most biogeographically diverse.

A barrier's effect also propagates well beyond the structure. Upstream, the impoundment converts running water to standing water and everything organised by current — oxygen delivery, particle transport, the substrate itself — changes with it, which is why an impoundment behaves in many respects like the systems described under [lake stratification and nutrient loading](/en/ecology/freshwater/lake-ecology-and-eutrophication). Downstream, the released water carries a different temperature, a different daily and seasonal rhythm, and a much smaller sediment load.

## Sediment: the effect that travels furthest

Of all the consequences, sediment starvation reaches the greatest distance from the dam, because a reservoir is a settling basin and what it retains never reaches the coast.

A satellite-based reconstruction of suspended sediment for 414 major rivers, published in *Science* in 2022 as [an analysis of rapid changes to global river suspended sediment flux](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35737794/), found that sediment trapping by dams in the global hydrologic north has contributed to a decline in global sediment flux to 49 per cent of pre-dam conditions. Meanwhile, intensive land-use change in the global hydrologic south has raised river suspended sediment concentrations by an average of 41 ± 7 per cent relative to the 1980s. The net result is not a simple global decline but a redistribution: the dominant source of suspended sediment delivered to the oceans has shifted from Asia to South America.

Two things follow. Deltas and coastal plains below heavily dammed basins lose the material that sustains them against subsidence and rising sea level, so a barrier's signature turns up far downstream of anything that looks like its consequence. And a global average conceals opposite regional trends, which makes any single figure for "sediment change" close to meaningless without the basin attached.

## Reservoir greenhouse gases, and why the range is so wide

Impoundments flood organic matter and then process it, and quantifying what comes off the water surface has proved unusually hard. A 2022 *Nature Geoscience* reconstruction of [reservoir CO₂ and CH₄ emissions from 1900 to 2060](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-022-01004-2) puts the global reservoir carbon footprint at 0.73–2.41 PgCO₂-equivalent per year. That spread — more than a factor of three from end to end — is the honest state of the estimate rather than a defect in the study.

The same analysis found that global reservoir carbon emissions peaked in 1987 and have declined since, as carbon dioxide emissions fall with reservoir age, while reservoir-induced radiative forcing continues to rise because methane emissions keep increasing; reservoir methane accounted for 5.2 per cent of global anthropogenic methane emissions in 2020. The authors project that ebullition and degassing — bubbles rising through the water column and gas released as water passes through turbines and spillways — will account for more than 75 per cent of future reservoir-induced radiative forcing.

Those two pathways are precisely the hardest to measure. Bubbling is episodic and spatially patchy, so a campaign that misses an event underestimates badly; degassing happens at the outlet rather than the surface and is often not sampled at all. The width of the global range is largely a statement about how emissions vary in space and time within a single reservoir, and about which pathways a given study instrumented.

## What removal has actually shown

Dam removal has become common enough to generate evidence, and the evidence is more interesting than either the promotional or the sceptical account of it.

The removal of two large dams on the Elwha River in Washington State between 2011 and 2014 remains the best-instrumented case. It released over 10 million tonnes of sediment to the coast. A study of [the nearshore subtidal response](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0187742) tracked bathymetry, grain size, turbidity and dive surveys before and during removal: kelp and foliose red algae, which together covered more than half of the surveyed subtidal seabed, were greatly reduced or eliminated where persistent deposits formed, and declined in proportion to suspended sediment where they did not. Most invertebrate and fish taxa thinned in depositional areas, but bivalves increased where mud settled over sand, and flatfish and Pacific sand lance increased where sand settled over gravel. As removal neared completion the water column cleared and macroalgae began to return.

Upstream the picture was similarly mixed and similarly slow. Riparian sampling before and after removal, reported in *Ecological Applications* in 2022 as a test of whether [dam removal restores downstream riparian vegetation diversity](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eap.2591), found native plant richness had been 41 per cent lower below the dams than in the upstream reference reach; six years after removal began it had risen by about 31 per cent in the reach between the two former dam sites, while non-native richness and cover appeared unaffected either way. Sediment deposition had opposite effects in the lowest segment depending on landform, increasing native richness on floodplains and decreasing it elsewhere.

Smaller structures give a cleaner but still complicated signal. Monitoring of a low-head dam removal and fishway installation on a tributary of the Jinsha River in southwest China, sampled from 2018 to 2023 and reported as [fish community dynamics following removal and fish passage](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39393694/), recorded a sharp increase in upstream fish abundance after removal alongside a slight short-term decrease in species richness, and found that a fishway built at another dam on the same river raised both richness and abundance and narrowed the difference between the communities above and below it.

The common thread is that the immediate response is dominated by the sediment pulse, that recovery is directional but not fast, and that "recovery" is measured against a reference reach whose own condition is not fixed. The removal literature is also geographically narrow: it is dominated by small and medium structures in temperate North America and Europe, monitored for years rather than decades. The evidence supports the claim that removing a barrier restores movement and returns sediment to the system. It does not yet support confident prediction of how long a given river takes to reorganise, and the consequences for the species those movements sustain are taken up in [the freshwater biodiversity record](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-biodiversity-decline).
