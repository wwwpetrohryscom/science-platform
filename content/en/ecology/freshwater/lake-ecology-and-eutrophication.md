Lake Baikal alone holds over 23,000 cubic kilometres of water — set against the 91,000 cubic kilometres the USGS assigns to all fresh lakes worldwide, that is roughly a quarter of the total, and enough that the combined volume of the North American Great Lakes would be needed to refill it. The concentration is typical of lakes as a class: a satellite census counted about 117 million water bodies larger than 0.002 square kilometres, yet a handful of very large basins hold most of the water. It is also a reminder that "lake" covers a range of physical systems whose behaviour has more to do with depth and mixing than with size.

## The density column, and the two events that break it

Fresh water reaches maximum density near 4 °C, and warms and cools around that point. In a lake deep enough to resist wind mixing, summer heating produces three layers: a warm, well-mixed **epilimnion** at the surface; a **metalimnion** across which temperature drops sharply; and a cold, dark **hypolimnion** beneath. The density difference across the middle layer is what does the work — it is large enough that wind energy at the surface cannot mix through it, so for months the bottom water is hydraulically isolated from the atmosphere.

Isolation has a consequence that governs much of what follows. Organic matter produced at the surface sinks into the hypolimnion and is decomposed there by bacteria, consuming oxygen that cannot be replaced from above until the stratification breaks down. In much of the temperate zone that happens twice a year, when surface cooling in autumn and warming in spring bring the whole column to a similar temperature and wind can turn it over. Deeper or warmer lakes may mix only once a year, or incompletely, and some tropical and very deep lakes effectively never mix to the bottom.

The productivity of the surface layer therefore sets the oxygen deficit at depth, with a lag of months. This is the mechanism by which nutrient enrichment becomes a habitat problem rather than merely an aesthetic one, and why in a stratified lake the damage shows up first in a place nobody is looking at.

## How phosphorus was identified as the lever

Through the 1960s it was genuinely contested whether carbon, nitrogen or phosphorus limited algal growth in lakes, and the argument mattered because sewage treatment for each is differently expensive. It was settled by manipulating entire lakes rather than bottles.

At Canada's Experimental Lakes Area, a small hourglass-shaped lake was divided at its narrows with a curtain. Both basins received equal amounts of nitrate and sucrose — nitrogen and carbon; only one also received phosphorus. The 1974 report of that work in *Science* records that the phosphate-enriched basin quickly became highly eutrophic, while the basin receiving only nitrogen and carbon remained at pre-fertilisation conditions. A contrast that ran across two halves of one lake made an argument that a regression could not.

A companion experiment ran far longer. A second lake at the same station was fertilised for 37 years, with phosphorus held constant while nitrogen inputs were progressively reduced and then, for the final 16 years (1990-2005), withheld entirely. The lake stayed highly eutrophic. Cutting nitrogen selected for nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria, which drew the shortfall from the atmosphere, so biomass continued to track phosphorus. The reading of that result in *PNAS*, where the experiment was reported, was blunt: reducing nitrogen alone made the problem worse, and phosphorus control has to be part of any management programme.

Two caveats travel with this. The experimental lakes are small, boreal and shield-bounded, and the result does not transfer unmodified to estuaries and coastal waters, where nitrogen limitation is common. And the finding concerns what limits algal biomass, not what determines which species produce toxins — a separate question with a weaker evidence base.

## Why cutting the load does not buy a quick recovery

Reducing external phosphorus input is necessary and frequently insufficient, because a lake that has been enriched for decades has been storing phosphorus in its sediments the whole time. Some of that store is bound to iron compounds whose grip depends on redox conditions, and some is in labile organic forms. When the hypolimnion goes anoxic — which enrichment itself makes more likely — iron-bound phosphorus is released back into the water column. The lake fertilises itself.

Reviews of [internal loading](/en/glossary/internal-loading) in shallow, enriched lakes make the timescale explicit: the duration of the recovery period depends on the loading history and may last for decades where sediment phosphorus has accumulated. Net release rates and the length of the release season do decline progressively as recovery proceeds, so the trajectory is a slow decay rather than a plateau — but a monitoring programme that expects water quality to follow catchment measures within a few years will read a successful intervention as a failure.

## Shallow lakes have two states, not a gradient

Shallow lakes complicate the picture further, because they can occupy either of two self-reinforcing conditions at the same nutrient level. In the clear state, rooted plants cover the bed, stabilise sediment, shelter zooplankton that graze algae, and hold nutrients out of the water column. In the turbid state, phytoplankton shade the plants out, sediment is resuspended by wind and by bottom-feeding fish, and the conditions that would let plants re-establish are absent.

Because each state maintains itself, the transition between them is not symmetrical. A lake pushed into turbidity by rising nutrients often does not return when nutrients fall to the level at which it flipped; it has to be taken considerably lower. The general theory of such shifts was set out in a 2001 paper in *Nature* on [catastrophic shifts in ecosystems](https://www.nature.com/articles/35098000), which draws on lakes alongside coral reefs, oceans, forests and arid lands and argues that it is a loss of resilience that usually paves the way for a switch — which is also why [indicators of ecosystem resilience](/en/ecology/biodiversity/ecosystem-resilience-indicators) try to measure the capacity to absorb change rather than the current state alone. Practically, it means that a restoration target expressed as a nutrient concentration may be unreachable without an additional intervention — biomanipulation, sediment treatment, water-level drawdown — to break the feedback.

## What a national survey sees when it samples every kind of lake

The US EPA's 2022 National Lakes Assessment sampled a statistical cross-section of lakes across the conterminous United States, from prairie potholes to reservoirs. Nutrients were the most widespread stressor measured: 50 per cent of lakes were in poor condition for phosphorus and 47 per cent for nitrogen, chlorophyll *a* was rated poor in 49 per cent, and 30 per cent of lakes were hypereutrophic. Benthic macroinvertebrate communities were in poor condition in 29 per cent of lakes and zooplankton in 25 per cent. Where phosphorus was elevated, invertebrate communities were 1.7 times more likely to be in poor condition, rising to 2.2 times in natural lakes as opposed to reservoirs.

Cyanotoxins were common at low concentrations and rare at high ones. Microcystins were detected in 50 per cent of lakes but exceeded the EPA's recreational criterion in 2 per cent, representing about 5,360 lakes. Cylindrospermopsin was detected in 12 per cent and exceeded the criterion nowhere in the survey.

The change signal between 2017 and 2022 is where interpretation has to be careful. Nutrient and biological indicators showed little national change, and the small share of lakes exceeding the microcystin health benchmark did not change significantly — but *detection* of microcystins rose by almost 30 percentage points, to 50 per cent. A rise in detections at concentrations far below a threshold, with no change in exceedances, is the kind of result that can reflect analytical sensitivity, sampling conditions or a genuine environmental shift, and a single survey pair cannot separate them. The general problem of reading detection frequency as an environmental trend is taken up in [water quality measurement](/en/ecology/freshwater/water-quality-measurement-explained).

## Warming changes the physics beneath the chemistry

The first worldwide synthesis of in situ and satellite lake temperature records found that lake summer surface water temperatures rose at a global mean of 0.34 °C per decade between 1985 and 2009. The more useful finding was about variance: warming rates depend on combinations of climate and local characteristics rather than on lake location alone, so, in the authors' phrasing, regional consistency in lake warming is the exception rather than the rule. Seasonally ice-covered lakes in areas where temperature and solar radiation were rising while cloud cover fell warmed at 0.72 °C per decade; ice-free lakes with rising air temperature and solar radiation warmed at 0.53.

This matters for eutrophication because warmer surface water strengthens and lengthens stratification, which extends the period of hypolimnetic isolation and therefore the oxygen deficit, while also favouring buoyant cyanobacteria over other phytoplankton. A lake can consequently deteriorate under a constant nutrient load. It also means catchment nutrient targets set against a mid-twentieth-century thermal regime are being applied to a lake that no longer has one — the same non-stationarity that complicates [flow regime baselines in rivers](/en/ecology/freshwater/river-ecology-and-flow-regimes), and one reason the [freshwater ecosystems overview](/en/ecology/freshwater/freshwater-ecosystems-explained) treats reference conditions as estimates rather than facts.
