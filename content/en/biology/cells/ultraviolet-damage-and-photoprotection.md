The membrane that makes [a cell](/en/biology/cells/what-is-a-cell) possible does not stop ultraviolet light, and ultraviolet light damages DNA because DNA absorbs it. That sounds obvious and is the entire explanation for why the damage is so wavelength-specific: absorption is a resonance phenomenon, and the bases absorb in a narrow band that overlaps only part of the ultraviolet spectrum reaching the ground.

## The three ultraviolet bands, and which one matters

A 2015 review in *Photochemistry and Photobiology* sets out the division: "UVA (315–400 nm), UVB (280–315 nm) and UVC (100–280 nm)."

UVC is the most energetic and is essentially absent at ground level, absorbed by atmospheric ozone and oxygen. That leaves a striking asymmetry between the other two, which the review states plainly: "UVB forms only about 5% of all UV radiation reaching the earth's surface, which effectively causes DNA damage", while "UVA forms about 95% of all UV radiation entering the earth, but is weaker than UVB."

Ninety-five per cent of the dose does relatively little direct damage to DNA and five per cent does most of it. The reason is not intensity but overlap: UVB sits where the nucleic acid bases absorb, and UVA largely does not.

## What the damage is

Direct absorption produces two characteristic lesions. As the review puts it, "UVB and UVC are absorbed directly by DNA, causing the formation of thymine dimers, mainly cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPD) and pyrimidine (6-4) pyrimidone photoproducts (6-4PP)."

Both are covalent links between adjacent pyrimidines on the same strand. Neither breaks the backbone, and that matters: the lesion is a distortion rather than a cut, so the cell's problem is recognising a shape rather than rejoining an end. That is a different repair problem from the one [ionising radiation](/en/biology/genetics/dna-damage-and-repair-after-radiation) creates, and it is solved by a different pathway.

The functional consequence is blockage. A distorted template stalls replication and transcription, and a stalled polymerase is what triggers the damage response.

## Nucleotide excision repair, and the enzyme humans do not have

Human cells cut the lesion out. The review describes the geometry of the incision: "XPA and RPA define the cleavage sites and strand specificity, to which XPG… and the nuclease complex XPF-ERCC1… bind to cleave the damaged site, followed by its excision", after which "The excised portion is replaced with a newly synthesized patch with the help of proliferating-cell nuclear antigen (PCNA)."

Cutting on both sides of a lesion and resynthesising the gap is an expensive way to fix a bond that light created and light could undo. Many organisms do exactly that: photolyases bind the dimer and use blue light to split it, restoring the two bases directly, in a single step with no excision.

The review notes the loss: although "a primitive, more efficient DNA repair mechanism involving photolyases has been identified, it is absent in humans."

This is worth dwelling on, because it inverts a common assumption about evolution. The simpler, cheaper, faster mechanism is the ancestral one, and the lineage leading to placental mammals lost it. What replaced it is more versatile — nucleotide excision repair handles bulky lesions of many kinds, not only photoproducts — but for this particular job it is strictly worse.

## What follows for skin and for sunscreen

Three consequences, in order of how often they are stated correctly.

**The action spectrum is not the intensity spectrum.** A measure of ultraviolet exposure weighted by total energy misrepresents biological risk, because the damaging band is a small part of the total. Erythemal weighting exists for exactly this reason.

**UVA is not harmless, but its route is indirect.** It generates reactive oxygen species that damage DNA and other molecules without being absorbed by the bases. Different mechanism, different lesion spectrum, different repair pathway.

**Repair capacity is the variable that matters clinically.** Two people with the same exposure differ in outcome partly through repair, which is why inherited defects in the excision pathway produce extreme photosensitivity and enormously elevated skin cancer risk. The pathway is not a background detail; it is the thing standing between an ordinary afternoon and a mutation.
