A skeleton that never changed would be useless to a cell. The structures that give a cell its shape are the same ones that have to dismantle and rebuild that shape to divide, crawl, or move a vesicle a metre down an axon. The way the cytoskeleton resolves that is to spend energy staying unstable — and the clearest line through the whole subject is which filaments do that and which do not. Responsiveness to the environment is the third requirement in the [operational account of what a cell is](/en/biology/cells/what-is-a-cell), and mechanical response is where much of it is executed.

## Three polymers, two nucleotides, one that opts out

| | Actin filaments | Microtubules | Intermediate filaments |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diameter | about 7 nm | about 25 nm | about 10 nm |
| Subunit | globular actin, 375 amino acids, 43 kd | α/β-tubulin dimers in 13 protofilaments | more than 50 proteins in six classes |
| Nucleotide bound | ATP | GTP | none |
| Structural polarity | yes | yes | no |
| Characteristic behaviour | treadmilling, rapid turnover | dynamic instability | stable load-bearing |

The first two rows are the ones every textbook gives. The third is the one that explains the rest. Actin binds and hydrolyses ATP; tubulin binds and hydrolyses GTP; intermediate filament proteins bind no nucleotide at all, and correspondingly do not treadmill, do not show dynamic instability, and do not serve as tracks. Their role is mechanical — providing tensile strength to cells and tissues — which is why mutations in them produce structural failures such as the skin blistering of epidermolysis bullosa simplex rather than defects of transport or division.

Actin is also simply abundant. It runs at 5 to 10 per cent of total protein in ordinary eukaryotic cells and around 20 per cent in muscle, which is a substantial fraction of a cell's protein budget committed to one polymer.

## Polarity is what makes a filament into a track

Actin filaments and microtubules are structurally polar: their two ends are chemically distinct and grow at different rates, with an actin filament's plus end elongating five to ten times faster than its minus end. Polarity does two things. It permits treadmilling, in which subunits add at one end and leave at the other so the filament persists while its material turns over. And it gives motor proteins a direction to read.

That second consequence is what converts a structural polymer into an addressing system. A microtubule radiating from the centre of a cell has a defined orientation, so a motor that walks towards plus ends moves cargo outward and a motor that walks towards minus ends moves it inward. Without polarity there is no outward and no inward.

## Instability that is paid for

Microtubules in cells typically switch between growth and shrinkage several times a minute, a behaviour named **dynamic instability**: an individual microtubule grows steadily, then converts abruptly to rapid depolymerisation, then may rescue and grow again. Actin filaments in a motile cell can persist for only a few minutes.

Read as engineering this looks perverse, and it is the point at which the nucleotide matters. GTP hydrolysis after tubulin adds to a growing end means the bulk of a microtubule is in a less stable, GDP-bound state held together by a cap of newly added subunits; lose the cap and the polymer disassembles. The cell is paying, in GTP, to keep its own scaffolding one step from collapse. What it buys is search speed. A network that constantly probes and retracts can find a kinetochore, a cortical attachment site, or a new direction of travel far faster than a stable network could be dismantled and rebuilt — and the same property makes the mitotic spindle able to capture chromosomes during [the brief window of nuclear division](/en/biology/cells/cell-division-mitosis-and-meiosis).

## An 8-nanometre step

Motor proteins convert nucleotide hydrolysis into directed movement along these tracks. Kinesin-1 moves towards microtubule plus ends in discrete steps of 8.2 nm, matching the 8.15 nm tubulin-dimer spacing of the lattice it walks on, and hydrolyses a single ATP per mechanical step. A wild-type motor takes on the order of 100 steps before releasing from the microtubule.

The molecules themselves are asymmetric in size and role. Kinesin is about 380 kd — two 120-kd heavy chains and two 64-kd light chains — with a motor domain of roughly 340 amino acids, considerably smaller than myosin's motor domain of about 850. Cytoplasmic dynein is far larger, up to 2,000 kd with heavy chains of about 500 kd each, and every member of the dynein family moves towards microtubule minus ends. Kinesins, by contrast, are a large and functionally varied family: 18 are encoded in the *Caenorhabditis elegans* genome, and there may be as many as 100 in humans.

The scale this operates over is easy to underestimate. The longest human axon runs from the base of the spinal cord to the tip of the big toe, up to a metre, and plus-end-directed kinesins carry material along it at a rate that covers that metre in about a day. Neuronal maintenance is, to a large extent, a logistics problem, which is one reason [nervous tissue](/en/biology/physiology/nervous-systems-and-neurons) is so sensitive to transport failures.

## Beating and crawling are different mechanical problems

A cilium or flagellum is a stable, precisely built structure: an axoneme about 0.25 µm across in which nine outer microtubule doublets surround a central pair. Each doublet is one complete 13-protofilament A tubule and one incomplete B tubule of 10 or 11 protofilaments, with two dynein arms attached to each A tubule. Cilia are around 10 µm long; flagella can reach 200 µm.

The motion is generated by conversion, not by contraction. Dynein bases on one doublet's A tubule engage the neighbouring doublet's B tubule and try to slide it. Because the doublets are cross-linked and cannot slide freely past one another, the sliding force is converted into bending — which is the beat. When this apparatus is defective, the consequences track the places where directed fluid movement matters: primary ciliary dyskinesia has an estimated prevalence of about 1 in 10,000, roughly half of affected people have a complete mirror-image arrangement of the major organs, and, as of the 2017 review cited here, pathogenic variants in more than 30 genes had been published, accounting for an estimated 60 to 70 per cent of cases — a share its authors expected to rise as further genes were found.

Crawling works on an entirely different principle. Instead of a fixed structure with motors distributed along it, a migrating cell polymerises actin at its leading edge, pushing the [plasma membrane](/en/biology/cells/cell-membrane-structure-and-transport) forward, while adhering to the substrate behind the protrusion and contracting at the rear. The filaments are disposable, the geometry is renegotiated continuously, and the direction is set by [signalling at the cell surface](/en/biology/cells/cell-signaling-pathways-basics) rather than by an inherited structure.

## Why tubulin became a drug target

Because dividing cells must build a spindle, microtubules are among the oldest targets in cancer chemotherapy: taxanes stabilise them, vinca alkaloids destabilise them, and both are long-established treatments. The mechanistic detail is more interesting than the stabilise-or-destabilise framing suggests. At the higher concentrations used in early laboratory work, these agents grossly polymerise or depolymerise the microtubule mass. At the lower, clinically relevant concentrations, both classes act by slowing microtubule *dynamics* rather than by destroying the polymer, and the resulting failure to satisfy the spindle checkpoint produces mitotic arrest.

The costs follow from the fact that the target is not specific to tumours. Neurotoxicity is the dose-limiting toxicity of vincristine, and peripheral neuropathy remains dose-limiting for the epothilone analogue ixabepilone, reported in approximately 65 per cent of treated patients. Long axons depend on exactly the transport described above, so a drug that interferes with microtubule dynamics has a predictable second effect on the peripheral nervous system.

The antimitotic explanation for the clinical effect is also incomplete, and known to be. Tumour cells in a patient divide far more slowly than cultured cells, and a [2013 review in *Drug Discovery Today*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4009386/) reasons from that mismatch that these agents predominantly hit interphase cells in patients, while also exerting substantial immunomodulatory effects that contribute to tumour rejection. Which of those mechanisms is decisive the same review leaves open.

## Numbers from a test tube, filaments in a cell

Almost every quantitative parameter in this article — step sizes, elongation rates, critical concentrations, switching frequencies — comes from purified components in dilute solution, or from an optical-trap assay under a defined load. The cytoplasm is neither dilute nor unloaded. It is crowded to the point where macromolecules occupy a substantial fraction of the volume, cross-linked by proteins that were not in the reconstituted mixture, and under tension generated elsewhere in the same network.

How closely a reconstituted number transfers to a filament embedded in that environment is a standing question rather than a solved one. It is the main reason cytoskeletal mechanics is still argued about at the level of the numbers and not only at the level of the mechanisms, and it is worth holding in mind whenever a figure like 8.2 nm is quoted as though it were a constant of nature rather than a measurement made under particular conditions.
