Mitosis is an error-correction problem. Meiosis is a randomisation problem. Both end with chromosomes distributed into new nuclei, which is why they are taught together and often conflated, but the specification each has to meet is close to opposite: one must produce two cells that are as nearly identical to the parent as physically achievable, the other must produce four that are deliberately, systematically different. The machinery that follows makes sense only against that difference. Self-replication is the second of the three requirements that [define a cell operationally](/en/biology/cells/what-is-a-cell), and these are the two ways cells meet it.

## Division is a small part of the cycle

In a typical human cell proliferating in culture, interphase occupies about 23 hours of a 24-hour cycle and M phase about one. S phase alone takes 10 to 12 hours, roughly half the total. The visible drama of chromosome segregation is therefore a brief event at the end of a long preparation, and most of what determines whether it succeeds happened hours earlier.

That ratio is not fixed. Early embryonic cycles in some animals run S and M phases of about 15 minutes each with no detectable G1 or G2 at all — because there is nothing to grow, no external signal to consult, and a stockpile of maternal material already in place. The gap phases exist to make room for decisions, not for chemistry.

## Control is a licensing system, not a timer

The cell-cycle control system is built on cyclin-dependent kinases, which have no kinase activity unless bound to a cyclin. Cyclin concentrations rise and fall, so Cdk activity does too, and the cyclical activation of successive cyclin-Cdk complexes drives the cycle forward. Layered over that oscillator are **checkpoints**: points at which the system pauses until a specific condition is confirmed.

The logic is worth stating carefully, because the word "checkpoint" invites the wrong mental model. These are not inspections that scan for faults. They are licences that are withheld until a positive signal arrives. The DNA damage checkpoints in G1 and G2 delay progression to allow repair, working alongside the [surveillance and repair machinery that acts on the DNA itself](/en/biology/genetics/dna-replication-and-repair). The spindle assembly checkpoint blocks anaphase until every chromosome is attached to the spindle, and it achieves that with an elegantly simple design: each unattached chromosome emits an inhibitory signal, so the attachment of the last one is detected by the disappearance of the last inhibition rather than by any counting.

## Five stages and a sixth

M phase divides into five stages of nuclear division plus cytokinesis. In prophase, replicated chromosomes condense while the cytoskeleton reorganises. In prometaphase the nuclear envelope breaks down and the spindle captures the chromosomes. At metaphase they are aligned at the spindle equator. In anaphase they are pulled to the two poles, and cytoplasmic division begins. Telophase completes it.

Every mechanical step there is performed by [filaments and motor proteins](/en/biology/cells/cytoskeleton-and-cell-motility): the spindle that captures and moves chromosomes is built from microtubules, and the contractile ring that pinches the cell in two is built from actin and myosin. Chromosome segregation is not a chemical process with a mechanical accompaniment; it is a mechanical process with chemical control.

## Meiosis makes variation the product

Meiosis runs one round of replication followed by two divisions, halving the chromosome number. Two mechanisms make its output diverse, and they are independent of each other.

The first is independent assortment. Each homologous pair orients on the meiosis I spindle without reference to any other pair, so a human cell with 23 pairs can produce at least 2²³, about 8.4 × 10⁶, genetically different gametes on that basis alone.

The second is crossing over. On average a human chromosome participates in two or three crossover events during meiosis, and every chromosome participates in at least one — the obligate crossover, which is a mechanical requirement for correct segregation as much as a source of variety. Genome-wide maps show a pronounced sex difference in the totals: recent work reports approximately 42 crossovers per maternal meiosis against approximately 26 per paternal meiosis. Because crossovers occur at different positions each time, they generate combinations that assortment alone cannot, which is what makes recombination a substrate for [selection acting on standing variation](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation) rather than only on new [mutations](/en/biology/genetics/mutation-types-and-rates).

## When segregation fails

Human meiosis is unusually error-prone. The frequency of chromosome missegregation in human oocytes is around 10 per cent of meioses — a rate no mitotic tissue would tolerate.

The consequences are visible in reproductive outcomes. A cytogenetic study of 340 first-trimester pregnancy losses found chromosomal abnormalities in 48.5 per cent of the conception products examined; aneuploidy accounted for 81.8 per cent of the abnormal cases, and trisomy for 86.7 per cent of the aneuploidies. The same study found abnormality rates rising with maternal age, reporting an odds ratio of 4.12 for women aged 37 and above compared with those aged 29 or younger.

Two cautions belong with those figures. Products-of-conception studies sample pregnancies that failed, which is exactly the population enriched for the abnormality being counted, so the proportions describe losses rather than conceptions in general. And a single-centre series of this size gives a usable estimate of proportions but not a population rate.

## Cancer as a control-system disease, and the limits of that framing

Uncontrolled proliferation is a failure of the licensing system described above, and the genes most often disrupted in cancers are the ones that administer it. TP53, which acts at the DNA damage checkpoints, is the single most commonly mutated cancer gene: an analysis in *Nature Communications* estimates it mutated in about 35 per cent of new cancer diagnoses in the United States.

That number is worth reading in both directions. Thirty-five per cent makes TP53 the clear leader — the next most frequent, PIK3CA, is at 13.5 per cent and KRAS at 10.5 per cent. It also means that the most common driver mutation in cancer is absent from roughly two thirds of new diagnoses. The authors' own framing is that common driver mutations are less common than previously believed. "Cancer is a cell-cycle-control disease" is a true statement about mechanism and a misleading one about genetics, because no small set of cell-cycle genes accounts for most cases, and the same clinical diagnosis routinely arises from different combinations of lesions.

## What these measurements cannot resolve

Crossover counts come from specific cohorts analysed with specific methods, and the maternal-paternal difference is better established than any particular pair of numbers. The oocyte missegregation estimate is a summary across studies using different detection methods, each with its own sensitivity to the errors it can see.

The maternal-age association in aneuploidy is robust and repeatedly observed, but the mechanism is not one thing: cohesion loss over decades of arrest, checkpoint permissiveness in oocytes, and selective survival of abnormal conceptions have all been proposed and are not mutually exclusive. Available data are too limited to apportion the effect among them with confidence.

And the checkpoint account of cancer is a model of how proliferation is normally restrained, not a complete causal account of how any individual tumour arises. Mutation frequency data describe what is altered, not which alterations are doing the work in a given tissue — a distinction that also shapes how [gene regulation](/en/biology/genetics/how-gene-expression-is-regulated) is read from sequence data.
