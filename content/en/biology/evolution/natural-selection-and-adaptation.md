Three conditions, holding simultaneously in a population, are sufficient to make its trait distribution shift between generations:

1. Individuals vary in some trait.
2. That variation is at least partly heritable — offspring resemble their parents in it more than they resemble the population average.
3. The variation is associated with differences in survival or reproduction.

Nothing else is required, and nothing in the argument is empirical. Grant the three premises and the conclusion follows; deny any one and it does not. This is why natural selection is so difficult to argue with in the abstract and so demanding to demonstrate in a particular case: each premise has to be established separately for the trait in front of you, which is exactly how field studies are built. Heritability is estimated from patterns of relatedness, fitness differences from survival or paternity data, and the trait variation is simply measured. The larger framework these estimates enter is set out in the overview of [how evolutionary change is partitioned among processes](/en/biology/evolution/evolution-explained).

## What "fitness" refers to, and what it does not

Fitness in this literature is a bookkeeping quantity: the number of offspring an individual contributes, usually expressed relative to the population mean. It is not vigour, health, longevity or complexity, and a genotype can be fitter in one environment and less fit in the next valley. Nothing about the definition implies that fitter organisms are better in any sense a reader would recognise outside the equation.

Because fitness is composite, selection can be measured through different components — survival, fecundity, mating success — and these need not agree. The 2001 review that remains the reference point for how strong selection actually is in nature found that estimates made through mating success were, on average, stronger than those made through survival. Which component a study happened to measure therefore partly determines what it reports, and it is one reason the evidential standards for [traits attributed to mate choice](/en/biology/evolution/sexual-selection-explained) are argued about separately from the rest of the field.

The standard measurement is the **selection gradient**, the partial regression of relative fitness on a standardised trait value, holding other measured traits constant. Its virtue over the simpler selection differential is that it separates direct selection on a trait from selection acting on something correlated with it — a distinction that matters whenever traits are measured in bundles, which is almost always.

## Three modes, and how often each is actually seen

| Mode | Trait values favoured | Signature in the gradients | How common in field estimates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Directional | One extreme | Non-zero linear gradient (β) | Median absolute β of 0.16; strong values uncommon |
| Stabilising | Intermediate | Negative quadratic gradient (γ) | Median absolute γ of 0.10; weak overall |
| Disruptive | Both extremes | Positive quadratic gradient (γ) | About as frequent as stabilising selection |

Those figures come from a synthesis of 63 published studies covering 62 species and more than 2,500 individual estimates, and they carry two uncomfortable implications. The first is that selection in the wild is usually weak: an exponential distribution of absolute linear gradients with a median of 0.16 is not the picture of relentless optimisation that popular accounts convey. The second is that the distribution of quadratic gradients was symmetric about zero, giving no evidence that stabilising selection is either stronger or more common than disruptive selection — a textbook claim that the data did not support.

The same synthesis noted that most published studies were unreplicated and had sample sizes below 135 individuals, which leaves them underpowered to detect selection of the magnitude typically reported. Weak power plus a publication process that favours significant results is a combination that inflates the apparent size of whatever does get published.

## Adaptation, exaptation, and the parts nothing selected for

A trait that improves fitness today is not automatically an adaptation for its current function. The vocabulary that keeps these apart is worth using precisely.

An **adaptation** is a trait whose form was shaped by selection for the function it now performs. An **exaptation** is a trait that performs a useful function it was not built by selection to perform — feathers used in flight having originated in a lineage that did not fly is the standard illustration. A **spandrel** is a byproduct of some other structural or developmental commitment, useful or not, that selection never targeted at all.

The third of those terms entered the field through a 1979 critique of what its authors called the adaptationist programme: the habit of atomising an organism into traits, proposing a plausible fitness benefit for each, and treating the plausibility as the finding. The criticism was never that adaptation is rare. It was that a story about why a trait would be useful is a hypothesis, and that the field had fallen into accepting it as a conclusion. Reading a trait's history off its current utility is the specific error, and it also explains why similar solutions in distant lineages need careful handling — a problem taken up in [what convergence implies about constraint](/en/biology/evolution/convergent-evolution-explained).

The same failure mode reappeared at the molecular level. A 2011 review argued that genes are routinely labelled adaptive on the strength of an association with a functionally important phenotype, or a nucleotide pattern consistent with positive selection, when neither establishes an effect on fitness. Its recommendation was blunt: experiments that test the targets and agents of selection in a genomic context are required before the label is applied. Regulatory change is a common substrate for such claims, which is why the mechanics described in [how gene expression is controlled](/en/biology/genetics/how-gene-expression-is-regulated) frequently sit underneath a proposed adaptation.

## What a real test looks like

Three field programmes show what the evidential bar looks like when it is actually met.

Rock pocket mice living on dark lava flows are dark; those on pale rock are pale, and the concealing coloration reduces predation. An association study using candidate pigmentation genes identified four mutations in the melanocortin-1 receptor gene, *Mc1r*, that account for melanism in one lava-dwelling population. The result that matters most, though, is the negative one: a second melanic population on a different lava flow showed no association with *Mc1r* at all. The same phenotype, under the same selective regime, had been built from different genetic material — which is a stronger statement about repeatability than either population alone could support.

In the peppered moth, the classical explanation of industrial melanism was directly challenged in the 1990s, and the response was an experiment rather than an argument. A six-year release programme involving 4,864 moths measured differential bird predation on pale and melanic forms and estimated daily selection against melanics of about 0.1 — sufficient in magnitude and direction to account for the observed decline of melanism in post-industrial Britain. The case is now well supported not because it is old but because it was re-tested when doubted.

The most instructive example is one where the adaptive hypothesis lost. Wild radish flowers show anther exsertion and a dimorphism in anther height, and a functional argument predicts that the dimorphism reduces pollen loss. A study combining single-visit pollen-removal measurements, paternity-based estimates of male fitness, experimental stamen removal and five generations of artificial selection found stabilising selection on anther exsertion, as expected — but marginally significant negative directional selection on the dimorphism itself, suggesting the trait as currently expressed may be maladaptive. A functional story that sounds compelling had survived only until someone measured fitness.

## Three limits built into every field estimate

Three limitations are structural rather than incidental. Field estimates of selection are snapshots, typically covering one or a few seasons, while the trait distributions they explain were built over far longer; a gradient measured in a drought year may reverse in the next wet one. Statistical power is chronically low, so published gradients are drawn from the upper tail of what was actually happening. And a signature of selection in sequence data is a statement about the past history of a locus, not a demonstration that a particular allele changes an organism's reproductive output now.

There is also a boundary condition that no amount of better fieldwork removes. Selection can only sort variants whose fitness differences are large enough to register above the random sampling of a finite population, so the answer to "is this trait under selection" depends on a parameter that has nothing to do with the trait itself — the interaction developed in [why effective population size decides whether weak selection matters](/en/biology/evolution/genetic-drift-and-population-size).

The cleanest demonstrations therefore share a profile: a strong coefficient, a short timescale, a named selective agent, and a population large enough that chance is not competing with the signal. [Drug pressure on bacterial populations](/en/biology/evolution/antibiotic-resistance-evolution-mechanisms) meets all four, which is why it, rather than any vertebrate field system, has become the standard demonstration case for [evolutionary adaptation](/en/glossary/evolutionary-adaptation). Most traits of interest to biologists meet none of them, and the honest position on those is that a selective explanation is a hypothesis with a measured effect size attached, not a settled account of the trait's history.
