A peacock's train, a stag's antlers and a bowerbird's construction are costly in energy, in predation risk, or in both. Natural selection on survival cannot account for them, and Darwin's answer was that they are paid for by differential access to mates. The modern version of that argument starts one step earlier, with anisogamy — the asymmetry between many small gametes and few large ones — which tends to make reproduction limited by mates for one sex and by resources or time for the other. Everything downstream of that premise is where the interesting empirical work sits, and where the field's internal arguments are sharpest. The general framework it sits inside is covered in the [account of how evolution works](/en/biology/evolution/evolution-explained).

## Two processes, one label

The term covers two mechanisms that need not act together and are measured differently. **Intrasexual selection** is competition among members of one sex for access to the other: it produces weapons, size asymmetry, territoriality and interference tactics, and it is comparatively easy to observe, because the contest itself is visible. **Intersexual selection**, or mate choice, is the preference exercised by the choosing sex, and it produces ornament, song, display and courtship structures. Its difficulty is that a preference has to be inferred from a pattern of matings that many other things could also produce.

Both mechanisms extend past copulation. Where females mate with several males, competition continues among ejaculates and within the female reproductive tract — sperm competition and cryptic female choice, together termed postcopulatory sexual selection. The best-resolved system is *Drosophila melanogaster*, where seminal fluid proteins transferred with sperm [modulate paternity share](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7661438/) and mediate both the competition and the conflict between the sexes over mating rate and remating. Because these effects are invisible from behavioural observation alone, any account of mating success built on who was seen mating with whom is measuring an incomplete quantity.

## What the standard metrics actually capture

Three quantities do most of the empirical work. The opportunity for selection, *I*, is the variance in reproductive success scaled by its mean. The opportunity for sexual selection, *I*ₛ, is the equivalent for mating success. The Bateman gradient, βₛₛ, is the slope of reproductive success on mating success — how much an extra mate is worth. The first two set upper bounds on how strong selection could be; only the gradient estimates its direction and average strength.

A phylogenetically controlled meta-analysis assembled 72 studies covering 66 animal species and yielding 81, 88 and 76 paired estimates of the three metrics. It found that [sexual selection is stronger in males across the animal kingdom](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4758741/) and that the sex difference is evolutionarily tied to sex biases in parental care and in sexual dimorphism, with the strongest male bias in species where females provide the care. That is the most direct comparative support the Darwin–Bateman framework has.

The same paper is careful about what weakens it. Most extracted estimates of mating success came from molecular parentage — the number of genetic partners, 64 of the estimates — rather than observed copulation partners, 13 estimates, or copulations, 19. Variance-based measures also confound selection with chance: a difference in *I*ₛ can be produced by sampling alone. And a rank-correlation test indicated moderate publication bias in the sex difference in *I*ₛ. The direction of the result is well supported; its magnitude carries more uncertainty than a single headline effect size suggests.

## The founding study did not survive re-examination

The framework's canonical empirical anchor is a 1948 *Drosophila* experiment that reported greater variance in mates and in reproductive success for males than females. Two later analyses examined it directly. A 2007 reappraisal identified [flaws including the elimination of genetic variance, sampling biases, miscalculated fitness variances, statistical pseudo-replication and selective presentation of data](https://academic.oup.com/evolut/article/61/11/2457/6853903), concluding that the reported variances were similar to those expected under random mating. A 2012 repetition using the original method of assigning parentage from offspring phenotypes located the mechanism: because double-mutant offspring were inviable, the method [overestimated the number of individuals with zero mates and underestimated those with one or more](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3406809/), producing systematically biased estimates of offspring number by sex — the very fitness variances the inference rested on.

This does not overturn the comparative result above, which does not depend on that experiment. It does mean the textbook demonstration cannot carry the weight it was given for six decades, and the episode is a reminder that a foundational finding can go unrepeated precisely because everyone treats it as established.

## Three explanations for the same ornament

Why a preference exists at all has three main candidate answers, and separating them empirically is the field's central difficulty.

Under **good genes** models, the ornament is an honest signal of heritable quality, so choosy females gain offspring of higher overall fitness. Under **Fisherian** models — formalised as the Lande–Kirkpatrick mechanism — the ornament need indicate nothing at all: a genetic correlation builds between preference and trait, and the trait is elaborated because attractive fathers have attractive sons. Under **sensory bias**, the preference predates the ornament, arising from perceptual tuning shaped in some other context, and the display evolves to exploit it.

A meta-analysis of 90 studies on 55 species tested the two genetic-benefit predictions directly. It found evidence that male attractiveness is heritable, but [no association between attractiveness and life-history traits](https://academic.oup.com/evolut/article/66/9/2665/6851558) tied to fitness; attractiveness did correlate positively with physiological traits such as condition and immunocompetence. The authors read this as supporting Fisherian models while leaving the evidence for good genes equivocal — a result that has not been overturned and is not what most popular accounts of mate choice assume.

There is also a structural argument about how the question has been asked. One influential critique holds that the Lande–Kirkpatrick mechanism is the appropriate null model for intersexual selection, arising from genetic variation in trait and preference in the absence of other forces much as Hardy–Weinberg proportions arise in the absence of evolutionary forces. If that is right, the prevailing standard is backwards: [demonstrating an arbitrary trait currently requires proving the null hypothesis](https://academic.oup.com/evolut/article/64/11/3085/6854107), which is not something evidence can do, while adaptive explanations are accepted on confirmation. The claim is contested, but the methodological point — that a field without an agreed null will tend to find what it looks for — is one the meta-analytic results are consistent with.

## Conflict, not only preference

A fourth process cuts across all of this. Where the reproductive optima of the two sexes differ, traits can be favoured in one sex that impose costs on the other, and the resulting antagonistic coevolution produces adaptations that no good-genes account would predict. The *Drosophila* seminal proteome is again the best-characterised case, since the same molecules that improve a male's paternity share also alter female receptivity and lifespan. Sexual conflict matters for interpretation as much as for mechanism: an elaborate trait is not automatically evidence that the other sex benefits from it, and mate-choice traits that diverge between populations are among the prezygotic barriers that contribute to [reproductive isolation between lineages](/en/biology/evolution/speciation-mechanisms).

## What these study designs cannot resolve

Three problems recur across this literature and are worth stating plainly. Fitness is measured over a fraction of a lifetime in almost every study, so the currency in which the models are stated is rarely the currency in which the data are collected. Most designs are correlational, and attractiveness, condition and territory quality covary in ways that a field study cannot easily unpick. And effect sizes in this area are small relative to the sampling variation of the experiments that estimate them, which makes the literature vulnerable to the selective-reporting problems examined in the note on [reproducibility in ecology and environmental science](/en/insight/reproducibility-in-ecology-and-environmental-science).

Two things follow. Strong-form statements — that a given ornament signals genetic quality, or that female preference maximises offspring fitness — are supported for particular species and not as generalities. And the mechanisms are not exclusive: a preference can begin as sensory bias, be elaborated by a Fisherian correlation, and acquire an honest-signalling component later, which is precisely why discriminating between them with a single experiment on a single species rarely settles anything. Designs that manipulate the trait or the preference, rather than measuring their covariation in the field, are better placed to separate the alternatives, and the same variance-partitioning logic governs [how much drift rather than selection shapes small populations](/en/biology/evolution/genetic-drift-and-population-size). That the same trait can be explained by adaptation, by correlation or by exploitation is not a failure of the theory; it is the reason the tests have to be designed against a stated null, in the same way that [any adaptive hypothesis is examined](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation).
