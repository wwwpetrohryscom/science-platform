Two populations are separate species when gene exchange between them has become improbable enough that each follows its own evolutionary trajectory. Stated that way, the question stops being "when did the split happen" and becomes "which barriers to gene flow are present, and how much do they actually stop". That is a question with measurable answers, and the answers have shifted considerably since whole-genome sequencing made it possible to look for gene flow rather than assume its absence. The wider machinery of allele-frequency change is set out in the [overview of evolutionary mechanisms](/en/biology/evolution/evolution-explained); this page is about the specific problem of how one lineage becomes two.

## Barriers, and where in the life cycle each one acts

Reproductive isolation is not a single property. It is a stack of barriers, conventionally split by whether they act before or after a zygote forms, and the stack matters because barriers multiply: two weak ones can leave more gene flow than one strong one.

| Barrier | Stage at which it acts | Usually measured by | Behaviour under renewed contact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ecological or habitat isolation | Before mating; individuals rarely meet | Habitat use, host association, spatial overlap of breeding | Collapses quickly if habitats merge |
| Temporal isolation | Before mating; breeding periods differ | Phenology of flowering, spawning or eclosion | Erodes when climate shifts one schedule onto the other |
| Behavioural isolation | Before mating; signals are not accepted | Mate-choice trials, song or pheromone response | Can be reinforced by selection against hybrids |
| Mechanical and gametic isolation | Between mating and fertilisation | Copulation success, fertilisation rate in vitro | Rarely reversed; often accumulates slowly |
| Intrinsic postzygotic isolation | After fertilisation; hybrids inviable or sterile | Hybrid survival and fertility in controlled crosses | Effectively irreversible once fixed |
| Extrinsic postzygotic isolation | After fertilisation; hybrids fit neither niche | Hybrid fitness in field enclosures or reciprocal transplants | Depends entirely on the environment persisting |

Intrinsic postzygotic isolation has a specific genetic explanation, the Dobzhansky–Muller model, and it is worth stating because it removes an apparent paradox. If hybrid sterility required a lineage to pass through a sterile intermediate, it could never evolve. It does not. An allele that arises in one lineage is tested only against that lineage's genetic background; an allele arising in the other is tested only against its own. Sterility appears when the two are combined for the first time in a hybrid, in a genetic pairing that natural selection has never had the opportunity to screen. Incompatibility is therefore a property of a combination, not of either lineage on its own.

## Geography sets the evidential burden, not the outcome

The familiar allopatric–parapatric–sympatric scheme is often read as three competing mechanisms. It is better read as three levels of evidential difficulty. When a physical barrier separates populations, divergence under [selection and drift](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation) needs no special explanation, and demonstrating it requires little beyond dating the barrier. Parapatric divergence, across a steep environmental gradient with continuous habitat, demands evidence that selection is strong enough to hold a cline against ongoing gene flow.

Sympatric divergence carries the heaviest burden, because the claim is that isolation arose while the populations remained in contact — which means the alternative, a period of separation followed by secondary contact, has to be excluded rather than assumed. Few cases survive that test. A study of Midas cichlids in Crater Lake Xiloá in Nicaragua reported [an early-stage hybrid lineage](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9537415/) that has diverged genomically and phenotypically from both parental species and carries a distinct stable-isotope signature, indicating a different trophic niche; the authors note that before their work only one case in animals, in insects, had been convincingly shown to have occurred in complete sympatry. Host-shifting insects supply the other well-worked example: hawthorn-feeding and apple-feeding *Rhagoletis pomonella* are separated by the fruiting phenology of their host plants and by the fruit volatiles the flies respond to, and comparable partially isolated host races have since been documented on several native southern hawthorn species.

## The one route that is genuinely instantaneous

Whole-genome duplication is the exception to everything above: a polyploid offspring can be reproductively isolated from its parents in a single generation, because odd chromosome pairing renders the backcross sterile. This is not a curiosity confined to a few crops. Drawing on the botanical community's cytogenetic and phylogenetic databases, one assessment established that [15 per cent of angiosperm and 31 per cent of fern speciation events](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2728988/) are accompanied by an increase in ploidy — roughly four times earlier estimates — with a standing incidence of polyploid species within genera of 35 per cent across 1,506 genera examined.

The same analysis found no direct evidence that polyploid lineages, once established, go on to diversify faster than their diploid relatives. Polyploidy is common because it generates new species readily, not because the species it generates are unusually successful afterwards. That distinction is easy to lose and it changes the interpretation entirely.

## How fast the stack can assemble

Reproductive isolation normally accumulates over hundreds of generations or more. A directly documented exception comes from Daphne Major in the Galápagos, where genome sequencing identified an immigrant male finch as *Geospiza conirostris* originating on Española, more than 100 kilometres away. It bred with a resident *G. fortis*, and from the second generation the resulting lineage [bred endogamously](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29170277/), showing transgressive bill morphology and remaining ecologically successful despite intense inbreeding. Isolation was established in three generations.

The value of that case lies in what was observed rather than inferred — the origin, the mating pattern and the genomes were all recorded. Its limits are equally plain: one lineage, one very small island, and no information about how often such lineages persist. Founding events of this kind are also exactly the situation in which [population size governs the fate of new variants](/en/biology/evolution/genetic-drift-and-population-size), so a documented origin is not evidence of a common route.

## What hybrid zones turned out to look like

The strongest revision of the textbook picture came from measuring hybridisation instead of assuming it away. In heliconiine butterflies, a survey of collections and field records found that [26 to 29 per cent of species](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1821009/) are involved in natural hybridisation, depending on the species concept applied — yet hybridisation is rare per individual. Where *Heliconius erato* and *H. himera* meet in parapatric contact, phenotypically detectable hybrids form around 10 per cent of the population; for species living in sympatry, hybrids are usually fewer than 0.05 per cent of individuals. Hybrid numbers decline roughly exponentially with genetic distance, and molecular dating in that group suggests gene exchange can continue for more than three million years after lineages separate.

Our own species supplies a comparable record. A catalogue of Neanderthal ancestry segments drawn from 334 genomes — 59 ancient individuals and 275 present-day people — reports that [most non-Africans carry 1 to 2 per cent Neanderthal ancestry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12184710/), with East Asians carrying about 20 per cent more of it than West Eurasians, and attributes the great majority of it to a single extended period of gene flow between 50,500 and 43,500 years ago.

## An F<sub>ST</sub> peak is not a barrier

One inference deserves separate treatment, because it was widely made and then substantially withdrawn. Regions of the genome showing unusually high differentiation between close relatives were interpreted as "islands of speciation" — loci resistant to gene flow while the rest of the genome was homogenised by it. Re-examining five species pairs for which full sequence data were available, a 2014 reanalysis in *Molecular Ecology* found that [absolute divergence is not elevated in those islands](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24845075/); what is unusual about them is reduced diversity, which inflates relative measures such as F<sub>ST</sub> without any reduction in gene flow. The same work found that simulation-based inferences of gene flow itself may be wrong in many published comparisons.

The general lesson travels beyond speciation genomics: a relative statistic behaves as a ratio, and a peak in it can be produced by a change in the denominator. Similar reasoning applies to reading independently derived similarity as shared ancestry, which is why [repeated evolution of the same trait](/en/biology/evolution/convergent-evolution-explained) is treated as a distinct inference problem.

## Living with a continuum

Taken together, these results describe a continuum rather than a threshold: geographic races, hybridising species and fully isolated lineages differ in degree, and the point on that continuum at which a name is applied is a decision, not a discovery. That is tolerable for evolutionary biology, where the underlying quantity — the rate of gene exchange — is what matters. It is more awkward for anything that counts species, since a taxonomic revision can change a total without any organism changing, a problem taken up in the treatment of [why species counts mislead conservation](/en/ecology/biodiversity/why-species-counts-mislead-conservation) and reflected in how [species richness](/en/glossary/species-richness) is defined. What the genomic evidence has not done is dissolve species into a continuum of equal shades. Hybridising *Heliconius* remain stably distinct where they coexist, which is the observation any account of the process has to accommodate.
