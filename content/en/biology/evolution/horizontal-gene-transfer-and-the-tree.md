The [tree of life](/en/biology/taxonomy/the-tree-of-life-and-domains) is a claim that ancestry is the dominant relationship between organisms — that [evolution](/en/biology/evolution/evolution-explained) proceeds by descent — that similarity reflects descent from a common ancestor. In prokaryotes, genes also move sideways, between lineages that are not in a parent–offspring relationship, and the question is whether that movement is a complication of the tree or a replacement for it.

## The rate

A 2016 review by Eugene Koonin gives figures that make the scale clear. Horizontal transfer is pervasive, with "the rate of gene gain and loss being comparable to the rate of point mutations and much greater than the duplication rate."

More specifically: "in all analyzed groups of microbes, the rate of gene gain exceeds the gene duplication rate by at least an order of magnitude."

Those two statements together describe a mode of evolution that differs qualitatively from the eukaryotic picture. In a lineage where genes arrive from elsewhere as fast as existing genes mutate, and ten times faster than they are duplicated internally, innovation is mostly acquisition rather than modification.

## Why the tree survives anyway

The natural conclusion is that a single tree cannot represent this, and for individual genes it cannot: different genes give different trees, and the disagreement is real rather than statistical noise.

What Koonin reports is that the disagreement is not uniform. Analysis of thousands of gene trees reveals "considerable orderliness among the topologies of several thousand trees in the microbial 'phylogenetic forest'", and "The consensus topology of the nearly universal trees explains nearly 40% of the variance in the tree topologies."

Forty per cent of the variance is not everything, and it is far more than a network with no vertical backbone would produce. Koonin's proposal is a "statistical tree of life" — a tree that represents "the central current of microbial genome evolution" rather than the history of every gene.

That framing is worth adopting because it states both halves. Vertical descent is a real, dominant central tendency. It is not the whole history, and no single tree is.

## What determines which genes move

Transfer is not uniform across the genome, and the pattern is informative.

Genes involved in translation, transcription and replication — the core machinery — transfer rarely, because they interact with many partners and a foreign copy must fit an existing system. Genes for metabolism, resistance and niche-specific functions transfer readily, because they are more self-contained.

This is why the core-gene tree looks more tree-like than the accessory-gene picture, and why phylogenies built on ribosomal genes recover a signal that whole-genome comparisons blur. It also means the choice of genes determines how tree-like the answer looks, which is a methodological fact worth stating whenever a phylogeny is presented.

## The consequences that reach outside microbiology

**Species concepts strain.** If lineages exchange genes routinely, the criterion that separates species in sexual eukaryotes has no straightforward analogue — one of the reasons [what a species is](/en/biology/taxonomy/what-is-a-species) is answered differently in different parts of the tree.

**[Antibiotic resistance](/en/biology/evolution/antibiotic-resistance-evolution-mechanisms) spreads without descent.** Resistance genes move between unrelated bacteria on plasmids and other mobile elements, which is why resistance can appear in a lineage never exposed to the drug — the mechanism behind much of [antimicrobial resistance](/en/biology/microbiology/antimicrobial-resistance-evidence).

**Dating gets harder.** Divergence dating assumes a topology and a rate; horizontal transfer disturbs both, and it does so unevenly across the genome. The confounding that already limits [molecular clocks](/en/biology/taxonomy/molecular-clocks-and-divergence-dating) is compounded where genes have arrived from elsewhere.
