Evolution is a property of populations, not of individuals. An organism does not evolve during its lifetime; what changes is how common each version of a gene is among the organisms that make up a breeding group, and how that distribution shifts from one generation to the next. Most popular misreadings — that lineages strive towards complexity, that a species somehow calls forth the variant it needs, that fitness means physical strength — dissolve as soon as the subject of the sentence is put back where it belongs.

The National Human Genome Research Institute defines evolution, in genomic terms, as the process by which living organisms change over time through changes in the genome. The population-genetic version is narrower and more useful: an **allele** is one of two or more versions of a DNA sequence at a given genomic location, and evolution is a change in how common each version is across generations. Everything below is an elaboration of that sentence.

## Why the null model matters

A population that is very large, mates at random, and experiences no selection, no mutation and no movement of individuals in or out would hold its allele frequencies constant indefinitely. No real population satisfies those conditions. The idealisation earns its place precisely because it fails: every observed departure from constancy has to be attributed to one of a short list of processes, and the work of evolutionary biology is largely the work of deciding which one, and how much.

## Four processes, not one

Textbook accounts often collapse evolution into natural selection alone. That is the single most consequential simplification in the subject, because three other processes change allele frequencies too, and in some circumstances they dominate.

| Process | What it does to allele frequencies | Is the direction predictable? | Dependence on population size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Natural selection | Shifts them in proportion to fitness differences among genotypes | Yes, given a specified environment | Weak selection is swamped in small populations |
| Genetic drift | Changes them by chance, through which gametes happen to become offspring | No — direction is random each generation | Strong dependence; the effect scales inversely with effective size |
| Mutation | Introduces alleles that did not exist before | Undirected with respect to what would be useful | Weak per generation, but the sole ultimate source of variation |
| Gene flow | Imports alleles from other populations | Depends on what the donor population carries | Homogenises populations that exchange migrants |

These are not competing hypotheses to choose between. All four operate at once in any real population, and the interesting questions are quantitative: how strong is selection on this trait, how small is this population, how much migration is there. [Natural selection and how adaptation is tested](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation) takes the first of those questions apart, and [genetic drift and effective population size](/en/biology/evolution/genetic-drift-and-population-size) takes the second, including the reason that the two cannot be assessed independently of each other.

## Where new variation comes from

Mutation is the only process on that list that creates something new; the other three redistribute what already exists. Its rate is measurable. Sequencing 78 Icelandic parent-offspring trios, with an average paternal age of 29.7 years, gave an average de novo mutation rate of 1.20 × 10⁻⁸ per nucleotide per generation. The same study found that the variation in that rate between families is dominated by the father's age at conception, adding roughly two mutations per year, with the paternal contribution doubling about every 16.5 years.

Two things follow. First, the supply of raw material is a quantity, not a metaphor: it can be counted, and it differs between species and between individuals. Second, mutation is undirected with respect to need. An environment that would reward a particular allele does not thereby make that allele more likely to arise. Selection can only sort what mutation has already supplied, which is why the timing of an adaptive change is often set by how long it takes for the right variant to appear rather than by how strongly it would be favoured.

## Evidence lines that could have disagreed and did not

Common descent is not supported by one argument repeated in different registers. It rests on several classes of observation that use different instruments, were assembled by different disciplines, and had ample opportunity to conflict.

**Fossils constrain sequence and timing.** Specimens of *Tiktaalik roseae* were recovered from the Fram Formation — Frasnian Stage, Late Devonian — on southern Ellesmere Island. A 2024 description of its axial skeleton found expanded, ventrally curved sacral ribs that most likely attached to the pelvis by ligament, showing that modifications to the vertebral column for head mobility, body support and pelvic-fin buttressing had evolved in finned vertebrates before limbs existed. The prediction that such an animal should occur in rocks of that age and depositional setting is what makes the find evidence rather than illustration.

**Genomes record the same tree independently.** The draft chimpanzee genome, compared with the human sequence, catalogued approximately 35 million single-nucleotide changes and about 5 million insertion or deletion events accumulated since the two lineages diverged. The analysis also found that patterns of change in protein-coding genes were dominated by the fixation of neutral and slightly deleterious alleles — a result that matters because it is not what a purely adaptive account of molecular divergence would predict.

**Evolution can be watched.** Twelve populations of *Escherichia coli* founded from a common ancestor in 1988 were grown in a medium containing citrate, which the bacterium cannot normally use as a carbon source in the presence of oxygen. No population acquired that ability for more than 30,000 generations. A citrate-using variant then arose in one population by generation 31,500, and replay experiments from frozen samples showed that a potentiating change had already occurred in that lineage by generation 20,000. The same kind of real-time observation, on a much shorter clock, underlies the well-characterised case of [resistance evolving in bacterial populations under antibiotic pressure](/en/biology/evolution/antibiotic-resistance-evolution-mechanisms).

Geographical distributions supply a fourth line. Island faunas, the split biotas of formerly joined landmasses, and the nested pattern of endemic species on archipelagos follow from descent with modification under barriers to dispersal, and they do not follow from independent creation of each form in place.

## The synthesis, and the argument about extending it

The modern synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s reconciled Mendelian inheritance with Darwinian selection and gave the field its quantitative core. The current disagreement is about whether that core needs restructuring.

Proponents of an extended evolutionary synthesis argue that developmental bias, developmental plasticity, inclusive inheritance and niche construction should be treated as causes of evolutionary direction rather than as details downstream of gene frequency change, and that causation runs in both directions between organism and environment. The authors of the 2015 statement of that position are explicit that the dispute concerns causal structure and emphasis rather than the empirical findings themselves. A companion argument holds that the standard framework has absorbed new results without altering its core predictions, and that absorbing findings is not the same as revising a theory.

This is worth stating plainly because the debate is routinely misrepresented. It is not an argument about whether populations evolve, whether species share ancestry, or whether selection is real. It is an argument among evolutionary biologists about which causes belong in the explanatory core — and it has a close relative in the question of what unit selection acts on, which the article on [cell types as a conserved unit of animal evolution](/en/biology/evolution/cell-types-as-units-of-evolution) examines from the comparative-genomics side.

## Where the theory predicts least well

Explaining the past is easier than forecasting the future, and the field is candid about the gap.

Contingency is the clearest case. The citrate result above required a specific prior mutation in a specific lineage; rerun the tape from an earlier point and there is no expectation that the same innovation appears. How far that finding generalises is unresolved, and it sits directly against the evidence that unrelated lineages often arrive at similar solutions — the tension examined in [convergent evolution and the predictability of outcomes](/en/biology/evolution/convergent-evolution-explained).

Species boundaries are a second soft spot. The processes that build reproductive isolation are reasonably well characterised, but genomic data have shown that many boundaries stay permeable to gene flow long after the lineages look distinct, which is the subject of [how reproductive isolation arises](/en/biology/evolution/speciation-mechanisms). A third is inference standards in specific subfields: the evidence that a trait is shaped by mate choice rather than by some correlated cause is harder to obtain than the literature sometimes implies, and the current critiques are covered in [sexual selection and its live disagreements](/en/biology/evolution/sexual-selection-explained).

None of these are gaps in the case for evolution. They are the places where quantitative evolutionary biology is still deciding how much of an observed pattern each process is responsible for, which is a different and more tractable problem.
