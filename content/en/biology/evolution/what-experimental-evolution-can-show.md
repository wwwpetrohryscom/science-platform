Most of what is known about [evolution](/en/biology/evolution/evolution-explained) is inferred backwards from its results. [Natural selection](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation) follows deductively from three conditions — variation, heritability of that variation, and its association with reproductive success. What is empirical, and testable case by case, is whether those conditions hold for a given trait in a given population. Experimental evolution is the design that tests them directly rather than reconstructing them.

## What the design gives you

Three things that historical inference cannot.

**A known starting point.** The ancestral population is preserved and can be revived, so the comparison is against the actual ancestor rather than an inferred one. Nothing in [comparative biology](/en/biology/evolution/cell-types-as-units-of-evolution) offers that.

**A known environment.** Selection pressure is imposed rather than reconstructed, so the question "what was this trait selected for" has an answer by construction.

**Replication.** Independent populations from the same ancestor under the same conditions show how repeatable evolution is, which is a question that cannot be asked of a history that happened once.

## Where it is strongest: resistance

[Antibiotic resistance](/en/biology/evolution/antibiotic-resistance-evolution-mechanisms) is the clearest case, and the reason it is clearest is a matter of scale. Bacterial populations are enormous, generations are short, and the selective agent can be applied precisely. Mechanisms proposed from sequence data — target modification, efflux, enzymatic inactivation, bypass pathways — can be produced in the laboratory and then found in clinical isolates.

The route that complicates the picture is the one that does not require descent at all. Resistance genes move between unrelated bacteria on mobile elements, at rates that make gene gain comparable to point mutation and, in Koonin's summary, exceeding "the gene duplication rate by at least an order of magnitude". An experiment following mutation in a single lineage will systematically understate how fast resistance appears in a community where [genes move sideways](/en/biology/evolution/horizontal-gene-transfer-and-the-tree).

## The limits, stated plainly

**Laboratory conditions are not the environment of adaptation.** A constant temperature, an unlimited nutrient supply and no competitors select for things that field populations are not selected for. Adaptations to laboratory conditions accumulate and are not always distinguishable from adaptations to the imposed treatment.

**Population size and structure differ.** Large well-mixed populations make selection efficient and drift weak, which is close to the opposite of many natural populations. Results transfer to organisms with similar demography and travel less well to those without.

**The timescale is short by construction.** Tens of thousands of generations is a great deal for a laboratory and very little for a phylum. Processes that operate over longer times — [speciation](/en/biology/evolution/speciation-mechanisms), major transitions — are not directly observable.

**Replicates share an ancestor and a chance.** Parallel outcomes across replicates show repeatability given that starting genome; they do not establish that the outcome would be repeated from a different one.

## What it settles and what it does not

Experimental evolution settles mechanism. It shows that a proposed route is available, how fast it is traversed, what it costs, and whether it recurs. That is a large contribution, and it is why the antibiotic case is among the best-supported in evolutionary biology.

It does not settle history. That a lineage could have evolved a trait a particular way, quickly, in a flask, is evidence about possibility rather than about what happened. Keeping those two apart is the discipline the design requires, and the reason its results are stronger than the arguments they are sometimes recruited into.
