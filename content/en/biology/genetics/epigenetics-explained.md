Two definitions of **epigenetics** are in circulation, and they do not license the same conclusions. The strict one, the one that gets used at the bench, covers states of gene activity that are propagated through cell division without any change in the DNA sequence. The loose one, common wherever the word reaches a general audience, covers roughly anything by which experience appears to leave a molecular trace. The [National Human Genome Research Institute](https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Epigenetics) defines the field as the study of changes in DNA that do not involve alterations to the underlying sequence — a definition about mechanism, not about how far an effect travels.

The gap between the two matters because they carry different evidential burdens. Demonstrating that a chemical mark exists is routine. Demonstrating that it is copied faithfully through mitosis is harder. Demonstrating that it survives the germline and reaches a descendant who was never exposed to the original stimulus is harder again, and in mammals it has rarely been done. The molecule underneath all of this is covered separately in [what DNA is and what it does not determine](/en/biology/genetics/what-is-dna).

## The chemistry, and how much of it actually varies

Three layers are usually meant by the word. DNA methylation attaches a methyl group to cytosine, overwhelmingly at CpG dinucleotides. Histone modifications — acetylation, methylation and others, on the tails of the proteins DNA is wound around — alter how tightly a region is packaged and which reader proteins bind it. Chromatin accessibility is the downstream result: whether [the transcriptional machinery](/en/glossary/transcription) can physically reach a stretch of sequence at all.

The methylation layer is the one most often described as a switch, and the description is misleading about scale. Whole-genome bisulphite sequencing across 30 human cell and tissue types found that most cell types carry [70 to 80 per cent of all CpG sites in the methylated state](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3821869/), and that only 21.8 per cent of autosomal CpG sites showed dynamic regulation in a normal developmental context. Most of that dynamic minority sat away from transcription start sites and coincided with enhancers and transcription-factor binding sites. The methylome, in other words, is mostly stable background; the informative part is a minority fraction concentrated at regulatory elements.

Mapping the histone and accessibility layers took a coordinated effort. The NIH Roadmap Epigenomics Consortium produced [111 reference human epigenomes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4530010/), analysed together with 16 previously reported by ENCODE for 127 in total, profiling histone modification patterns, DNA accessibility, methylation and RNA expression, and summarising the combinations into a 15-state chromatin model. One of its findings connects this field directly to statistical genetics: disease- and trait-associated variants were enriched in tissue-specific epigenomic marks, which is how a non-coding hit from a [genome-wide association study](/en/biology/genetics/genome-wide-association-studies-explained) gets assigned to a plausible cell type.

## Two cases where the strict definition clearly holds

Genomic imprinting is the cleanest. A small percentage of human genes are expressed from only one parental copy, and methylation is what marks which copy came from the egg and which from the sperm. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/understanding/inheritance/updimprinting/) describes two major imprinted clusters, on chromosome 11p15 and chromosome 15q11–15q13, and links their disruption to Prader–Willi syndrome, Angelman syndrome and Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome. These marks are laid down in the germline, survive fertilisation, and are maintained through the [rounds of cell division that build a tissue](/en/biology/cells/cell-division-mitosis-and-meiosis). That is inheritance of a state without a change to the sequence, in the full sense.

X-chromosome inactivation is the second, and it also shows that the textbook version is too tidy. A profile covering an estimated 95 per cent of assayable genes in fibroblast-based systems found that [about 15 per cent of X-linked genes escape inactivation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15772666/) to some degree, and that a further 10 per cent are inactivated variably — expressed from some inactive X chromosomes and not others. Silencing a whole chromosome is about as decisive an epigenetic event as mammalian biology offers, and a quarter of the genes on it turn out to be either exempt or inconsistently affected.

Both examples share a feature worth holding onto: their function is developmental. The reason a genome that is identical in every cell produces a hepatocyte in one place and a neuron in another is that [which genes a cell actually reads](/en/biology/genetics/how-gene-expression-is-regulated) is set and then remembered. That is the job these mechanisms demonstrably do.

## The germline erases almost everything

The obstacle facing any claim that an acquired state passes to the next generation is that mammalian germ cells are stripped. Single-cell analysis of human primordial germ cells found that at roughly 10 to 11 weeks after gestation they are [nearly devoid of DNA methylation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26046443/), with median levels of 7.8 per cent in male and 6.0 per cent in female cells — against the 70 to 80 per cent baseline of a somatic cell. A second wave of reprogramming follows fertilisation.

Erasure is not total. Reviewing the mechanisms, work in Cell identified roughly [4,730 loci that retain more than 40 per cent methylation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4020004/) through primordial germ cell reprogramming, predominantly repeat-associated and concentrated in IAP long-terminal-repeat elements. So there is a physical route. It is narrow, and it runs mostly through transposon-derived sequence rather than through genes.

## What a transgenerational claim has to clear

The generational bookkeeping is where most popular accounts go wrong. If a pregnant animal is exposed to something, three generations are exposed at once: the mother, the fetus, and the fetal germ cells that will become her grandchildren. An effect only counts as transgenerational once it appears in a generation that was never present during the exposure — the third for maternal exposure, the second for paternal, as set out in both the Cell review above and a [critical assessment in Nature Communications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6065375/). Anything shorter is an intergenerational effect: real, sometimes important, but a direct exposure rather than an inherited memory.

The best-known animal example fails this test in an instructive way. Agouti viable yellow mice are epigenetic mosaics for methylation of an inserted retrotransposon, with coats ranging from yellow through variegated to full agouti in genetically identical animals. The distribution of coat phenotypes among offspring tracks the phenotype of the dam, and paternal transmission has no effect — the signature of a maternal effect, not of a state carried through the germline. The same review's summary of the field is blunt: in plants and in some animals such as nematodes, transgenerational epigenetic inheritance is well documented and relatively common, while in mammals epialleles exist but are extremely rare, presumably because germline reprogramming is robust.

The best-known human observation is often reported as if it cleared the bar. Individuals prenatally exposed to the Dutch Hunger Winter of 1944–45 were found, six decades later, to have [less methylation of the imprinted IGF2 gene](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2579375/) than their unexposed same-sex siblings, with the association specific to periconceptional exposure. That is a persistent mark in the exposed individuals themselves. It is not a demonstration that anything was transmitted to descendants who were never exposed. The Nature Communications assessment argues that human studies of this kind are confounded by genetic, ecological and cultural inheritance running in parallel, and concludes that the evidence is not yet conclusive.

## Where the causal arrow has not been established

Three limitations recur across the human literature, and none of them is resolved.

The first is direction. Finding that a methylation state differs between people with and without a condition does not establish which came first. Transcription factor binding can drive local methylation change as readily as methylation can obstruct binding, and cross-sectional designs cannot separate the two.

The second is composition. Most human studies sample blood, because the tissue of interest is usually inaccessible. Blood is a mixture of cell types with different epigenomes, so a difference in the proportion of cell types between groups reads out as a difference in methylation without any cell having changed. Age and smoking status produce large, well-characterised signals that swamp weaker effects unless they are modelled explicitly.

The third is measurement. Whole-genome bisulphite sequencing is informative but wasteful — in the same 30-tissue analysis, most of the sequencing reads carried little or no relevant information about CpG methylation — which is why large studies generally use arrays covering a small, preselected fraction of sites instead. What an array does not interrogate cannot appear in the result, and the selection of sites was made on prior expectations about where regulation happens.

None of this makes the field weak. It makes the strong version of the popular claim — that experience rewrites inheritance — a claim that the mammalian evidence does not currently support, while the developmental version is among the better-established results in molecular biology. Settling the question would take designs that follow unexposed descendants, measure the germline rather than a surrogate tissue, and distinguish a mark that is copied from one that is merely present — the kind of unambiguous parent-to-child tracing that [changes to the sequence itself](/en/biology/genetics/mutation-types-and-rates) allow.
