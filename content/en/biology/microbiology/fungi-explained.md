There are four classes of antifungal medicine in clinical use, against dozens of classes of antibacterial. The reason is not neglect. It is that a fungal cell is built like ours: the ribosomes, the cytoskeleton, the replication machinery and most of the metabolic enzymes a drug might attack have close human counterparts, so a compound that stops the pathogen tends to stop the patient's cells too.

That constraint is a direct consequence of ancestry, and the ancestry was established in 1993 by protein sequence rather than by anatomy. An analysis of 25 proteins found four insertions and deletions shared uniquely by animals and fungi — including a 12-amino-acid insertion in translation elongation factor 1α and three small gaps in enolase — and four independently sequenced proteins each placed animals and fungi together, to the exclusion of plants and a wide range of protists. Fungi look like sessile plants and are related to us; it is one of the cleaner cases in which resemblance and descent point in opposite directions, a tension that runs through [taxonomy and classification](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained) generally.

## Digestion turned inside out

Almost everything distinctive about fungi follows from one decision about how to eat. An animal ingests material and digests it internally; a fungus secretes hydrolytic enzymes into its surroundings and absorbs the soluble products. Feeding therefore requires surface area in contact with the substrate rather than a gut, and the body plan that maximises that is a network of thin filaments — hyphae — extending at their tips and branching behind them.

Two consequences matter. First, a fungus can attack substrates that are chemically difficult and physically dispersed, because it does not have to move them: it grows into them. Second, the mycelium is a search structure. It explores, and where it finds a resource it thickens the connections leading to it, which is a form of resource allocation without a nervous system or a circulatory system.

This is also why the boundary between microbiology and macrobiology is blurred here. A mushroom is visible from across a field; the organism producing it is a diffuse network in soil or wood that no one has ever seen whole. The wider survey of [microbial life at planetary scale](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiology-explained) puts fungal biomass third among the kingdoms of life, behind plants and bacteria and ahead of archaea, protists and animals.

## Seven phyla, after a rearrangement

Genome-scale phylogenetics has reorganised the higher classification substantially. The group traditionally called the zygomycetes turned out not to be a natural one: a study using genome-scale data resolved it into two phyla, Mucoromycota — comprising Glomeromycotina, Mortierellomycotina and Mucoromycotina — and Zoopagomycota, comprising Entomophthoromycotina, Kickxellomycotina and Zoopagomycotina. Together with Cryptomycota, Chytridiomycota, Blastocladiomycota, Basidiomycota and Ascomycota, that analysis recognised seven phyla in the kingdom.

The split is ecologically meaningful rather than merely nomenclatural: Zoopagomycota members are predominantly pathogens and parasites of animals, while Mucoromycota members associate mainly with plants. The chytrids also retain a flagellated swimming stage that the rest of the kingdom lost, which is one reason the early-diverging fungi were mistaken for protists for so long.

## The partnership habit

Most land plants do not feed themselves alone. A synthesis of mycorrhizal associations across global plant diversity puts 72 per cent of vascular plant species in arbuscular mycorrhizal partnerships, 2.0 per cent in ectomycorrhizal ones, 1.5 per cent ericoid and 10 per cent orchid mycorrhizal. Just 8 per cent are completely non-mycorrhizal, and a further 7 per cent are inconsistently so. Most of the non-mycorrhizal minority are specialists of one kind or another — carnivores, parasites, hydrophytes, epiphytes — which is to say that the exception is the exception for identifiable reasons.

Those percentages count species, not biomass or carbon flux, and the distinction matters: the 2 per cent of species that are ectomycorrhizal include the dominant trees of boreal and many temperate forests, so their share of the actual carbon moving between plants and fungi is far larger than the species count implies. The soil side of this exchange is developed in the article on [the soil food web](/en/ecology/soils/soil-biology-and-the-soil-food-web), and the stand-level consequences in [forest ecosystems](/en/ecology/forests/forest-ecosystems-explained).

Lichens are the other classic fungal partnership, and the textbook version of them was revised recently. The long-standing model — one fungus, one photosynthetic partner — did not explain why two lichens with the same recognised fungal and algal species could differ consistently in form and chemistry. A 2016 study found specific basidiomycete yeasts embedded in the cortex of macrolichens across six continents, a third partner that had been present in the specimens all along and had gone unnoticed because nobody had looked for it in that tissue layer. The general lesson is one that recurs across the study of [host-associated microbial communities](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiomes-and-host-microbe-interactions): a symbiosis characterised by the partners you thought to assay is characterised only provisionally.

## Lignin, and a coincidence in the record

Wood resists decay largely because of lignin, an irregular aromatic polymer that cannot be broken down by simple hydrolysis. The only organisms that degrade it substantially are white rot fungi within the Agaricomycetes.

A comparative analysis of 31 fungal genomes reconstructed the ancestor of the Agaricomycetes as a white rot species, with lignin-degrading peroxidases expanding in the lineage leading to it and then contracting independently in the lineages that became brown rot and ectomycorrhizal fungi. Molecular clock analysis in the same study suggested that the origin of lignin degradation might have coincided with the sharp decline in the rate of organic carbon burial around the end of the Carboniferous.

That coincidence is frequently retold as though the evolution of white rot ended coal formation, and the original wording — "might have coincided" — did not claim that. The evidence is mixed on the causal reading, and the case against it is specific. Carboniferous coal-forming peats were dominated by arborescent lycopsids whose biomass came largely from periderm that was not lignified; coal accumulated at comparable levels across major floral transitions in the North American Carboniferous record; and fossil woods from the Devonian onward show damage consistent with white rot decay, well before the proposed enzymatic origin. The alternative account attributes the Carboniferous peak to a combination of everwet tropical climate and the extensive subsiding foreland basins formed during the assembly of Pangaea.

What survives the dispute is the narrower biological result: substantial lignin decay is a derived capacity with a traceable enzymatic history, gained in one lineage and lost repeatedly in its descendants. The flux is large either way, and the recycling of plant polymers by fungi is a major term in the terrestrial carbon budget, alongside the prokaryotic transformations covered in [microbial biogeochemistry](/en/biology/microbiology/microbial-biogeochemistry).

## When the fungus is the pathogen

Fungal pathogens are unevenly distributed across hosts, and the amphibian case is the most severe documented example of disease-driven biodiversity loss. A global assessment attributed the decline of at least 501 amphibian species over roughly half a century to chytridiomycosis, caused by *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, including 90 presumed extinctions. Declines peaked in the 1980s; as of that assessment, only 12 per cent of declined species showed signs of recovery, while 39 per cent were still declining.

In human medicine the burden is harder to quantify and the response is constrained by chemistry. The World Health Organization's first fungal priority pathogens list, published in 2022, ranks 19 fungi across critical, high and medium priority tiers, and notes that only four classes of antifungal medicine are currently available with few candidates in the clinical pipeline. WHO also states plainly that exact burden figures are unknown because quality data are scarce — a limitation of surveillance capacity rather than of interest.

This is the pipeline problem stated at the top of this article, seen from the clinical end. An antibacterial can target peptidoglycan synthesis or the bacterial ribosome, structures the human host simply does not have. Against a eukaryotic pathogen the exploitable differences are few: ergosterol rather than cholesterol in the membrane, and a cell wall of chitin and glucans. The available drug classes cluster on those two features, which is why losing one of them to resistance costs proportionally more than losing an antibacterial class. The parallel with bacterial resistance, and the surveillance problems both share, is taken up in the page on [antimicrobial resistance evidence](/en/biology/microbiology/antimicrobial-resistance-evidence).

## How much of the kingdom is described

Around 120,000 fungal species have been formally described. The most careful published estimate of the true total puts it at 2.2 to 3.8 million, derived by combining the ratio of cryptic species uncovered when known morphological species are examined molecularly — a weighted median of roughly ten to eleven species per previously recognised one — with an updated fungus-to-vascular-plant ratio of about 9.8 to 1 applied to some 380,000 vascular plant species. Description has run at roughly 1,300 species a year over the past four decades, rising to about 1,800 a year after 2010.

At that rate the gap does not close. The same assessment cautions against the obvious shortcut: clustering environmental DNA sequences can overestimate species counts by orders of magnitude, so the sequencing that reveals the scale of the shortfall cannot by itself measure it. The honest summary is that mycology knows the order of magnitude of its own ignorance and not much more precisely than that.
