The biology of antimicrobial resistance is comparatively settled. A bacterium survives a drug by destroying it, altering what the drug binds to, pumping it out, or keeping it from entering — and any of those capabilities can arrive on a plasmid from an unrelated species. What is genuinely contested is the arithmetic: how many infections are resistant, how many deaths follow from that, and how much of either number is measurement rather than model.

This page takes the mechanisms as the starting point rather than the destination, and spends most of its length on the evidence chain. The evolutionary dynamics behind the same phenomenon are treated separately in the existing article on [how resistance evolves in bacterial populations](/en/biology/evolution/antibiotic-resistance-evolution-mechanisms); the organisms themselves are introduced in the [overview of the microbial world](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiology-explained).

## Four escape routes and a delivery system

Resistance mechanisms fall into a small number of categories, and the clinically important thing about them is not the chemistry but the genetics.

| Mechanism | How it works | Typical genetic location |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enzymatic inactivation | β-lactamases and carbapenemases cleave the drug; aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes chemically alter it | Frequently plasmid-borne, often within integrons and transposons |
| Target alteration | The binding site is modified or replaced, as in altered penicillin-binding proteins or ribosomal methylation | Chromosomal mutation or an acquired gene cassette |
| Efflux | Membrane pumps export several drug classes at once | Chromosomal, but expression is regulated and can be selected upward |
| Reduced permeability | Loss or modification of porins slows entry | Chromosomal, usually loss-of-function |

The distinction between the last two columns is the one that governs how fast a hospital's problem changes. A chromosomal mutation is inherited vertically and spreads at the pace of the clone that carries it. A plasmid-borne gene can cross into a different genus in a single conjugation event, which is why [a term such as antibiotic resistance](/en/glossary/antibiotic-resistance) describes a property of a gene pool rather than of a species. It is also why acquired resistance to a last-line agent — colistin being the textbook case — is treated as an emergency at the point of detection rather than at the point where it becomes common.

Selection is not confined to hospitals. WHO reports that in 2022 the "Access" group of antibiotics, the narrow-spectrum agents with lower resistance potential, made up only about 53 per cent of global human antibiotic use, while "Watch" antibiotics made up roughly 45 per cent and exceeded 70 per cent of total use in nearly one-third of countries. On the agricultural side, FAO records 27 antimicrobial classes in use in animals, and notes that 118 countries reported quantitative animal-use data for 2015–2017 against 89 in 2015 — an improvement in reporting that itself indicates how partial the earlier picture was.

## What surveillance actually counts

The global instrument is WHO's Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System. Its 2025 report drew on data from 104 countries for 2023, and from 110 countries across 2016–2023, covering more than 23 million bacteriologically confirmed infections and 93 infection-type–pathogen–antibiotic combinations, with trends assessed for 16 of those combinations from 2018 to 2023.

Three properties of that denominator matter. It counts *laboratory-confirmed* infections, so it measures the resistance among specimens that reached a microbiology laboratory — which under-represents settings where cultures are rarely taken, and over-represents severe and hospitalised cases. It depends on national laboratory capacity, so coverage is thinnest precisely where the burden is thought to be highest. And it standardises on a fixed list of pathogen–drug pairs, so an emerging combination outside the list is invisible until the list changes. WHO's 2024 bacterial priority pathogens list, covering 24 pathogens across 15 families in three priority tiers, is the parallel exercise that decides what the system is looking for.

Within those limits the direction of travel is consistent. WHO reports that approximately one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide were resistant in 2023, that in the South-East Asia and Eastern Mediterranean regions the figure was closer to one in three, and that resistance rose in more than 40 per cent of monitored pathogen–antibiotic combinations between 2018 and 2023, at an average annual increase of 5 to 15 per cent. National systems add resolution rather than contradiction: the United States programme puts the domestic toll at more than 2.8 million resistant infections and more than 35,000 deaths a year, rising above 3 million infections and 48,000 deaths when *Clostridioides difficile* is included.

## Two numbers that are not the same number

The global death estimates come from the Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance project, and they are routinely quoted without the qualifier that determines their meaning. For 2019 the analysis reported 4.95 million deaths (95 per cent uncertainty interval 3.62–6.57) *associated with* bacterial resistance and 1.27 million (0.911–1.71) *attributable to* it. Those answer different counterfactual questions. The associated figure compares a resistant infection against no infection at all; the attributable figure compares it against the same infection caused by a susceptible organism. Only the second isolates the contribution of resistance itself, and it is roughly a quarter of the size.

The 2024 update, extending the series to 1990–2021 and forecasting to 2050, gave 4.71 million associated and 1.14 million attributable deaths for 2021, and reported divergent age trends: deaths fell by more than half among children under five between 1990 and 2021 while rising by over 80 per cent among adults aged 70 and over. Meticillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* attributable deaths rose from 57,200 in 1990 to 130,000 in 2021. The reference forecast for 2050 is 1.91 million attributable and 8.22 million associated deaths, with cumulative attributable deaths of 39.1 million (33.0–46.0) between 2025 and 2050.

These are modelled quantities, and the model is explicit about where it is weak. Seven of 204 countries and territories had no data for any component. Linking laboratory results to patient outcomes is scarce almost everywhere, so relative-risk estimates lean on regional patterns and covariates where country data are thin. Passive surveillance carries selection bias, and low diagnostic-culture rates bias which infections enter the dataset at all. The uncertainty intervals reflect that structure; they are not measurement error around a counted total, and a point estimate quoted without its interval misrepresents what the exercise produced.

## What stewardship has measurably changed

The intervention evidence is stronger than the burden evidence, because it comes from trials rather than models. A Cochrane review of interventions to improve antibiotic prescribing for hospital inpatients synthesised 221 studies, 58 of them randomised. Across 29 randomised trials and 23,394 participants, compliance with prescribing policy rose by an absolute 15 percentage points (95 per cent CI 14 to 16), from 43 to 58 per cent of patients, rated high-certainty evidence. Treatment duration fell by 1.95 days (95 per cent CI 1.67 to 2.22) from a baseline of 11.0 days, also high certainty. Length of stay probably fell by 1.12 days (0.7 to 1.54), moderate certainty. Mortality was unchanged at 11 per cent in both arms.

That last result is the one worth dwelling on, because the standing objection to shorter courses is that patients will be harmed. In this synthesis they were not, within the precision available. What the review cannot show is an effect on resistance prevalence itself, which no trial of this size and duration is powered to detect — the gap between changing prescribing and changing a population's resistance profile is the open question in the field.

Closing it will take better inputs rather than better models. Resistance is measured on organisms that were cultured, so surveillance inherits every bias of [culture-based and sequence-based methods](/en/biology/microbiology/culturing-and-sequencing-microbes); and the same antibiotic exposure that selects for resistance also removes the resident community that would otherwise exclude an invading pathogen, a mechanism examined in [what microbiome surveys can establish](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiomes-and-host-microbe-interactions). Both effects are real, and neither is captured by counting resistant isolates.
