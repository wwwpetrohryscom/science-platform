A census of the biosphere published in 2018 put the total living carbon on Earth at roughly 550 gigatonnes, of which plants account for about 450. Bacteria come second at approximately 70 Gt C, followed by fungi at 12, archaea at 7, protists at 4, animals at 2 and viruses at 0.2. Those figures, assembled by researchers at the Weizmann Institute and Caltech, carry very different uncertainties — plants are pinned to within a factor of 1.2, bacteria only to within a factor of 10, and archaea a factor of 13 — but the ordering is the point. Almost everything alive that is not a plant is microbial, and most of it is somewhere no one has looked directly.

## Where the cells actually are

The same census places the bulk of bacterial and archaeal biomass in deep subsurface environments — sediments and rock far below the seafloor and the land surface — rather than in soil, water or hosts. That conclusion inherits a much older result: an assessment published in 1998 estimated 4–6 × 10³⁰ prokaryotic cells on Earth holding 350–550 petagrams of carbon, with the oceanic subsurface alone accounting for around 355 × 10²⁸ cells against 26 × 10²⁸ in soil and 12 × 10²⁸ in all aquatic habitats.

Two decades of drilling and sequencing have revised the totals downward while leaving the geography intact. The revision matters for how the numbers should be read: subsurface cells turn over on timescales of months to thousands of years, so a large standing stock there does not translate into a large flux. Biomass and activity are separate quantities, and conflating them is the most common error in popular accounts of microbial abundance.

Near the surface, the arithmetic is different and better constrained. Global surveys of the two dominant marine picocyanobacteria give annual mean abundances of 2.9 ± 0.1 × 10²⁷ cells of *Prochlorococcus* and 7.0 ± 0.3 × 10²⁶ of *Synechococcus*, together contributing on the order of a quarter of ocean net primary production. Two genera of single-celled organisms, in other words, run a substantial share of the marine carbon fixation that feeds [marine food webs](/en/ecology/oceans/marine-food-webs-and-productivity).

## Metabolism, not morphology, is the axis of variation

A microbiologist looking down a microscope sees rods, spheres, spirals and filaments — a vocabulary of shapes so limited that it cannot carry the diversity it is asked to describe. The variation that matters is chemical. A 2008 review in *Science* framed the point in terms of redox chemistry: virtually all non-equilibrium electron transfers on Earth are driven by a small, ancient and highly conserved set of protein complexes, evolved in microbes early in the planet's history and moved between lineages largely by horizontal gene transfer.

That is what distinguishes microbial metabolism from animal and plant metabolism. Animals oxidise organic carbon with oxygen; plants add oxygenic photosynthesis. Prokaryotes do both and also respire nitrate, sulfate, ferric iron, manganese and carbon dioxide; oxidise ammonia, nitrite, hydrogen, sulfide, methane and reduced metals; fix atmospheric nitrogen, which nothing else does; and generate methane, which nothing else does either. The consequences for the elemental cycles are the subject of a separate treatment of [how microbes close the elemental cycles](/en/biology/microbiology/microbial-biogeochemistry), and they are the reason the [nitrogen cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/nitrogen-cycle-explained) has the shape it does.

## What the culture plate could not grow

For most of the twentieth century, microbiology was limited to what would form a colony. The mismatch between cells counted under a microscope and colonies counted on a plate — often two or more orders of magnitude — was recognised early and has been called the [great plate count anomaly](/en/glossary/great-plate-count-anomaly) ever since.

Sequencing did not so much solve the problem as measure it. An analysis published in 2018 combined ribosomal RNA surveys with cell counts across environments and estimated that uncultured genera could account for 7.3 × 10²⁹ cells, about 81 per cent of the microbial cells on Earth, and that uncultured phyla — lineages with no cultured relative at all — account for roughly 2.2 × 10²⁹ cells, about a quarter of the total. Across individual environments the uncultured share ranged from 22 to 87 per cent at the genus-to-class level. The striking exception was human and human-associated habitats, where cultured genera dominate at 45 to 97 per cent, which says more about where a century of clinical culturing effort was spent than about the organisms.

Metatranscriptome data from the same analysis showed uncultured phyla contributing 46 to 84 per cent of sequences, so these are not dormant curiosities: they are viable cells whose physiology is known only from genomes. What that inference can and cannot support is taken up in the companion page on [how microbes are cultured and sequenced](/en/biology/microbiology/culturing-and-sequencing-microbes).

## Three domains and the groups that sit outside them

The recognition in 1990 that cellular life divides into Bacteria, Archaea and Eucarya came from molecular sequence comparison rather than from any observable property of the cells, and it placed two of the three domains entirely within microbiology. The differences between [bacteria and archaea](/en/biology/microbiology/bacteria-and-archaea-explained) run through membrane chemistry, transcription machinery and cell-wall composition, none of which is visible at the microscope's resolution.

Two other groups are conventionally taught as microbiology without fitting the three-domain scheme neatly. [The fungal kingdom](/en/biology/microbiology/fungi-explained) is eukaryotic, and phylogenetically closer to animals than to plants, but its absorptive, filamentous way of feeding puts most of it in the microbial toolkit. [Viral replication strategies](/en/biology/microbiology/viruses-explained) belong to entities that are not cells at all, and that hold their own place in the census at about 0.2 Gt C — a small mass attached to an enormous influence on mortality and gene flow. How these groupings relate to the wider classification of life is set out in the treatment of [the tree of life and its domains](/en/biology/taxonomy/the-tree-of-life-and-domains).

## The organisms in us, and a correction

The claim that bacterial cells outnumber human cells ten to one circulated for decades. A 2016 re-estimate replaced it: a 70 kg reference adult carries about 3.8 × 10¹³ bacteria against about 3.0 × 10¹³ human cells, a ratio near 1.3 to 1, with 25 per cent uncertainty on the bacterial figure. The older ratio survives partly because it is roughly right if red blood cells are excluded from the human count — an exclusion the re-estimate treats as a question of definition, though its authors count red blood cells as cells.

The correction is worth making precisely because it changes nothing important. The [microbiome](/en/glossary/microbiome) matters because of what its members do metabolically and immunologically, not because of a headcount, and the difficulty of moving from an observed association to a demonstrated cause is the central problem of the field — examined in the page on [what microbiome surveys can establish](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiomes-and-host-microbe-interactions). The same logic governs the clinical end of the discipline, where resistance genes move between organisms and settings faster than surveillance systems can follow, as set out in the page on [antimicrobial resistance evidence](/en/biology/microbiology/antimicrobial-resistance-evidence).

## How many kinds are there? A six-order-of-magnitude disagreement

The most honest thing microbiology can say about its own scope is that it does not know how many microbial species exist, and the published estimates are not close.

One approach extrapolates diversity scaling laws from a compilation of about 35,000 sites and predicts that Earth holds upward of one trillion (10¹²) microbial species. A second approach, published three years later, used a census-based method on the same class of sequence data and estimated 0.8–1.6 million prokaryotic operational taxonomic units at the conventional 97 per cent similarity threshold in the 16S V4 region, or 2.2–4.3 million using full-length 16S sequences. The second team concluded that global richness is about six orders of magnitude lower than the scaling-law prediction, and that phenomenological scaling relationships cannot be extrapolated to global scales when they have been fitted only to individual communities.

Neither number is a measurement. Both are extrapolations from sampling that covers a vanishing fraction of the habitats involved, and they differ because they make different assumptions about how rarity behaves at scales no survey reaches. This is the shape of most quantitative claims in microbiology: an abundance figure with a stated fold-uncertainty is trustworthy in its order of magnitude and not in its digits, and a diversity figure should be read as an assumption made explicit rather than a count. The reference taxonomy used to organise sequence records is a reminder of the same limit — the NCBI Taxonomy database describes itself as covering about 10 per cent of the described species of life, and described species are themselves a small and biased sample of the living ones.
