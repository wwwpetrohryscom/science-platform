Nitrogen is 78 per cent of the atmosphere and unavailable to almost everything alive except the [microorganisms](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiology-explained) that can reduce it, because the triple bond holding N₂ together is too strong to break with ordinary metabolism. A small group of bacteria and archaea can break it. What they pay to do so is worth stating precisely, because the price explains most of the biology built around the reaction.

## The stoichiometry

A 2022 perspective in *FEBS Letters* on the molybdenum nitrogenase system writes the reaction out: "N2+8H++8e−+16MgATP→2NH3+H2+16MgADP+16Pi".

Three features of that equation deserve attention.

**Sixteen ATP per nitrogen molecule.** At a working free energy of hydrolysis around 50 kJ per mole, that is roughly 800 kJ spent per mole of N₂ reduced — an enormous outlay by cellular standards, and set against the [ordinary ATP budget of a cell](/en/biology/cells/atp-and-cellular-energy-budgets) it is a major commitment of the organism's economy.

**Eight electrons for a six-electron reduction.** Converting N₂ to two NH₃ requires six electrons. The reaction consumes eight, and the extra two go into producing a molecule of hydrogen. That H₂ is not a side reaction that better catalysis would eliminate; it appears in the balanced equation and is thought to be mechanistically obligatory.

**Nothing about the temperature.** The reaction runs at ambient conditions, which the industrial route cannot manage. The authors make the comparison explicitly, noting the equation "highlights that both the biological and industrial processes are energy consumptive."

## The oxygen problem

The second constraint is structural rather than energetic. As the perspective records, "it has been well-documented that the nitrogenase proteins exhibit considerable oxygen sensitivity and thus must be manipulated within anaerobic environments."

An enzyme destroyed by oxygen, in organisms that mostly live in an oxygenated world, is a design problem that has been solved several times over in different ways: heterocysts in filamentous cyanobacteria, thick slime layers, temporal separation between fixing and photosynthesising, and — in the legume case — a root nodule with an oxygen-binding protein that keeps free oxygen extremely low while still delivering enough for respiration.

The nodule is often described as a structure for housing bacteria. It is more precisely a device for maintaining an oxygen concentration in a narrow window: high enough to respire, low enough not to destroy the catalyst.

## Why the symbiosis exists

Fixing nitrogen costs energy the bacterium has to get from somewhere, and in the legume symbiosis the plant supplies it as carbon compounds. That is a straightforward trade, and it explains the distribution of the strategy: fixation is worth it where nitrogen is scarce and carbon is not, which is to say where there is light and poor soil.

It also explains why the trade is unstable. A plant with abundant soil nitrogen gains nothing and pays anyway, which is why nodulation is suppressed when nitrogen is available. And a symbiont that takes carbon without fixing nitrogen would be favoured, which is why legumes sanction underperforming nodules rather than treating the partnership as automatic.

## What this means at the ecosystem scale

**Fixation is a bottleneck by design.** Because the reaction is expensive and its catalyst is fragile, biological nitrogen input to an ecosystem is limited by energy supply rather than by the availability of N₂. That is why nitrogen limitation is common in a world where nitrogen is the most abundant gas.

**Industrial fixation removed the bottleneck.** Haber–Bosch supplies reactive nitrogen at a rate that biological fixation cannot approach, which is the basis of modern agricultural yields and the reason [the industrial process consumes around 2 per cent of world final energy](/en/physics/energy/the-energy-cost-of-ammonia-synthesis).

**The consequence is downstream.** Nitrogen applied and not taken up moves. The EPA's account of nutrient pollution identifies "agriculture and stormwater" as key contributors, and describes the result: excess nitrogen and phosphorus cause "algae and algae-like bacteria to grow faster than ecosystems can handle, forming algal blooms." The cost of relieving the bottleneck is paid in the water rather than in the field.
