Until the late 1970s, a methanogen from a cow's rumen and *Escherichia coli* from the same animal's gut were filed under the same heading. Both were small, both lacked a nucleus, and under a light microscope both were rods. The reclassification that separated them did not come from any new observation of the cells; it came from sequencing a molecule that both of them use for the same job, and finding that the two versions were about as different from each other as either was from the version in a human ribosome.

Between them these two domains hold most of the living carbon that is not plant tissue, as set out in the wider survey of [the organisms that run planetary chemistry](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiology-explained). This page is about what the division between them turned out to consist of, and about a second, quieter result: that despite pervasive gene exchange between lineages, prokaryotic genomes fall into discrete clusters rather than a continuum.

## What the ribosome revealed

Small-subunit ribosomal RNA is present in every cell, performs the same function everywhere, and changes slowly enough that homologous positions can still be aligned across billions of years. That combination makes it a usable molecular chronometer, which is why the domain-level rearrangement of life came out of rRNA comparison rather than from physiology or morphology.

The formal proposal in 1990 introduced a rank above kingdom — the domain — and split cellular life into Bacteria, Archaea and Eucarya, arguing that molecular structures and sequences reveal evolutionary relationships more reliably than classical phenotypes, and that the differences between the three groups are more profound than those separating animals from plants. The same proposal divided Archaea into Euryarchaeota, containing the methanogens and their relatives, and Crenarchaeota, containing the thermophilic forms. It also noted a detail that has aged well: archaeal RNA polymerases resemble the eukaryotic enzyme in structure, not the bacterial one.

That last point generalises. Archaea run bacterial-style metabolism with eukaryote-style information processing — transcription, translation initiation and DNA replication machinery closer to ours than to *E. coli*'s. The broader placement of the three domains is treated in the article on [the tree of life and its domains](/en/biology/taxonomy/the-tree-of-life-and-domains); what follows here is the cell-level detail.

## The lipid divide

The most chemically absolute difference between the domains is in the membrane. Archaeal phospholipids are built on *sn*-glycerol-1-phosphate with isoprenoid chains attached by ether bonds; bacterial and eukaryotic phospholipids are built on *sn*-glycerol-3-phosphate with fatty acids attached by ester bonds. The glycerol backbones are mirror-image stereoisomers, made by non-homologous enzymes, and the chains are chemically different classes of hydrocarbon joined by a different linkage.

Ether bonds are more resistant to hydrolysis than ester bonds, and many archaea take the arrangement further by fusing two lipids end to end into a tetraether that spans the membrane as a monolayer rather than a bilayer. The thermoacidophile *Picrophilus* has core lipids that are mainly bis-phytanyl tetraethers — a membrane structure that is hard to make leaky.

| Feature | Bacteria | Archaea | Eukarya |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Membrane glycerol backbone | *sn*-glycerol-3-phosphate | *sn*-glycerol-1-phosphate | *sn*-glycerol-3-phosphate |
| Chain linkage | ester | ether | ester |
| Hydrocarbon chains | fatty acyl | isoprenoid | fatty acyl |
| Membrane-spanning monolayers | absent | common | absent |
| Principal wall polymer | peptidoglycan | pseudomurein, S-layer or none | none (cellulose or chitin in some) |
| RNA polymerase | bacterial type | eukaryote-like | eukaryote type |

Read across a row and the pattern is not "archaea are intermediate". On membrane chemistry, archaea are the outlier and bacteria side with eukaryotes; on transcription, bacteria are the outlier. Any attempt to arrange the three domains on a single axis of similarity fails, which is the reason the domain concept was needed in the first place.

## Genomes organised for speed

A prokaryotic genome is typically a single circular chromosome with high coding density, few introns, and functionally related genes arranged in operons transcribed as one polycistronic message. That arrangement couples regulation to function directly: one promoter, one signal, a whole pathway switched together.

The size range is wide and informative at both ends. *Candidatus* Carsonella ruddii, an obligate symbiont of sap-feeding psyllids, has a genome of about 160 kb — among the smallest known for any cellular organism, and small enough that its status as a bacterium rather than an organelle is arguable. At the other extreme, a *Sorangium cellulosum* strain from an alkaline habitat carries 14,782,125 base pairs, described on publication as the largest prokaryotic genome then reported. That is a roughly ninety-fold span within one grade of cellular organisation, and it tracks lifestyle: reduction toward the symbiotic end, expansion toward the free-living, chemically versatile end. The way genes are read and regulated within that architecture connects to the general account of [how genetic information is stored](/en/biology/genetics/what-is-dna).

## Gene transfer, and why species survive it anyway

Prokaryotes acquire DNA laterally — by transformation from the environment, by conjugative plasmid transfer, and by transduction inside [the capsids of bacteriophages](/en/biology/microbiology/viruses-explained). A network analysis of 539,723 genes across 181 sequenced genomes concluded that, on average, at least 81 ± 15 per cent of the genes in each genome had been involved in lateral transfer at some point in their history.

That figure is often taken to mean the bacterial species concept is incoherent. The genomic evidence points the other way. A pairwise comparison of about 90,000 prokaryotic genomes found that 99.8 per cent of the roughly 8 billion genome pairs analysed conformed to either greater than 95 per cent average nucleotide identity, within a species, or less than 83 per cent, between species — a discontinuity, not a continuum, and one robust to the removal of the most heavily sequenced organisms.

Both results are correct. Genes move, and the genomes they move into still cluster. The reconciliation is that transfer is not uniform: it is biased toward close relatives by mechanism and by recombination efficiency, and transferred genes are filtered by selection in the recipient. **Average nucleotide identity** — the mean identity across shared genomic regions — has become the operational species criterion for prokaryotes for this reason, and it is a threshold justified by an observed gap rather than by a definition imposed in advance. What it cannot do is tell you whether two genomes with 96 per cent identity are ecologically interchangeable, which is a separate question the metric was never designed to answer.

## Where the limits of growth have actually been measured

Extremophile records are often quoted as though they were properties of life. They are properties of organisms that someone managed to grow.

The highest demonstrated growth temperature is 122 °C, achieved by *Methanopyrus kandleri* strain 116 under 20–40 MPa of hydrostatic pressure; at atmospheric pressure the same strain stops at 116 °C. Raising the pressure raised the ceiling, which means "the upper temperature limit for life" is not a single number but a surface in temperature–pressure space, and the surface has only been probed where someone has had a pressure vessel and a candidate organism. At the acid end, *Picrophilus torridus* and *P. oshimae* grow around pH 0 with a pH optimum of 0.7 and a temperature optimum of 60 °C; genome analysis of *P. torridus* found an exceptionally high ratio of secondary to ATP-consuming primary transporters, indicating that the surrounding proton concentration is not merely tolerated but used to drive transport.

These are lower bounds on what is possible. Because the great plate count anomaly means most lineages have never been cultured at all — a problem examined in the companion page on [culture-dependent and sequencing methods](/en/biology/microbiology/culturing-and-sequencing-microbes) — the recorded limits track cultivation effort as much as biology. Genomic evidence from uncultured lineages regularly implies metabolisms and tolerances that no isolate demonstrates, and the honest statement is that the boundaries of the habitable range are known only where someone has tried and succeeded.

The practical stakes are not confined to the extremes. The same physiological diversity underpins the elemental transformations described in [microbial biogeochemistry](/en/biology/microbiology/microbial-biogeochemistry), and it is why a survey of a soil or a sediment can identify what organisms are present far more confidently than what they are doing. That gap between presence and rate is the recurring limitation of the whole field, and it is inherited by every application built on top of it, including the study of [what microbes are doing inside a host](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiomes-and-host-microbe-interactions).
