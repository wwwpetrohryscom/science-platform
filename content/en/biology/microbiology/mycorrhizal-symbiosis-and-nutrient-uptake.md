Much of what [microbiology](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiology-explained) contributes to a plant happens within a few millimetres of a root, and this is the clearest case. A root takes up phosphate from the soil solution immediately around it. Phosphate diffuses through soil slowly, because it binds to mineral surfaces, so the root removes it faster than it is resupplied. A 2019 review in *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology* states the consequence in one line: "a depletion zone of nutrients develops rapidly around the roots."

Everything about the mycorrhizal symbiosis follows from that sentence. The problem is not that phosphorus is absent from the soil; it is that the phosphorus in the soil is a few millimetres too far away.

## The fungal solution

Fungal hyphae are thin enough to explore soil at a resolution roots cannot, and they extend, in the review's description, "beyond the rhizosphere's depletion zone, mobilize and translocate nutrients, chiefly Pi, from regions inaccessible by roots."

The geometry is the whole advantage. A hypha is one or two orders of magnitude thinner than a root, so the same investment of biomass reaches vastly more soil volume, and it grows into soil the root would have to displace.

## What the plant pays and what it gets

The transaction is quantified, and both sides are large.

On the cost side, the review reports that "up to 22% of the photosynthetically fixed carbon is traded for symbiotic Pi." Between a tenth and a fifth of everything a plant makes can go to its fungal partner.

On the benefit side, "plants capable of this symbiosis can obtain up to all of the phosphate from symbiotic fungi." Specific cases are given: "In species such as tomato and flax, the mycorrhizal pathway contributed 100% of Pi acquired by the plant", while for rice "AMF contributes up to 70% of the P acquired by the plant".

A hundred per cent is the striking number. It means that in those species the direct root uptake pathway, which is present and functional, delivers essentially nothing when the symbiosis is established. The plant has not supplemented its uptake; it has outsourced it.

## Why this is not simply a good deal

Three complications keep the relationship from being a straightforward mutualism.

**The exchange rate varies.** Carbon costs and phosphate returns are both context-dependent, and there are conditions under which colonisation continues while the phosphorus benefit does not — a plant paying for a service it is no longer receiving.

**The partners are not matched pairs.** A single plant hosts multiple fungal genotypes, and a single fungal network connects multiple plants. Benefits and costs are distributed across a network rather than settled bilaterally.

**Fertiliser changes the calculation.** Where soil phosphate is abundant, the depletion zone does not form and the fungal route adds little. Plants suppress colonisation accordingly, which is why the symbiosis is most important in exactly the low-input systems where it is least measured.

## Why it matters beyond the root

**It is a control on the phosphorus cycle.** Phosphorus has no atmospheric route and moves slowly through soil, as [the phosphorus cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/phosphorus-cycle-explained) sets out. Mycorrhizal networks are a substantial part of how it moves the last few centimetres into a plant.

**It is a carbon flux in its own right.** Up to a fifth of terrestrial photosynthesis passing into fungal biomass is a large term, and some of that carbon ends up in soil organic matter rather than returning to the atmosphere.

**It complicates any nutrient-addition experiment.** Adding phosphorus to a plot changes the symbiosis as well as the soil chemistry, so the measured plant response is a combined effect of two changes rather than one.
