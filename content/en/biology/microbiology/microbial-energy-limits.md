Most descriptions of [microbial life](/en/biology/microbiology/microbiology-explained) are descriptions of cells that are growing, because growing cells are the ones that can be cultured. The subsurface biosphere breaks that assumption. Cells buried in marine sediment persist for periods that make laboratory generation times irrelevant, on energy supplies so small that the interesting question stops being how fast they grow and becomes whether they are doing anything at all.

## The power figures

A 2019 study in *Geobiology*, discussed in a subsequent review of dormancy in marine sediments, puts a number on it for South Pacific Gyre sediments: "maintenance power is equivalent to ~4 × 10−19 J s−1 cell−1" for the most metabolically active fraction of the community.

Four times ten to the minus nineteen watts is 0.4 attowatts. The same review records the lower bound proposed in the literature: a "minimum power limit for microbial life" suggested as "as low as 10−21 J s−1 cell−1" — one zeptowatt.

The comparison that makes these numbers legible is with a cell that is not energy-limited. A human cell produces "∼10^7–10^8 ATP/s", and at a working free energy of hydrolysis "around 50 kJ mol⁻¹" that is a power on the order of 10⁻¹² watts — seven orders of magnitude above the subsurface maintenance figure. Subsurface cells are not scaled-down versions of cultured ones; they are operating in a regime the culture-based measurements never covered.

## Timescales that follow

Power that low forces slow everything. The same review notes published "estimated generation times for the deep biosphere, up to several thousands of years", with biomass turnover estimates "spanning 1,000 to 73,000 years".

A turnover time of tens of thousands of years is not a slow version of laboratory growth. Over such intervals, damage accumulation, racemisation of amino acids, and spontaneous depurination of DNA become comparable in rate to the repair a cell can afford. Maintenance in the subsurface is therefore not idling — it is a race between decay and a repair budget of a fraction of an attowatt.

## Dormancy as a one-way door

The most striking claim in the review concerns the direction of transitions. In ancient sediment, it argues, microorganisms "transition only from an active state to a dormant/increasingly dormant state, and not vice versa", making dormancy in this setting "a 'dead-end strategy'" — because "conditions only worsen with increased burial."

That is a departure from the standard picture of dormancy as a reversible bet-hedging strategy, in which a cell waits out a bad patch and resumes when conditions improve. It is reversible in a soil that dries and rewets. In sediment that is being buried, there is no improving state to wait for, and the population structure that results — a diminishing active fraction, an accumulating dormant one — is a consequence of the environment rather than of a strategy.

## What this does to the culturing problem

The gap between cells counted under a microscope and colonies grown on plates has been recognised for a century, and it is usually framed as a media problem: the right nutrients had not been offered. Culturomics has vindicated that framing for many taxa, recovering organisms once described as unculturable.

Energy limitation adds a second, separate reason, and it is not fixed by better media. A cell running at 10⁻¹⁹ W is not merely fastidious about substrates; it is operating so far below culturable rates that the act of providing abundant substrate changes what is being studied. Both explanations are true, they apply to different organisms, and the [distinction between culturing and sequencing approaches](/en/biology/microbiology/culturing-and-sequencing-microbes) is where it becomes practical.

## Why the numbers matter beyond microbiology

Three consequences reach outside the field.

**Biogeochemical rates.** Subsurface communities mediate slow transformations of carbon and sulfur over geological time. Rates that are undetectable per cell integrate into fluxes that are not, because the cell numbers are enormous and the durations are long. This is the connection to [microbial biogeochemistry](/en/biology/microbiology/microbial-biogeochemistry).

**Habitability arguments.** The lower bound on power per cell is a quantitative criterion for whether an environment could support life at all, and it is one of the few criteria in astrobiology that can be stated in watts rather than in analogies.

**What "alive" means operationally.** A cell dividing once in ten thousand years satisfies no practical test for activity on the timescale of an experiment. The category is being applied to something the category was not built for, and the honest response is to report the measured power and the measured turnover rather than to argue about the word.
