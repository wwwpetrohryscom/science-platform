Most of the engineered crop area planted worldwide carries one of two traits: insecticidal proteins from the soil bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*, or tolerance to a broad-spectrum herbicide. Everything else — altered oil composition, virus resistance, delayed browning, provitamin A — is real but small by hectare. That matters for reading the evidence, because there is no such thing as a study of "the safety of genetic modification". There are studies of particular constructs in particular crops, and the two dominant traits carry almost all of the accumulated data. The techniques that produce those constructs are described in the wider [survey of what biotechnology's methods actually do](/en/biology/biotechnology/biotechnology-explained).

## The agronomic record, and how it was assembled

A pooled analysis of 147 original studies reported that adoption of the technology reduced chemical pesticide use by 37 per cent, increased crop yields by 22 per cent and increased farmer profits by 68 per cent, with yield advantages 14 percentage points higher in developing than in developed countries. Those are large effects and they have been quoted extensively.

They deserve the caveat the authors themselves attached. Several of the original studies did not report sample sizes or measures of variance, which constrained the weighting; the pooled figure is an average over trials that differ in crop, pest pressure, region, comparator variety and year. The National Academies' review reached a more granular version of the same picture, noting that herbicide-resistant crops sprayed with glyphosate "often had small increases in yield in comparison with non-HR counterparts" — a modest, trait-specific statement rather than a claim about the technology as a class. A headline percentage that travels without its heterogeneity is one of the more common ways a defensible result becomes an indefensible claim, a pattern examined generally in the note on [what gets lost between a dataset and a headline](/en/insight/uncertainty-lost-between-dataset-and-headline).

## The food-safety literature and the limit of what it establishes

On human health the evidence is unusually consistent. The National Academies committee, which heard 80 speakers and read more than 700 public comments in the course of its review, re-examined the primary animal-feeding studies and found "reasonable evidence that animals were not harmed by eating food derived from GE crops", and its analysis of epidemiological data found "no substantiated evidence that foods from GE crops were less safe than foods from non-GE crops". The World Health Organization states that "GM foods currently available on the international market have passed safety assessments and are not likely to present risks for human health", and that no health effects have been shown from consumption in countries where such foods have been approved.

The phrasing in both cases is deliberate and worth preserving. These are statements about the products that have been assessed, not a theorem about any future construct. That is exactly why assessment under the Codex framework is done case by case and per product, evaluating direct effects of the inserted sequence together with unintended effects. Someone who reports the conclusion as "engineered food is safe" has generalised past what either body wrote; someone who reads the case-by-case requirement as evidence of unresolved doubt has misread it in the other direction. The requirement exists because the relevant hazard depends on what was introduced, not on how it was introduced.

## Refuges, resistance, and why one country's failure was another's success

Deploying a single insecticidal protein across millions of hectares imposes exactly the selection pressure that [natural selection](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation) describes, and the outcome followed. A review of the first 25 years of cultivation analysed global monitoring data for 24 pest species across 73 cases, each case being one pest species in one country against one Bt toxin. Cases of practical resistance — field-evolved resistance that measurably reduces crop efficacy — rose from 3 in 2005 to 26 in 2020, affecting populations of 11 pest species across nine Cry toxins in seven countries.

The other two-thirds of the same table are the part usually left out. Thirty cases showed no decrease in susceptibility, across 16 pest species in 10 countries, and 17 more were early warnings: a genetically based drop in susceptibility with no loss of field efficacy yet. The same technology produced durable control and rapid failure in different places, which points the explanation at management rather than at the trait.

Pink bollworm in cotton makes the contrast concrete across the three largest cotton producers. In the southwestern United States, growers planted non-Bt refuges from 1996 to 2005 and then combined Bt cotton with mass releases of sterile moths and other tactics, eradicating the pest regionally. In China, farmers planted second-generation hybrid seed, which yields a refuge of roughly 25 per cent non-Bt plants randomly interspersed through the field; they adopted it for its short-term agronomic and economic advantages rather than to manage resistance, and low-level resistance reversed. In India, where non-Bt refuges have been scarce, resistance to pyramided cotton producing both Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab became widespread, and control now depends on integrated tactics such as shortening the season and destroying crop residues.

Weeds tell the same story on a longer time base. A global overview counted 220 weed species with resistance to at least one herbicide and 404 unique species × site-of-action cases; 24 species had evolved glyphosate resistance, 16 of them within herbicide-tolerant cropping systems. Compounding it, no herbicide with a genuinely new site of action had reached the market in over 30 years at the time of that review, so the chemistry available to respond was the chemistry already being outrun. The [evolutionary adaptation](/en/glossary/evolutionary-adaptation) here is ordinary population genetics operating at field scale, and the agronomic consequences run into the wider questions covered in [agricultural ecosystems and agroecology](/en/ecology/ecosystems/agricultural-ecosystems-and-agroecology).

## Regulated on how it was made, not on what it is

Regulation almost everywhere is triggered by how a plant was made rather than by what it is. The National Academies committee said plainly that "emerging technologies have blurred the distinction between genetic engineering and conventional plant breeding to the point where regulatory systems based on process are technically difficult to defend", and recommended that new varieties be safety-tested when they have novel characteristics with potential hazards, whether they were engineered or conventionally bred.

Jurisdictions then split, and the split does not track any measurable property of the resulting plant.

| Jurisdiction | What triggers GMO regulation | Treatment of edits leaving no foreign DNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| European Union | The process used, following the 2018 Court of Justice ruling | Regulated as GMOs, carrying the approval obligations of transgenic organisms |
| Canada | Novelty of the trait in the resulting plant | Exempt where no foreign DNA is present |
| India | The class of edit made | SDN-1 and SDN-2 edits exempted from GMO rules in March 2022 |
| China | Presence of exogenous genes | Covered by separate guidelines, which exclude SDN-3 |
| Kenya | Assessed case by case | Deregulation possible for deletions and knockouts |

The SDN classes in that table refer to how the cut is repaired: SDN-1 leaves a small deletion or substitution with no template supplied, SDN-2 uses a template to make a defined change without leaving foreign sequence, and SDN-3 inserts new DNA. Several of these national positions are under active revision, so the table is a snapshot rather than a settled map.

A review of these policies across countries found that the rationales offered for the split are about predictability, familiarity and reduced off-target effects — policy arguments rather than a criterion that distinguishes the products by hazard. The technique most of these rules now turn on is described in the companion page on [CRISPR and how the editing outcome is controlled](/en/biology/biotechnology/crispr-genome-editing-explained).

The market has moved more slowly than the debate. A 2021 survey by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre found one plant product from these newer techniques marketed globally, one micro-organism approved for environmental release, several in contained industrial use, and roughly 30 applications across plants, animals and micro-organisms at pre-commercial stage.

## What the safety evidence does not settle

Three questions sit entirely outside the toxicological record and are frequently answered as if it covered them. Who captures the gains is one: the pooled analysis found profit gains larger in developing countries than in developed ones, but that is a measurement of farm margins in the studies sampled, not of how surplus is distributed between seed suppliers, growers and consumers over time. Whether pesticide reductions persist is another: the 37 per cent figure is an average over a period during which resistance was accumulating in some of the same systems, and a reduction that erodes as susceptibility is lost is a different economic proposition from one that holds. And the counterfactual is the hardest of the three — the comparison is always against what growers would otherwise have done, and that alternative has itself changed over three decades of shifting chemistry, seed markets and agronomic practice.

Current evidence indicates that the food-safety question, as asked of the products assessed so far, has a clear answer. The agricultural-systems question — which crops, under whose control, with what resistance-management obligation attached — is a different question, and the safety literature was never designed to answer it.
