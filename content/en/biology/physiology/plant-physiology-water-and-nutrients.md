A tall tree lifts water tens of metres above the ground continuously, through a network with no valve, no chamber and no moving part, and spends no metabolic energy on the lift itself. The work is done by evaporation at the leaf surface, and the water is not pushed from below but pulled from above, under tension, through a continuous liquid thread. Understanding that one mechanism explains most of the rest of plant water relations, and it is a case where [the general problem of holding a physiological state steady](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained) is solved by physics rather than by a pump.

## Water potential is the only currency

Plant water movement is bookkeeping in a single variable. **Water potential** (Ψ, in megapascals) is the free energy of water per unit volume relative to pure water at atmospheric pressure, which is defined as zero. Dissolved solutes lower it; positive hydrostatic pressure such as cell turgor raises it; the matrix forces in cell walls and soil pores lower it further. Water moves spontaneously from higher to lower potential, and nothing else needs to be invoked.

That framing makes an apparently disparate set of processes one process. Water in a moist soil, held at a mildly negative potential, moves into a root held more negative still; sap in the xylem is drawn on towards leaf air spaces, where the potential of water vapour in unsaturated air is more negative again by a wide margin. Root uptake, cell expansion and evaporative loss are not three mechanisms but three positions on one gradient, and the plant sits in the middle of that gradient as a regulated resistance.

## The pull, the thread, and what breaks it

Evaporation from the wetted walls of mesophyll cells leaves curved air-water menisci whose surface tension puts the adjoining water under negative pressure. Because water molecules cohere by hydrogen bonding and adhere to the hydrophilic walls of narrow xylem conduits, that tension is transmitted downwards through unbroken columns to the roots. This is the cohesion-tension mechanism, and it has an uncomfortable implication: sap in a transpiring plant is under negative absolute pressure, a metastable state in which the liquid is technically liable to vaporise.

The theory was seriously contested. Direct pressure-probe measurements in the 1990s recorded much lower tensions than cohesion-tension required, alternative mechanisms for sap ascent were proposed, and the argument was live enough that a 1999 assessment in *Trends in Plant Science* was written to adjudicate it. Its conclusion was that the inferences from the probe work had been premature, and that new direct measurements of xylem pressure supported both cohesion-tension and the earlier indirect measurements. The episode is a useful reminder that a technique which appears to measure the quantity of interest can be measuring something adjacent to it.

The metastability is real, though, and it sets the limit on the whole system. As tension rises, gas is drawn into conduits through pit membranes and the water column breaks — cavitation, leaving an air-filled embolism that no longer conducts. Vulnerability is summarised as P50, the xylem pressure at which half of hydraulic conductivity is lost. In three tropical lowland rainforest tree species, P50 fell at −2.1 to −3.1 MPa in stems and at −1.7 to −2.2 MPa in leaves, giving leaf-to-stem safety margins of only −0.4 to −1.4 MPa. Leaves failing before stems is not a design fault; it is hydraulic segmentation, sacrificing the cheap replaceable organ to protect the expensive permanent one. The bulk-flow physics involved is the same treated in [fluid dynamics](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/fluid-dynamics-explained), applied to conduits a few tens of micrometres across.

## Stomata: the trade the whole plant is built around

Carbon dioxide entering a leaf and water vapour leaving it pass through the same pores. The two fluxes cannot be decoupled, and they are grossly asymmetric, because the vapour-pressure gradient driving water out is far steeper than the concentration gradient drawing CO₂ in. Every stoma open for [photosynthetic carbon fixation](/en/biology/cells/photosynthesis-explained) is a leak, and stomatal regulation is the continuous management of that exchange rate.

Guard cells integrate several inputs — light, intercellular CO₂, evaporative demand, and the hormone abscisic acid. The ABA signalling core is now resolved to three components: PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, PP2C protein phosphatases and SnRK2 protein kinases, which together translate a water-deficit signal into ion efflux, loss of guard-cell turgor and pore closure. Agronomic water accounting sits on top of this physiology: the FAO's reference evapotranspiration framework, built on the Penman-Monteith equation, represents a canopy through an aerodynamic resistance and a bulk surface resistance, which is a way of writing stomatal behaviour into a catchment-scale water balance. The underlying diffusion problem — maximise gas exchange, minimise the losses that come with it — is the same one solved differently in [animal gas exchange](/en/biology/physiology/respiration-and-gas-exchange).

## Sugar travels the other way, under positive pressure

Phloem transport runs on the opposite sign. Loading sucrose into sieve elements at a source lowers their solute potential, water follows osmotically from the adjacent xylem, and the resulting positive turgor drives bulk flow towards sinks where sugar is unloaded and water leaves again. This is Münch's pressure-flow hypothesis, proposed in the 1920s, and its plausibility in tall trees was long doubted on the grounds that the source-to-sink distance seemed incompatible with the pressures available.

That objection has been tested against anatomy. Compiled measurements from 32 angiosperm and 38 gymnosperm trees spanning heights from 0.1 to 50 m showed a close correlation with the scaling predictions of an optimised Münch mechanism, indicating that vascular plants of very different size and form have converged on efficient osmotic pumping units. One mechanism, operating across roughly a five-hundred-fold range of transport distance.

## Nutrients do not simply arrive with the water

Mass flow delivers dissolved ions towards roots, but uptake is selective, often against a concentration gradient, and mediated by transporter proteins whose affinities are matched to the concentrations plants actually encounter — a physiology that only makes sense alongside [how soils supply nutrients](/en/ecology/soils/nutrient-availability-and-soil-fertility).

Nitrate uptake illustrates both the design and a correction to it. Plants have long been known to possess a high-affinity system operating at micromolar nitrate and a low-affinity system operating in the millimolar range, and these were generally assumed to be genetically distinct. They are not always. The *Arabidopsis* CHL1 (NRT1.1) protein, characterised as a low-affinity transporter, turned out on expression in oocytes to show biphasic kinetics with a Michaelis constant of approximately 50 µM for the high-affinity phase and approximately 4 mM for the low-affinity phase — one protein spanning both regimes. Uptake capacity is also a geometric problem: root hairs, fine-root proliferation and mycorrhizal partnerships all work by extending absorbing surface into soil volume that has not yet been depleted.

## Running close to the limit

The hydraulic safety margin — the gap between the water potentials a plant actually experiences and the potential at which its xylem fails — turns out to be small almost everywhere. A synthesis of embolism vulnerability across 226 forest species from 81 sites found that 70 per cent operate with margins narrower than 1 MPa, and that those margins are largely independent of mean annual precipitation. Wet forests are not comfortably far from failure; they are close to it too, having built xylem matched to the conditions they normally experience. That is why drought-driven forest decline has been observed outside arid regions.

Three caveats travel with those numbers. P50 is measured on excised material rather than in an intact transpiring tree, so values are more safely compared within a study than across studies using different preparations. A single safety margin also compresses out stomatal regulation, stem water storage, rooting depth and any capacity to refill embolised conduits, all of which shift the threshold that matters in the field. And hydraulic failure is described in the synthesis itself as one primary cause of drought mortality rather than the only one, so a narrow margin indicates exposure rather than a predicted outcome. Where those thresholds sit matters well beyond the individual plant, because transpiration is a major term in [the global water cycle](/en/ecology/earth-systems/global-water-cycle-explained).
