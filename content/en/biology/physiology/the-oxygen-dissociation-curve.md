Two neighbouring accounts stop at the same place. [Gas exchange](/en/biology/physiology/respiration-and-gas-exchange) gets oxygen across a membrane into blood; [circulation and the heart](/en/biology/physiology/circulation-and-the-heart) moves the blood past tissues. Neither draws the curve that decides how much of the cargo comes off where it is wanted. That curve — haemoglobin saturation against the partial pressure of oxygen around it — is why a litre of blood carries roughly 197 mL of oxygen protein-bound against only about 3 mL dissolved at atmospheric tension, a split usually quoted as 98 per cent to 2 per cent. It is also why the bound fraction does not simply stay bound. The wider frame is set out in [the physiology overview](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained).

## What the curve plots

The axes are unremarkable: oxygen tension along the bottom, the fraction of haemoglobin's binding sites occupied up the side. The shape is not. Plotting the two against each other "reveals a sigmoid curve", and not every carrier gives one — haemoglobin's curve is sigmoidal, "whereas that of other oxygen-carrying molecules (such as Myoglobin) is hyperbolic".

A hyperbola is what a single independent binding site gives you: avid uptake where oxygen is scarcest, then a gradual flattening. That makes a superb sponge and a poor courier — the right design for myoglobin, which sits "in the cell cytoplasm" and "releases oxygen to the respiratory chain of the mitochondrion" rather than ferrying it anywhere. Haemoglobin has the opposite job, to ferry oxygen "from an extreme gradient of partial pressure from lungs (where it must remain tightly bound) to tissues (where it has to be easily released)". The sigmoid is how those two incompatible demands are reconciled. It is not a chemical accident that happens to be convenient; it is the mechanism.

## Why it bends

In most vertebrates haemoglobin is a tetramer of two α-subunits and two β-subunits, and they do not bind independently. The molecule occupies two quaternary arrangements: "the tense (T) state (unliganded Hb) which exhibits low affinity for O2, and the relaxed (R) state (liganded Hb) which exhibits high affinity for O2." Binding one oxygen destabilises T and eases the transition for the others, so saturation lags at low tension, accelerates through a narrow band, then flattens. That is the sigmoid.

How the subunits communicate is still argued. The MWC two-state model "assumes that, upon ligand binding, the T state switches to the R state without intermediate states"; the KNF model has one unliganded conformation changing sequentially with each binding event; the Perutz model routes the signal through direct α1–β2 contact. All three produce a sigmoid, so the curve alone does not adjudicate between them.

The steepness of the sigmoid is conventionally summarised by the Hill coefficient, about 2.6 in normal adult haemoglobin — below four, the number of haem groups the tetramer carries, which is itself the signal that cooperativity is real but incomplete. Variants make the link explicit: haemoglobin Bassett, a low-affinity mutant, has a Hill coefficient of 1.4 against 2.6 in HbA. Take the cooperation away and the curve straightens towards the hyperbolic case.

## P50 is a point, not a property

The number most often used to stand in for the whole curve is P50 — the oxygen tension at which haemoglobin is half saturated. For normal adult human haemoglobin it is "about 26 mmHg", and clinical reference sources give an accepted normal of 27 mmHg. A higher P50 means lower affinity and a rightward-displaced curve; a lower P50 the reverse.

The trouble is that P50 is a property of a measurement, not of a molecule. A 2019 reanalysis in *eLife* re-fitted classic comparative blood-oxygen curves — the Schmidt-Nielsen datasets of 1958 and 1970 — and ranked mammalian haemoglobins by affinity. P50 falls as body size rises: mouse 57.86 ± 0.76 mmHg, cat 43.22 ± 0.62, human 33.02 ± 0.29, elephant 23.03 ± 0.32 mmHg, with Hill coefficients falling the same way, from 3.32 in the mouse to 2.49 in the elephant. The correlation the authors themselves report is with metabolic rate: "an inverse linear correlation was found between the metabolic rate and Hb affinity", and "the greater an animal's metabolic rate, the higher was the observed maximum Hill cooperativity value". Small mammals with high [mass-specific metabolic rates](/en/biology/physiology/metabolic-scaling-and-body-size) run low-affinity, high-cooperativity haemoglobin, which unloads readily; large ones need not. The ranking is the result worth keeping — because the human value of 33.0 mmHg in that same table sits well above the 26 to 27 mmHg quoted for human blood elsewhere. P50 values are comparable within one protocol and not across them.

Reviewers of high-affinity haemoglobinopathies extend the caveat to the Hill coefficient: both are "commonly determined using in vitro standardized environmental conditions", which "does not account for transient changes in the *in vivo* modulation of Hb-O2 affinity during circulatory transit".

## The Bohr effect and its residues

The curve moves. Rising carbon dioxide and falling pH lower haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen — the Bohr effect — and the logic is close to elegant: working tissue produces carbon dioxide and acid, and those products are themselves the signal releasing more oxygen there. Carbon dioxide contributes twice. Inside the red cell it is hydrated to carbonic acid, which dissociates to bicarbonate and hydrogen ions and lowers pH; it also binds haemoglobin directly as carbaminohaemoglobin, which "stabilizes the T state, lowers affinity for oxygen, and induces oxygen unloading".

The proton-dependent part is attributed to the C-terminal residues of both chains, αArg141 and βHis146, which in the deoxygenated molecule form salt bridges — βHis146 with βAsp94 and αLys40 — holding the T state together.

Its size can be stated. The Bohr factor — the change in log P50 per unit change in pH — is about −0.5 in human HbA under standard in vitro conditions; deer mouse haemoglobins measured at 37 °C give −0.62 ± 0.04 for a high-altitude variant and −0.57 ± 0.00 for a lowland one, of the same order. The reciprocal relationship — haemoglobin "releasing its oxygen molecules in favor of binding H+ ions", so serving "as a buffering agent" while bicarbonate leaves the red cell in exchange for chloride — is the Haldane effect, a statement about carbon dioxide carriage rather than about the shape of this curve.

## The other levers

**2,3-bisphosphoglycerate.** The organic phosphate binds preferentially into the cleft between the β-subunits of deoxygenated haemoglobin, contacting βHis2, βLys82, βHis143 and βHis146; linking those subunits stabilises the T state and lowers affinity. Raised concentrations shift the curve right.

**Temperature.** Oxygen binding is exothermic, so warming lowers affinity — but the magnitude is species-specific and the spread is wide. The enthalpy of oxygenation for human HbA is −50.7 kJ per mol O₂; deer mouse haemoglobins average about −10.5 kJ per mol O₂, a "low temperature sensitivity" the authors liken to bovine haemoglobin's, and which they suggest "may therefore have adaptive significance in regional heterotherms that live in polar or alpine environments".

**A different globin altogether.** Fetal haemoglobin, α₂γ₂, sits left of the adult curve — a P50 of 19 mmHg against 27 mmHg for adult haemoglobin. That higher affinity works alongside the double Bohr effect, which the clinical literature credits with "facilitating oxygen transfer across the placenta from mother to fetus".

## Shuttle, not sponge

Put the pieces together and the shape becomes arithmetic. Where oxygen tension is high the curve has flattened into its plateau — "the high O2 tension of the lungs (100 mmHg) provides full saturation" — and saturation stays near complete even if alveolar tension falls somewhat. Where tension is low, in the systemic capillaries, the curve is steep, and a small further fall releases "large amounts of oxygen" to metabolically active cells.

That asymmetry is what a hyperbolic carrier cannot deliver. A sponge with high affinity everywhere would reach the tissue full and leave nearly full; one with low affinity everywhere would never load. Haemoglobin gets both, because its affinity depends on how much it is already carrying — then gets a second adjustment free, because the Bohr effect moves the steep region rightwards exactly where acid is being made. With only 3 mL per litre dissolved against 197 mL bound, the curve's shape governs almost the entire oxygen content of blood, and so almost everything reaching the [mitochondria that consume it](/en/biology/cells/mitochondria-and-cellular-respiration).

## How much the shifting explains

It is worth resisting the temptation to credit the Bohr effect with the bulk of delivery. A 2015 modelling study in *PLoS One* put numbers on it. Many fish carry haemoglobins so pH-sensitive that an acidosis reduces not only affinity but carrying capacity — the Root effect. Modelling arterial-to-venous pH changes of 0.2 units in trout and 0.035 units in humans, and holding the arterial-to-venous oxygen tension difference constant, the authors calculated that the right-shift increased oxygen released from haemoglobin by 73.5 per cent in trout but by only 1.3 per cent in the human. Across saturations up to 80 per cent, and at the pH change expected in trout, "the ΔPO2 for rainbow trout was, at minimum, 4-fold that of the human ΔPO2 and over 21-times greater at greater ΔpH values".

The human figure should be read carefully rather than repeated as a debunking. It is a modelled increment under one assumption — a constant arterial-to-venous oxygen tension difference — and the authors are explicit that they assume "all other aspects of the oxygen cascade remain constant, and in particular there is no change in tissue metabolism or perfusion", and that intracellular pH "cannot yet be reliably measured in real-time at the tissue/cellular level *in vivo*". What the comparison establishes is that the Bohr effect in a resting human works across a very small pH excursion, and that the sigmoid shape itself, not the shifting of it, does most of the work. It also shows what the mechanism achieves where an animal builds a large local acidosis on purpose, as teleosts do at the swim bladder and the eye: a gas gland secretes acid, and the *retia* — "dense capillary networks that can serve to localize and magnify an acidosis" — in conjunction with the Root effect "greatly elevate arterial PO2". The paper's larger modelled pH steps, of 0.55 and a full unit, are ones the authors say "far exceed what might be seen *in vivo*".

## Which direction is better is not settled

The natural assumption is that lower affinity always serves delivery better, since it unloads more readily. The high-altitude literature does not support so simple a rule. Below about 4,500 m, acclimatising humans reduce haemoglobin-oxygen affinity through elevated 2,3-DPG — yet several species adapted to high altitude have evolved *higher* affinity instead. A 2022 review in *Frontiers in Physiology* puts it plainly: "there is ongoing debate about the advantages of higher or lower hemoglobin-oxygen (Hb-O₂) affinity in humans, particularly during hypoxia". The benefit turns on the balance between loading in the lung and unloading in the periphery, and hypoxia changes both.

The human evidence is suggestive and thin. Over 200 mutations are known that raise affinity, defined there as a P50 below 24 mmHg, most falling between 12 and 17 mmHg. At Leadville, Colorado, around 3,100 m, maximal oxygen uptake fell by about 28 and 19 per cent below sea-level values in two siblings with normal affinity, while two siblings with high-affinity haemoglobin showed no reduction at all; in normobaric hypoxia equivalent to roughly 2,600 m, high-affinity carriers lost 4 ± 5 per cent against 13 ± 6 per cent in controls. Four siblings is a case series, not a settlement.

The deer mouse work shows why the question resists a general answer. High-altitude deer mice do carry higher-affinity haemoglobin, and the investigators concluded the resulting unloading penalty "is compensated by an associated increase in the tissue diffusion capacity of O2 (via increased muscle capillarization)". The curve is one term in a cascade that also contains ventilation, cardiac output, capillary density and [the diffusion distance at the far end](/en/biology/physiology/diffusion-limits-on-body-size). Shifting it adjusts one term only — the honest reason a right shift is not universally good and a left shift not universally bad.
