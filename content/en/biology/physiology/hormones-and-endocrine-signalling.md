A hormone carries no address. Once secreted it reaches every tissue the circulation reaches, at a concentration set by secretion rate, distribution volume and clearance. All of the specificity sits at the receiving end: a cell responds if and only if it holds a receptor with the right binding site, and the same molecule can therefore mean different things in different tissues. That is a strange way to build a control system, and it constrains what endocrine regulation is good at — which is one of the clearest illustrations of [what it takes to keep an internal variable within bounds](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained).

## Solubility decides where the receptor waits

The first sorting rule is chemical. Peptide hormones and catecholamines are water-soluble and cannot cross the lipid bilayer, so their receptors face outwards from the plasma membrane and the message is relayed inwards by second messengers — the machinery covered in more detail under [signal transduction pathways](/en/biology/cells/cell-signaling-pathways-basics). Steroid and thyroid hormones are lipid-soluble, cross the membrane, and bind receptors inside the cell that act as ligand-regulated transcription factors, binding response elements in target promoters. Their output is therefore a change in which proteins the cell makes, which puts them squarely in the domain of [transcriptional regulation](/en/biology/genetics/how-gene-expression-is-regulated).

The tidy corollary usually drawn from this — surface receptors act in seconds, intracellular receptors act in hours — is only half right, and the exception matters. Reviews of vitamin D metabolites describe both routes for the same class of molecule: a genomic route through the nuclear receptor affecting transcription, and non-transcriptional effects that occur quickly and are unaffected by inhibitors of transcription and protein synthesis, mediated by membrane-associated receptors. A steroid can act fast. The receptor's location, not the hormone's solubility, sets the timescale.

## Amplification is what makes vanishingly small concentrations sufficient

Circulating hormone concentrations are extremely low, and the reason a cell can respond to them at all is that surface-receptor signalling is a cascade in which each stage multiplies. One occupied receptor can activate many G proteins; each activated effector enzyme can generate many second-messenger molecules; each activated kinase can phosphorylate many substrate molecules before it is switched off. The signal is not relayed, it is progressively amplified.

Amplification also shows up in feedback loops as steepness. In the pituitary–thyroid axis, minor changes in circulating thyroxine produce considerably larger changes in thyroid-stimulating hormone, and for most people the relationship between the two is log-linear. A control loop built this way is exquisitely sensitive near its operating point, which is exactly what a regulator should be — and it also means that a modest disturbance upstream is not a modest disturbance downstream.

## The set point is individual; the reference range is a population

That same axis exposes a measurement problem that generalises across endocrinology. Thyroid-stimulating hormone varies widely across a population but only narrowly within one person, because each individual has a distinct set point in the hypothalamic–pituitary axis. A single value can therefore sit comfortably inside a population reference interval while being far from where that particular axis normally operates. The reference range describes a population; it does not describe a person, and no single measurement recovers the set point. This is a statement about what the measurement can resolve, not a basis for interpreting anyone's results.

## Some of the message is in the timing, not the amount

Concentration is not the only variable an endocrine signal carries. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone reaches the pituitary in pulses, and it is the frequency and amplitude of those pulses that determine gonadotropin subunit gene expression and the balance of hormones released: rapid pulses — more than one per hour — favour luteinising hormone, while slower frequencies favour follicle-stimulating hormone. The same average concentration delivered continuously does not produce the same result. A single blood sample is a point estimate of a waveform, and for pulsatile hormones that is a genuine limitation of the measurement rather than a detail.

## Set against a nerve

The contrast with [signalling by controlled ion leaks](/en/biology/physiology/nervous-systems-and-neurons) is instructive, because the two systems solve different problems and neither substitutes for the other.

| Property | Neural signalling | Endocrine signalling | Paracrine signalling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Delivery | Anatomically wired axon to target | Bloodstream to all perfused tissue | Diffusion through local extracellular space |
| Addressing | Set by the connection | Set by receptor presence on the target | Set by proximity and receptor presence |
| Latency | Milliseconds | Seconds to hours | Seconds to minutes |
| Persistence | Terminated by transmitter removal in milliseconds | Limited by clearance and metabolism | Limited by diffusion and local degradation |
| Coding | Spike rate and pattern | Concentration, pulse frequency, receptor density | Concentration gradient |

The row that does most of the explanatory work is addressing. Because a hormone's target list is defined by receptor expression rather than by wiring, changing receptor abundance changes who hears the message without changing the message at all. It also means any molecule that fits the receptor is, from the cell's point of view, indistinguishable from the hormone.

## Where environmental chemistry meets the axis

That last point is the mechanistic basis for concern about endocrine-disrupting chemicals. The WHO and UNEP assessment of the state of the science notes that endocrine systems are very similar across vertebrate species and that endocrine effects manifest themselves independently of species, so effects observed in wildlife or laboratory animals may also occur in humans exposed at a vulnerable time and at concentrations that alter endocrine regulation. The assessment singles out early development as the period of particular concern, because effects initiated then are often irreversible and may not become evident until much later in life — a sensitivity that follows directly from [how developmental patterning works](/en/biology/physiology/developmental-biology-explained).

Regulatory science has had to build screening around this. The US Environmental Protection Agency runs a screening, testing and research programme, organised around defined lists of chemicals, to gather the information it uses to evaluate possible endocrine effects associated with a chemical's use. The difficulty is that conventional toxicology assumes response increases with dose, and for endocrine-active compounds that assumption is contested: a substantial literature reports non-monotonic dose–response relationships, in which effects at low doses are not predicted by effects at high ones. Assessments of that literature reach different conclusions about how general the phenomenon is and how it should be handled in risk assessment, and the evidence is genuinely mixed rather than settled in either direction. Persistence compounds the problem for the subset of these compounds that are also [long-lived organic contaminants](/en/ecology/pollution/persistent-organic-pollutants).

Three limitations run through all of this. Hormones are measured in plasma, but the concentration that matters is the free concentration at the receptor, which depends on binding proteins and on local metabolism inside the target tissue. Assay platforms differ enough that values are not always comparable between laboratories. And pulsatile secretion means that a design sampling once has already discarded part of the signal. None of these are reasons to distrust endocrine measurements; they are reasons to ask what a specific number was sampling before reading a change into it.
