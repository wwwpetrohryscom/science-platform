Arterial blood in a healthy person sits between pH 7.35 and 7.45. Nothing in chemistry privileges that band; it is held there because several processes push against each other continuously, and because leaving it disables the enzymes doing the pushing. The band is defended on two clocks at once — the lungs adjust carbon dioxide removal within minutes to hours, while the kidneys reabsorb bicarbonate and excrete fixed acids over days. That is the shape of almost every physiological story: not one mechanism, but several with different time constants, and which one you notice depends on how long you watch.

## What makes a variable "regulated"

A regulated variable is one an organism has effectors for. Blood pH, arterial carbon dioxide tension of roughly 35 to 45 mmHg, plasma bicarbonate near 22 to 26 mEq/L, core temperature, osmolality, glucose — each has dedicated machinery that changes when the value drifts. Plenty of other quantities inside a body simply follow along. The distinction matters because it is easy to mistake a consequence for a target: a variable can be remarkably constant and still not be regulated, if what holds it steady is the regulation of something upstream.

Effectors come in two broad speeds. The fast ones are electrical, running on ion gradients across membranes, and are treated in the article on [neurons and nervous systems](/en/biology/physiology/nervous-systems-and-neurons). The slower ones are chemical and broadcast rather than addressed, the subject of [endocrine signalling and its feedback loops](/en/biology/physiology/hormones-and-endocrine-signalling). Most regulated variables are defended by both, which is why a single measured response rarely characterises a control system.

## The set point is a modelling convenience, not a number in the body

The thermostat metaphor is the most persistent misconception in this field, and the useful correction is not that feedback control is wrong but that "the set point" is being asked to do work it cannot do. In a 2025 review in *Biological Reviews*, a group of comparative physiologists defended negative feedback while rejecting a single organismal set point. Their definition is deliberately narrow: a set point is the value of an input variable at which one thermo-effector just becomes active. Sweating has one, shivering has another, and the gap between them is a zone in which nothing is being corrected at all.

Two consequences follow. First, an animal under sustained challenge never actually reaches a set point, because holding equilibrium requires its effectors to stay switched on; the value it settles at is better called an operating or balance point. Second, an experiment that reports "the set point shifted" may only have shown that one effector's threshold moved. The distinction is worked through in detail for temperature in [thermoregulation and the energetics of holding a body temperature](/en/biology/physiology/thermoregulation-in-animals).

**Allostasis** is the refinement usually offered in place of the thermostat: stability achieved through change rather than around a fixed value, with the cumulative cost of chronic adjustment described as allostatic load. It is a genuinely useful framing for chronic stressors, and it is also worth saying plainly that allostatic load is a constructed index rather than a directly measured quantity, and that the framework is easier to state than to test.

## A "normal" value is a population statistic with a date attached

Body temperature is the cleanest demonstration. The figure of 37.0 °C entered medicine from Carl Wunderlich's 1851 survey, which also gave a range of 36.2 to 37.5 °C. When a 2020 analysis in *eLife* pooled three United States cohorts — Union Army veterans measured between 1860 and 1940, the NHANES I survey of 1971 to 1975, and a clinical dataset covering 2007 to 2017, more than 670,000 measurements in total — it found body temperature falling monotonically by about 0.03 °C per birth decade after adjustment for age, height, weight and time of day. Across 197 birth years that amounted to roughly 0.59 °C in men and 0.32 °C in women, and contemporary means come out nearer 36.6 °C.

Whether that decline reflects changed metabolism, changed exposure to chronic infection, changed instruments or changed measurement sites is not resolved by a trend line, and the three cohorts were assembled for entirely different purposes. What the comparison does establish is narrower and still useful: the number taught as a constant is a nineteenth-century population mean, and the reference interval around any physiological variable inherits the sampling of whoever built it.

## Size decides which design an organism can afford

Nothing in physiology travels across body sizes for free. Metabolic rate rises with mass, but less than proportionally, so a gram of mouse costs far more to run than a gram of elephant — which is why a mouse is not a small elephant but a differently engineered animal. The exponent describing that relationship has been argued over for a century. A 2003 analysis in *PNAS* covering 619 mammal species from 19 orders, spanning five orders of magnitude in mass and correcting for body temperature, digestive state and phylogeny, found no support for the quarter-power exponent of 3/4 and reported basal metabolic rate scaling as mass to the power 2/3. Field measurements complicate the picture further: doubly labelled water data from 229 terrestrial vertebrate species show scaling slopes that frequently differ from 0.75 and differ between classes.

Those same field data carry the starkest number in comparative energetics. Mammals expend about 12 times, and birds about 20 times, the daily energy of an equivalent-sized reptile. The cost of running warm is not a detail; it is the constraint that organises feeding rate, habitat and life history.

Scaling also sets the geometry of exchange organs, because surface area and volume do not grow together. The consequences run through [gas exchange, where surface area and diffusion distance are the whole problem](/en/biology/physiology/respiration-and-gas-exchange), and through [circulation, where pressure and flow must be delivered against gravity](/en/biology/physiology/circulation-and-the-heart). At the small extreme, the 2 g Etruscan shrew has the thinnest air–blood barrier measured in any mammal, a harmonic mean of about 0.23 µm — close to the limit at which two cell layers can still be cells.

## Comparative physiology is a method, not a tour of animals

The organising heuristic is usually credited to August Krogh: for many problems there is an organism in which the phenomenon of interest appears in an extreme or unusual form, and that organism is the one to study. A 2026 paper in *History and Philosophy of the Life Sciences* states the principle in that form and draws out its logic — extreme constraint makes a mechanism legible. Diving mammals for hypoxia tolerance, giraffes for hydrostatic pressure, hibernators for metabolic suppression: each is chosen because the variable of interest is pushed to a value no ordinary animal reaches.

The heuristic has an obvious hazard. An organism chosen for being extreme is by construction unrepresentative, so generalising from it is an argument to be made rather than an assumption to be inherited. The same caution applies to the plant literature, where the physical problem is genuinely different — [moving water without a pump](/en/biology/physiology/plant-physiology-water-and-nutrients) has no animal analogue — and to [development, where the object being regulated changes shape while it is being regulated](/en/biology/physiology/developmental-biology-explained). Regulation is also not confined to the classical variables: [immune defence is a layered control problem](/en/biology/physiology/the-immune-system-explained) with the same structure of thresholds, effectors and costs. Where a physiological difference is adaptive rather than incidental is a question for [natural selection and how adaptation is tested](/en/biology/evolution/natural-selection-and-adaptation), and the mechanism-level account of what an [evolutionary adaptation](/en/glossary/evolutionary-adaptation) is belongs there rather than here.

## What this field is least able to settle

Three limitations are structural rather than temporary.

**Levels do not chain cleanly.** A [membrane transporter characterised in a cell line](/en/biology/cells/cell-membrane-structure-and-transport), an organ measured in isolation and an animal in its habitat are three different systems, and a mechanism established at one level constrains but does not predict the next. Much of physiology's replication difficulty lives in that gap.

**Laboratory conditions are themselves a variable.** A 2026 study in *Communications Biology* using indirect calorimetry across ambient temperatures from 22 °C to 35 °C found that neither rats nor mice have a broad thermoneutral zone at all — they have a thermoneutral point, below which energy expenditure rises and above which body temperature rises, with rats at 30 °C already at the edge of their thermal tolerance. Standard housing temperatures therefore impose a chronic thermal load on the animals from which a great deal of mammalian physiology is inferred.

**Measurement in free-living organisms is still the bottleneck.** Most of what is confidently known concerns restrained, fasted or anaesthetised subjects. Biologging has begun to change that for large animals, and the values it returns have repeatedly been outside what laboratory work anticipated.

This page describes what the evidence supports about how bodies regulate themselves. It is not clinical guidance, and no figure here — a reference interval, a metabolic rate, a temperature — should be read as a statement about any individual.
