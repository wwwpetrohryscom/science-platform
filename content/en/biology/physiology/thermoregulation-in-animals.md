Heat storage in an animal is a residual. Metabolic heat production adds to it; radiation, convection and conduction add or subtract depending on which way the gradient runs; evaporation only ever subtracts. Written as a budget, S = M ± R ± C ± K − E, with S the rate of heat storage, and everything an animal does about temperature is an intervention on one of those five terms. Nothing in that equation refers to a thermostat, and treating temperature regulation as thermostatic is the fastest route to misreading it.

The terms are not equally important. In most real heat exposure, conduction is minor, while the radiative and convective terms depend strongly on solar load, the temperature of surrounding surfaces, posture, wind and any covering; the physics behind those three routes is the subject of [heat transfer by conduction, convection and radiation](/en/physics/thermodynamics/heat-transfer-conduction-convection-radiation). For an animal under substantial heat stress, evaporation is the dominant way out, which converts a thermal problem into a water problem — and gives the useful criterion that an environment is survivable for a given activity when the evaporation required stays below the evaporation achievable.

## Endothermy is a standing charge, not a feature

Maintaining a high, stable body temperature by internal heat production is expensive, and the size of the bill is best seen in free-living animals rather than in resting ones. Daily energy expenditure measured by doubly labelled water across 229 terrestrial vertebrate species spans nearly six orders of magnitude, from 0.23 kJ per day in a small gecko to 52,500 kJ per day in a seal. More than 70 per cent of that variation is explained by body mass alone. Most of the rest is thermal strategy: mammals expend roughly 12 times, and birds roughly 20 times, the daily energy of an ectothermic reptile of equivalent size.

That multiplier is the reason ectothermy is not a deficiency. An animal that does not pay it can survive on a fraction of the food, tolerate long fasts, and put a larger share of assimilated energy into growth and reproduction. What it gives up is independence from ambient conditions and the capacity for sustained aerobic activity when cold.

It is also worth separating two words that are routinely merged. **Endothermy** is heat produced internally; **homeothermy** is a body temperature held stable over time. Reptiles that bask and then shuttle into shade achieve genuine behavioural homeothermy without endothermy at all, and mammals defend species-specific temperatures spread across a wide band — often from the low 30s to around 40 °C — rather than converging on a single value. The stable 37 °C of human textbooks is one lineage's solution, not the mammalian norm.

## The thermoneutral zone, and whether it is a zone

Below some ambient temperature, an endotherm must generate extra heat to hold core temperature; that threshold is the lower critical temperature, and the interval above it in which metabolic rate is flat is the thermoneutral zone. Measured carefully, the threshold turns out to vary between individuals in ways that are informative about what actually sets it. A 2024 study in *PNAS* exposed lean young adults to ambient temperatures from 17 to 31 °C and found women's lower critical temperature at 21.9 ± 1.3 °C against 22.9 ± 1.2 °C in men. The difference was driven by insulation — more body fat — offsetting the lower basal metabolic rate that comes with smaller body size, and no differences were detected in brown adipose tissue glucose uptake, muscle electrical activity, skin temperature, cold-induced thermogenesis or reported comfort. The determinants were physical rather than categorical.

Whether a broad flat zone exists at all is a live question for the animals most of this work is done in. Indirect calorimetry with continuous body-temperature recording across ambient temperatures from 22 to 35 °C found that neither rats nor mice have a broad thermoneutral zone: they have a thermoneutral point, below which energy expenditure rises and above which body temperature rises. Rats at 30 °C were already at the edge of their thermal tolerance, and the rise in body temperature at warmer ambients came with falling food intake and body weight — a heat-stress signature, not a benign adjustment. Since standard vivarium temperatures sit well below that point, a large share of mammalian physiology is measured in animals under continuous mild cold load.

## Effectors have thresholds; the animal does not have a dial

Sweating, panting, shivering and non-shivering thermogenesis each switch on at their own body temperature, and between the highest cooling threshold and the lowest heating threshold there is an interval in which no effector is being recruited. That structure — several thresholds rather than one target — is why an experiment showing a shifted response may have moved one effector rather than the whole control system, an argument developed in the pillar on [how organisms hold internal conditions steady](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained).

Two of the effectors are borrowed from other systems. Skin blood flow, which sets how much core heat reaches the surface at all, is a redistribution problem handled by [the circulation and its capacity to route flow regionally](/en/biology/physiology/circulation-and-the-heart). Respiratory evaporative cooling — panting — runs heat out through the same surfaces that handle [gas exchange](/en/biology/physiology/respiration-and-gas-exchange), which is why it trades against carbon dioxide balance and cannot simply be increased without limit.

## Spending less: torpor, hibernation and a shifting Arctic

The cheapest response to a bad thermal environment is to stop defending a high temperature. In deep hibernation, body temperature falls close to ambient and metabolic rate collapses, converting an animal from a heat producer into something closer to a cooling object. Arctic ground squirrels make the constraint explicit: in the Alaskan Arctic they hibernate in ground that freezes around them, and they must up-regulate heat production during torpor specifically to keep from freezing.

That makes them an unusually direct readout of soil conditions, and a 25-year record published in *Science* in 2023 used them as one. Autumn soil freeze has been delayed, and the squirrels have correspondingly delayed the point at which they start producing heat during torpor. Spring emergence from hibernation has advanced by about four days per decade in females but not in males — a divergence that matters because it changes how long the sexes overlap, and because these animals are prey for much of the local food web. It is a rare case where a warming signal is legible in the thermoregulatory behaviour of individual animals rather than inferred from a distribution shift, and the same interpretive care that applies to [attributing single events to a warming climate](/en/ecology/climate-change/extreme-weather-attribution-basics) applies to reading it.

## The 35 °C wet-bulb limit was a theory, and measurement has undercut it

A wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C has circulated for years as the point at which human thermoregulation fails, on the reasoning that skin can no longer shed metabolic heat when the environment matches core temperature at saturation. It was derived from heat-balance theory and had never been tested on people.

When it was, the limit came out lower. In a 2022 study, young healthy adults performing tasks at metabolic rates typical of daily activity reached uncompensable heat stress at a critical wet-bulb temperature averaging 30.55 ± 0.98 °C in humid conditions between 36 and 40 °C — and no participant, in any of six tested environments, reached 35 °C. In hot, dry conditions the departure was larger still, because dry heat gain outpaced evaporative cooling even though sweat rates did not differ significantly between environments. The finding is not that the theoretical argument was wrong in kind but that a single wet-bulb threshold cannot be applied across climatic conditions at all.

Substituting the empirical limits into climate projections changes the exposure picture. A 2023 analysis in *PNAS* found brief exceedances in parts of the Middle East and the Indus River Valley at 1.5 °C of warming, more widespread but still brief dangerous heat stress at 2 °C including in eastern China and sub-Saharan Africa, and a sharp rise at 3 °C, at which the US Midwest emerges as a moist-heat hotspot. The same analysis notes that holding warming below 2 °C nearly eliminates widespread uncompensable moist heatwaves.

Population-scale evidence is consistent with heat already being a substantial burden without reaching any theoretical limit. The World Health Organization reports approximately 489,000 heat-related deaths per year for 2000–2019, 45 per cent of them in Asia and 36 per cent in Europe, and an increase of roughly 85 per cent in heat-related mortality among people over 65 between 2000–2004 and 2017–2021.

Two cautions belong with those figures. Heat indices such as the wet-bulb globe temperature are screening tools: they do not incorporate clothing evaporative resistance, individual metabolic heat production, or the wide interindividual variation in sweating capacity and cardiovascular reserve, so they classify environments rather than people. And the physiological thresholds above were measured in young, healthy volunteers under controlled conditions, which is the population least likely to define the limit that matters. This page describes what the evidence shows about heat and physiology; it is not health guidance.
