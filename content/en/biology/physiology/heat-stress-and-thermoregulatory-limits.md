Thermoregulation in a warm environment is a heat-balance problem: metabolic heat is produced continuously and must be shed at the same rate, or core temperature rises. [Physiology](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained) has four routes for shedding it — conduction, convection, radiation and evaporation — and above skin temperature the first three run backwards, leaving evaporation alone.

That is why humidity matters so much more than intuition suggests, and why the limits of tolerance are set by a combined variable rather than by temperature.

## What the idealisation gets wrong

The theoretical limit assumes an efficient, unclothed, fully sweating body with skin at 35 °C. A 2024 review in *Temperature* identifies where each assumption departs from measurement, and the departures all run the same way.

**Skin runs hotter than 35 °C.** Measured skin temperatures during controlled heat trials were 36–38.5 °C. Since heat transfer depends on the gradient between skin and environment, warmer skin means a smaller gradient at any given air condition — and a lower environmental threshold at which transfer fails.

**Sweat rate is controlled.** The idealisation lets evaporation proceed at whatever rate the vapour-pressure gradient permits. Real sweating is under physiological control, subject to hydration status, sodium balance and glandular capacity, and it is lower than the unconstrained rate.

**Clothing intervenes.** Fabric between skin and air adds a resistance to vapour transfer that the model does not include.

**Acclimatisation matters, and cuts both ways.** Trial participants were unacclimatised, so their measured thresholds understate what an adapted population tolerates. But most people encountering an unprecedented heat event are also unacclimatised, which is precisely why such events are dangerous.

The combined effect is a measured compensability threshold "closer to 30.6°C in warm-humid conditions", with "a linear decrease across hotter and drier environments" — that is, the threshold is lower still in dry heat, once expressed in wet-bulb terms.

## Compensable and noncompensable

The useful distinction is not between comfortable and uncomfortable but between conditions in which core temperature stabilises at an elevated level and conditions in which it does not stabilise at all.

In the compensable regime, a person is hot, sweating heavily, and losing performance, but the heat balance closes. In the noncompensable regime it does not close, and core temperature rises for as long as exposure continues. The time to a dangerous core temperature then depends on how far above the threshold conditions are, how much heat the person is producing, and their starting point.

That is why exposure duration appears in every serious statement of the limit. Six hours is a common assumption, and it is an assumption rather than a physiological constant.

## Why the population distribution matters more than the mean

Thresholds derived from healthy young adults in a laboratory are the upper end of the distribution, not the middle of it. Age reduces sweating capacity and cardiovascular reserve; some medications impair thermoregulation directly; chronic illness reduces the margin. A threshold that a fit adult tolerates for six hours may be lethal to an older person in two.

This is the same structure as [the temperature dependence of biological rates](/en/biology/physiology/temperature-and-biological-rates) in ectotherms: the mean response is a poor guide to the tail, and it is the tail that produces the mortality. In practical terms, the number of people at risk in a heat event is set by the low end of the tolerance distribution rather than by its centre.
