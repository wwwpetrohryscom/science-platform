Basal metabolic rate — "the minimal amount of energy expended to maintain all the body's vital processes at rest", in the Endotext definition, and around 60% of total energy expenditure in a sedentary person — is one of the few numbers [physiology](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained) can compare across the animal kingdom. It rises with body mass, and it rises more slowly than mass does: a mammal ten times heavier does not burn ten times the energy at rest. The shape of that relationship is one of the few places where biology has a candidate quantitative law, which is why the argument about its exponent has been unusually sharp.

## Two predictions, both principled

The older prediction is geometric, and the 2015 *Biology* review states it plainly: "Since heat loss depends on surface area, which scales with body mass to the 2/3 power in mammals, both heat loss and compensating metabolic heat production should also scale to the 2/3 power." The review dates the argument: "This simple argument was first proposed approximately 175 years ago."

The competing prediction is three-quarters — the "3/4-power law", associated later with models deriving quarter-power exponents from the branching geometry of internal distribution networks. Quarter-power scaling was for decades presented as near-universal across organisms from unicells to trees.

The two differ by 0.083 in the exponent. Over a mass range of six orders of magnitude, that is a factor of three in predicted rate — not a subtlety.

## What the reanalysis found

A 2003 study in *PNAS* reworked the mammalian data with controls the earlier compilations lacked. Its title states the conclusion: mammalian basal metabolic rate is proportional to body mass to the two-thirds power. The analysis covered 619 species across mammalian orders, and after accounting for body temperature, digestive state, and phylogeny, the authors report that "both interspecific (0.68) and interordinal (0.65) allometric exponents were significantly different from 3/4 and were not significantly different from 2/3."

The mechanism of the earlier error is as instructive as the result. Early datasets over-represented ruminants, whose measured rates sit high for their mass, and did not consistently separate true basal measurements from post-absorptive or thermally stressed ones. Neither is a subtle statistical artefact; both are the ordinary consequence of assembling a dataset from studies that were not designed to be pooled.

## Why the question did not close

The 2003 result did not settle matters, and a 2015 review in *Biology* explains why: after "over 80 years" of debate about the causes of sublinear metabolic scaling, "a consensus has yet to be reached."

Two distinct complaints sit behind that. The first is that the relationship may not be a single straight line on log–log axes. The review confirms the departure exists and is careful about its size: "Although the scaling relationship for log mammalian basal metabolic rate versus log body mass shows significant curvature… this departure from linearity is slight (increasing r² by only 0.003)." Statistically detectable, in other words, and small — which means curvature is a reason to state the mass range a fitted exponent came from, not a reason to abandon the fit.

The second complaint is heavier. The exponent is not one number even within warm-blooded vertebrates: the review reports mass-specific scaling slopes of −0.279 for mammals against −0.348 for birds, and finds that cultured cells scale almost not at all, with slopes of −0.029 to −0.070. Physiological state moves it too — "during deep hibernation, down-regulation of metabolism results in a marked shift of interspecific metabolic scaling in mammals from being allometric… to isometric."

If a mammal's exponent changes when the mammal falls asleep for the winter, the exponent is not a constant of nature being measured with increasing precision. It is a summary of a population under stated conditions.

## What survives

Three things are robust, and they are not the exponent.

**Scaling is sublinear.** Whatever the precise value, larger animals use less energy per unit mass at rest than smaller ones. That is why a shrew must eat almost continuously and an elephant need not, and why mass-specific rates cannot be transferred between species.

**The exponent is a claim about a dataset.** Reporting one without the mass range, the taxonomic composition, the temperature control, and the digestive state is reporting a number without its conditions.

**The physiological constraints are real regardless.** Surface-to-volume geometry does bear on heat exchange, and distribution networks do impose costs. The failure was not in either mechanism but in the expectation that one of them would produce a universal constant.

## The connection to temperature

Body size is one of two variables that dominate metabolic rate; the other is temperature, and it enters differently. Mass scaling is a power law, while temperature dependence is closer to exponential over the biologically ordinary range — which means that comparing an ectotherm at 15 °C with one at 25 °C is a larger correction than comparing two mammals differing severalfold in mass. The [temperature dependence of biological rates](/en/biology/physiology/temperature-and-biological-rates) has its own literature and its own contested constant.

For a whole-organism budget, both corrections are needed, and both are approximations. What they are approximating is the cellular expenditure described in [ATP budgets per second](/en/biology/cells/atp-and-cellular-energy-budgets), aggregated over tissues that do not all scale the same way.
