Every nucleus in an early embryo carries the same genome. Development therefore cannot be explained by what the instructions say; it has to be explained by how a cell finds out where it is, when, and relative to what. That reframing — from information content to positional information — is what turned embryology from a descriptive discipline into an experimental one, and it sits alongside [the regulation of internal state](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained) as one of the two great organising problems in organismal biology.

## Geometry comes before identity

The first phase after fertilisation, cleavage, is division without growth. The zygote's volume is partitioned into progressively smaller cells, so the ratio of nucleus to cytoplasm rises sharply while the embryo's total size barely changes — a distinctive use of the machinery described in [mitosis and meiosis](/en/biology/cells/cell-division-mitosis-and-meiosis). Nothing has yet been decided about what any cell will become.

Gastrulation is the movement that follows: a coordinated set of cell rearrangements that converts a largely single-layered structure into one with an inside, an outside and a middle. The germ layers established by those movements — ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm — are the coordinate frame everything later is specified within. The order matters. Position is established by geometry and movement first, and identity is assigned to positions afterwards.

## How precisely can a cell read a gradient?

The standard model for assigning identity to position is a graded signal, a morphogen, whose local concentration tells a cell where it sits. In the fly embryo, maternally deposited Bicoid protein forms an anterior-to-posterior gradient, and nuclei activate different target genes at different concentration thresholds.

The obvious objection is that molecular concentrations are noisy. That objection was tested directly: four separate measures of precision for the Bicoid system — the concentration difference distinguishing neighbouring nuclei, the limit imposed by the random arrival of molecules at their targets, the noise in reading Bicoid out through Hunchback activation, and the reproducibility of Bicoid concentration at equivalent positions across different embryos — all came out at approximately 10 per cent. The agreement between four independent estimates is the informative part. It indicates that the embryo is not coping with a sloppy input signal by cleaning it up downstream; it is controlling absolute concentrations tightly and responding reliably to small differences, close to limits set by physics rather than by biology.

## The screen that turned pattern into genetics

Positional information as a concept needed molecules. It got them from saturation mutagenesis. A systematic search for embryonic lethal mutants of *Drosophila* published in 1980 identified 15 loci that, when mutated, alter the segmental pattern of the larva, and argued these probably represented the majority of such genes in the species. The mutant phenotypes fell into a hierarchy, indicating that segmentation involves at least three levels of spatial organisation: the entire egg as one developmental unit, a repeat unit two segments long, and the individual segment.

That nesting is the durable result. Pattern is not laid down at one scale and then decorated; it is built at successively finer scales, each level of genes reading the output of the level above and subdividing it further.

## Hox genes, and the parts of the story that do not generalise

The genes that assign identity to those subdivisions are the homeotic selector genes, clustered in the Hox complexes. Their best-known property is spatial colinearity: the order of genes along the chromosome corresponds to the order of their expression domains along the head-to-tail axis. That correspondence is conserved widely across the Bilateria, and the shared possession of a Hox cluster by protostomes and deuterostomes implies the last common ancestor of the group already had one.

The popular version of this — a universal animal body-plan toolkit obeying universal rules — needs three corrections that a 2018 review of colinearity across animal phyla sets out. Temporal colinearity, in which chromosomal order also predicts the timing of expression, is so far documented only in vertebrates, cephalochordates and some arthropods and annelids, not across Bilateria as a whole. Hox genes are recognisable in cnidarians, but the characteristic clustered arrangement and function are commonly found only in bilaterians. And several lineages, lophotrochozoans especially, have co-opted Hox genes extensively outside the colinearity rules to build novel structures. The genes are ancient; the cluster is narrower; the rules are narrower still. That distinction matters for [how evolutionary change is explained](/en/biology/evolution/evolution-explained), because much morphological change is change in where and when a conserved gene is deployed rather than change in the gene itself.

## Induction, competence, and the shrinking of the organizer

The other classical mechanism is induction: one tissue changing the fate of a neighbouring one. The founding experiment transplanted the dorsal blastopore lip of an amphibian gastrula into the ventral side of a host, where it induced a second, largely complete embryonic axis.

A century of follow-up has narrowed rather than widened the claim. Under the formal definition — a region that both induces, by changing the fate of adjacent cells, and patterns, by generating an organised set of structures — a 2016 review counts only about four demonstrated organizers in vertebrates: the primary or Spemann organizer, corresponding to Hensen's node in amniotes; the notochord; the zone of polarising activity of the limb bud; and the mid-hindbrain boundary. Work in zebrafish has gone further, indicating that organising activity is not confined to the dorsal lip at all but distributed along the entire blastula-gastrula margin, and that it emerges from the interaction of two opposing morphogen gradients, BMP and Nodal. Notably, no single molecule identified as an organizer component can by itself induce a main body axis from naive pluripotent cells.

Induction also depends on the receiver. **Competence** — the responding tissue's capacity to react to a signal at a particular moment — is set by its own history, including its chromatin state, which is why [heritable regulatory marks](/en/biology/genetics/epigenetics-explained) belong in an account of development rather than beside it.

## Potency is a maintained state, not a substance

The US National Human Genome Research Institute defines a stem cell as one with the potential to form many of the different cell types found in the body, noting that embryonic stem cells have the potential to form a complete individual whereas adult stem cells can only form certain specialised types. For most of the twentieth century that hierarchy looked like a one-way street.

It is not. Introducing four transcription factors — Oct3/4, Sox2, c-Myc and Klf4 — into mouse fibroblasts produced cells that behaved like embryonic stem cells: they formed tumours containing tissues from all three germ layers, and after injection into blastocysts they contributed to embryonic development. Nanog, expected to be required, turned out to be dispensable. The same four factors reprogrammed adult human fibroblasts the following year. The implication for developmental theory is larger than the technique: differentiation is not the progressive loss of genetic information but the active maintenance of a regulatory state, and a state that is maintained can be overwritten. Single-cell methods have since made that state directly measurable, which is the subject of the note on [single-cell approaches to evolutionary development](/en/biology/genetics/single-cell-evo-devo).

## What is not known about human development

The mechanisms above come overwhelmingly from flies, frogs, fish, chicks and mice. For humans, the evidence base is thin in a specific and structural way. Technical and ethical limits mean there have been no experimental studies beyond the fourteenth day after fertilisation, precisely the window in which gastrulation establishes the body plan; much of the descriptive anatomy still in use comes from histological study of a collection of human embryos assembled more than half a century ago; and results from mouse are routinely extrapolated despite documented differences in morphology, molecular detail and developmental tempo.

Stem-cell-derived embryo models are being built to address that gap, and they are informative, but they are models whose correspondence to the embryo is itself under investigation. Statements about human developmental mechanism should be read with that provenance attached — not as a reason to discount them, but because the confidence attached to a fly result and a human result is not the same, and the difference is rarely stated.
