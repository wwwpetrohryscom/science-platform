Over the last micrometre into a red blood cell, oxygen moves by diffusion and nothing else. No pump acts on it, no carrier drags it across, and the rate is fixed by three quantities: the area available, the distance to be crossed, and the partial-pressure difference driving it. Every breathing organ that has ever evolved — alveolar lung, fish gill, insect trachea, frog skin — is a way of making those three numbers favourable, and the differences between them are mostly differences in which of the three was cheapest to buy.

The morphometric version of the relationship is unglamorous and exact: the membrane diffusing capacity is a permeation coefficient multiplied by barrier surface area and divided by barrier thickness. Anything that raises area or thins the barrier raises the capacity; anything that lets the partial-pressure gradient collapse wastes it. This article follows that logic through the organs, and it assumes the framing set out in the pillar on [how organisms hold internal conditions steady](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained).

## The human lung, measured properly

The figure most people carry for lung surface area is too small. Electron-microscopic stereology on seven young adults averaging 70 kg puts the alveolar surface at roughly 130 m², with the capillary surface about 10 per cent smaller. Older textbook values, derived from light microscopy, were substantially lower because that technique could not resolve the texture of the alveolar surface. The barrier those surfaces enclose is correspondingly extreme: a harmonic mean tissue thickness near 0.6 µm, or about 1.11 µm counting the whole path from alveolar epithelium to the erythrocyte membrane, holding roughly 200 mL of capillary blood.

Counting the compartments gives the same impression from a different direction. A design-based stereological study of six adult lungs found a mean of 480 million alveoli, with a range from 274 to 790 million and a coefficient of variation of 37 per cent — a spread wide enough that "the number of alveoli in a human lung" is not really a single quantity. Mean alveolar volume was far more consistent at about 4.2 × 10⁶ µm³ regardless of lung size, implying roughly 170 alveoli per cubic millimetre of parenchyma. Larger lungs, in other words, have more alveoli rather than bigger ones.

Holding that surface open costs almost no tissue. Type-1 epithelial cells cover about 95 per cent of the alveolar surface, one cell spreading a cytoplasmic leaflet over some 5,000 µm² while its nucleus sits tucked into a niche between capillaries where it will not thicken the diffusion path.

## Why water breathing is the harder job

Water is a poor medium to extract oxygen from. It is dense, viscous, and carries far less dissolved oxygen per litre than air does — so a water breather must move a heavier, stickier fluid, and move much more of it, for the same oxygen return. The cost of ventilation therefore eats a much larger share of the budget, and the design pressure falls squarely on extraction efficiency.

That is the setting in which [countercurrent exchange](/en/glossary/countercurrent-exchange) makes sense. In a fish gill, water flows across the lamellae in the direction opposite to the blood beneath them. Because the two streams run against each other, blood that is already well oxygenated meets water that is still nearly fully oxygenated, and a positive gradient is maintained along the whole length of the exchanger rather than decaying to a shared equilibrium value partway along. The arrangement does not create oxygen; it prevents the gradient from being spent early.

The alternatives can be ranked by how much of the gradient they preserve. A tidal lung is the weakest case: air enters and leaves by the same route, mixes with the gas already resident, and alveolar and arterial oxygen tensions therefore sit below the tension of inspired air. Birds do better with a cross-current arrangement, in which air moves unidirectionally through parabronchi while blood capillaries cross it at an angle; the geometry allows roughly 25 per cent more efficient oxygen extraction than an alveolar lung, and it permits something a tidal lung cannot manage at all — arterial oxygen tension higher than that of the expired gas. Countercurrent flow, available where the medium can be driven in one direction across a fixed surface, is the strongest of the three.

Measured extraction efficiencies show how much room there is between designs. Respirometry on dragonfly nymphs, which ventilate rectal gills tidally rather than unidirectionally, returned an oxygen extraction efficiency of 40.1 ± 6.1 per cent in normoxic water. When the water was made progressively hypoxic, from 21.3 kPa down to 5.3 kPa, tidal volume did not change significantly but ventilation frequency rose from 9.4 ± 1.2 to 35.6 ± 2.9 breaths per minute, and extraction efficiency held — a result the authors attribute to increased gill conductance or a lowered internal oxygen tension preserving the diffusion gradient.

## Carrying what has diffused

Diffusion delivers oxygen to plasma, but plasma is a hopeless solvent for it: about 98 per cent of the oxygen in blood travels bound to haemoglobin and only 2 per cent dissolved. Haemoglobin's four binding sites interact, so the saturation curve is sigmoid rather than linear — the first oxygen bound makes the next easier, which is why saturation stays high across a broad range of arterial tensions and then falls steeply once tissue tensions are reached.

The curve is not fixed. Falling pH and rising carbon dioxide shift it rightwards, lowering affinity and releasing oxygen where metabolism is producing acid — the Bohr effect, which is a delivery mechanism disguised as a chemical quirk. Rising temperature and rising 2,3-diphosphoglycerate shift it the same way; the reverse conditions shift it back. A working muscle therefore extracts more oxygen than a resting one from identically saturated blood, without any change in the blood itself. The gas leaving on the return trip is carried by the same protein under the opposite bias, which is the point at which gas exchange stops being separable from [the pressures and flows of the circulation](/en/biology/physiology/circulation-and-the-heart) and from the [mitochondrial demand that sets the whole cascade in motion](/en/biology/cells/mitochondria-and-cellular-respiration).

## Thin air, and what adapting to it looks like

Around 83 million people live above 2,500 m, where the inspired partial pressure of oxygen is permanently reduced and the gradient at the alveolar surface is correspondingly smaller. Populations resident on the Tibetan Plateau, in the Andes and in the Ethiopian highlands have been there long enough for selection to act, and the genomic signatures found in them are not the same. In Tibetans they concentrate in the hypoxia-inducible factor pathway, notably *EPAS1*, which encodes HIF-2α, and *EGLN1*, which encodes one of its regulators. Andean and Ethiopian populations show different genes, which the reviewers read as converging on shared hypoxia-response pathways and on the same physiological processes — erythropoietic and cardiovascular regulation — rather than on a shared phenotype. The haematological outcomes in fact differ: Andean highlanders show the classic excessive erythrocytosis with arterial hypoxaemia, Tibetans a blunted erythropoietic response with hypoxaemia, and Ethiopian highlanders haemoglobin and arterial oxygen saturation inside sea-level ranges. Three routes, three destinations, one environment survived.

The maladaptive side is equally instructive. Chronic mountain sickness, an excessive polycythaemic response to sustained hypoxia, is reported at prevalences from about 1.2 to 33 per cent across high-altitude samples, varying with age, sex and population of origin. Living high is not a solved problem for everyone who does it, and the spread in those prevalence estimates is itself a warning about how differently the same environment is being measured in different studies.

## What a morphometric capacity does not tell you

The structural numbers above describe a capacity, not a working rate, and the gap between the two is large. When physiological and morphometric estimates of diffusing capacity were compared in the same animals, the physiological value came out at roughly half the morphometric one, with the discrepancy distributed unevenly between the membrane and blood components; a study across four canid species found the same pattern. Part of that gap is model error, from an older morphometric treatment of tissue and plasma as two barriers in series. The larger part is that the physiological measurements were made on resting or anaesthetised animals, which never call on the capacity: measured on exercising foxhounds, the physiological value rises with blood flow and comes into close agreement with the morphometric estimate near maximal oxygen uptake.

Two further limits are worth naming. Capillary transit time, the interval a red cell has in contact with the surface, is short — measured at about 0.3 s in dogs running at maximum oxygen uptake — so a capacity that looks generous at rest can become the binding constraint during exercise. And gas exchange organs are plastic: chronic hypoxia enlarges them across phyla, from the gills of fish and salamanders to the skin folds of bullfrogs to the mammalian lung and placenta. A measurement taken from an animal is therefore a measurement of that animal's history as much as of its species, which is one reason field values keep departing from laboratory expectations. The same tension between exchange surface and the cost of maintaining it recurs where the medium is water vapour rather than oxygen, in [the stomatal trade-off that plants have to solve](/en/biology/physiology/plant-physiology-water-and-nutrients).
