A heart is an expense. It runs continuously, it cannot be switched off for maintenance, and in an ordinary resting mammal the left ventricle alone accounts for something on the order of a tenth of whole-body metabolic rate. All of that buys one thing: fluid moved past tissues fast enough that diffusion — which stalls beyond about a millimetre, because the time it takes rises with the square of the distance — is only ever asked to cover the last short step.

Reading circulatory designs as a set of bills rather than a ladder of sophistication makes the comparative literature much easier to follow. Each lineage faces the same charges — generating pressure, distributing it selectively, resisting gravity, keeping fluid inside the vessels — and has settled them differently. The exchange problem waiting at the far end of the delivery is the subject of [gas exchange and the diffusion gradients that drive it](/en/biology/physiology/respiration-and-gas-exchange); the framing of regulation used throughout is set out in the pillar on [how organisms hold internal conditions steady](/en/biology/physiology/physiology-explained).

## "Open" and "closed" describe plumbing, not sophistication

The textbook contrast — closed vessels in vertebrates, an open haemocoel in insects — is usually presented as a progression, and it is not one. An insect circulatory system is a dorsal heart plus a set of accessory pulsatile organs that push haemolymph into the antennae, wings and legs, which is a directed distribution problem solved with different hardware rather than an absent solution. Reviewers of the system make the further point that it has been badly understudied relative to its importance, and that its jobs extend well beyond transport: it [moves heat around the body](/en/biology/physiology/thermoregulation-in-animals), it assists tracheal ventilation in strong fliers, and it is functionally integrated with the [insect's immune defences](/en/biology/physiology/the-immune-system-explained), with reflex bleeding used against predators.

The real distinction is not open versus closed but whether the system can hold a pressure difference between where fluid is delivered and where it is collected. Closed systems can, which is what makes selective redistribution possible — sending flow to working muscle and away from the gut — and that capability is what costs.

## Separating the circuits changed what pressure could mean

In a fish the heart drives blood through the gills first and then onward to the body, so the same pressure head must serve gas exchange and systemic perfusion; whatever the gill vasculature dissipates is lost to the rest of the animal. Splitting the circuit into pulmonary and systemic halves removes that constraint and allows the two to run at different pressures — a low-pressure lung circuit that will not force fluid across a barrier built to be thin, and a high-pressure body circuit that can be regulated regionally.

Reptiles occupy the informative middle. Their incompletely divided ventricles allow blood to be shunted between circuits, and that has repeatedly been read as an unfinished version of the mammalian arrangement. It is better read as a control option: an animal that is not ventilating continuously has reason to be able to route blood past the lungs.

## The pump is not one design

Mammalian cardiomyocytes are wide and dense with transverse tubules, invaginations that carry the depolarising signal deep into the cell so that calcium release is uniform and contraction is strong. Bird cardiomyocytes are long, thin and have no t-tubules at all — the same morphology found in ectothermic non-avian reptiles, whose hearts run at low rates and low pressures. Birds nonetheless achieve higher contraction rates and higher developed pressures than mammals do.

The resolution, set out in a 2022 review in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, is that birds rely on a strong transsarcolemmal calcium influx through the L-type calcium current together with high-gain calcium-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, plus an internal relay that propagates release inward without needing tubules. Two architectures, opposite in their cellular geometry, reach overlapping performance. The same review is candid that cellular calcium dynamics in bird hearts remain thinly characterised — an unusual admission for a system this well studied, and a reminder of how much comparative cardiac physiology is extrapolated from a handful of mammals.

Coordination is a separate problem from contraction. The conduction system that sequences atrial and ventricular activation develops under the influence of haemodynamic forces and of the epicardium, and comparative embryology rather than fossils is what makes its evolutionary history accessible, since the tissues involved do not preserve.

## Gravity charges by the metre

Standing tall is expensive in a way that has nothing to do with metabolism and everything to do with hydrostatics. Every metre of height between the heart and the head requires roughly an additional 77 mmHg of pressure at heart level simply to deliver blood upward, and in an adult giraffe the head can sit more than 2 m above the heart. The consequence is a mean systemic arterial pressure of 200 to 250 mmHg at heart level, more than twice that of most mammals, maintained regardless of posture or activity.

That pressure is a permanent energy charge. A 2025 analysis in *The Journal of Experimental Biology* estimates that the giraffe's left ventricle consumes about 16 per cent of resting whole-body metabolic rate, against roughly 9 per cent for a mammal of the same mass with a shorter neck. The same model shows why the animal is built the way it is: had the giraffe gained its height by extending the neck alone, the cost would have been about 21 per cent, but the long limbs — which evolved before the long neck — raise the heart itself, shortening the column it has to lift. The vertical heart-to-head distance in adult giraffes appears to be the largest ever evolved in a terrestrial vertebrate, which suggests the design is close to a limit rather than merely unusual.

Genomic work adds a second layer. A chromosome-level giraffe genome flagged *FGFRL1* as an outlier carrying seven amino acid substitutions found in no other ruminant, and mice edited to carry the giraffe version proved unusually resistant to hypertension and had higher bone mineral density — a rare case where a comparative hypothesis about blood pressure was tested experimentally rather than argued from correlation.

## Where fluid actually crosses the vessel wall

The version of capillary exchange most people learn — filtration at the arterial end, reabsorption at the venous end, driven by the balance of hydrostatic and colloid osmotic pressures — is the part of this subject most in need of correction. The revision, laid out in a 2010 review in *Cardiovascular Research*, has two components.

First, the semipermeable layer is not the endothelial cell membrane but the **glycocalyx**, the gel-like coat on the luminal surface, and the osmotic pressure that matters is the one immediately beneath it rather than the one out in the interstitium. Because narrow breaks in the junctional strands produce high local outward velocities, the sub-glycocalyx protein concentration is held low, and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure influences filtration far less than the classical model predicts.

Second, and more consequentially, sustained venous reabsorption does not happen in most tissues. Absorption is transient; in the steady state slight filtration prevails even in venules, with the lymphatics returning the difference. Sustained absorption occurs only where an epithelium is secreting fluid that flushes interstitial protein away, as in the kidney and the intestinal mucosa. The classical diagram is not a simplification of this picture; it points the arrow the wrong way for most of the body.

## Switching the system off on purpose

Diving mammals run the cardiovascular system through transitions no terrestrial animal experiences. Each dive cycles lung collapse and reinflation, bradycardia and tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction and vasodilation, and oxygen-store depletion and restoration. Blood oxygen partial pressures during a dive can fall below 20 to 30 mmHg — levels considered injurious to oxygen-dependent tissues such as brain and heart in humans, tolerated here because of enhanced onboard oxygen stores, selective delivery to the tissues that cannot wait, and unusual tissue buffering.

Most of what is known about this comes from biologging rather than from the laboratory. Time-depth recorders and, later, instruments logging heart rate, blood flow and body temperature in free-ranging animals turned diving physiology from an inference into a measurement, and the values they returned repeatedly differed from what restrained-animal work had suggested. That is the standing limitation of this whole field: pressures and flows are easy to record in an anaesthetised, supine subject and hard to record in a moving animal, and the two are not the same system. A haemodynamic trace also says nothing directly about the fluid mechanics producing it — whether flow is laminar or disturbed, and how vessel geometry shapes resistance, is the province of [fluid dynamics and the Reynolds number](/en/physics/mechanics-waves/fluid-dynamics-explained) rather than of the pressure record itself.
