Two very different quantities get quoted for the diversity of life on Earth, and most of the apparent disagreement comes from treating them as one. The first is a tally of names in a catalogue. It can be recomputed on demand, and it is right or wrong in a way that can be audited entry by entry. The second is an extrapolation from that tally to a total nobody has observed, and it is right or wrong only in the weaker sense that its assumptions hold or fail. Published extrapolations currently span from a few million to a trillion. That range is not uncertainty around a shared measurement; it is the signature of methods that do not agree on what they are counting.

## What the catalogues hold, and why two of them disagree

The August 2026 release of the **Catalogue of Life**, COL26.8, contains 2,183,133 accepted names at the rank of species. Animals account for 1,531,146 of them, plants for 388,686 and fungi for 157,044. Alongside those accepted entries sit 1,931,136 species-rank names classed as synonyms — names published in good faith that later work folded into an existing taxon. Nearly half the species-level names the catalogue tracks, in other words, do not currently designate a distinct organism. The machinery that decides which name survives is the subject of the [rules of biological nomenclature](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained); the point here is that a raw count of published names would overstate the catalogued total by something close to a factor of two.

The Global Biodiversity Information Facility maintains a different reconciliation of the same literature. Its backbone taxonomy, the synthetic classification used to align names across contributing datasets, resolves to about 2.61 million accepted species and underpins roughly 3.93 billion occurrence records as of September 2026. The difference of more than 400,000 names between the two products is not a dispute about biology. It reflects how aggressively each one merges names it cannot confidently separate, and which regional and group-specific checklists each has ingested. Any headline "number of known species" inherits whichever choice its source made.

## Turning a name count into a world total

Every global estimate works the same way in outline: find a relationship that holds where sampling is good, then apply it where sampling is poor. The relationships chosen are what separate the answers.

| Method | What it assumes | Where it has been applied | Known weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Host specificity | A known number of specialist consumers per host lineage, extrapolated across all hosts | Tropical canopy arthropods on trees | Specificity measured on a few sites, generalised to all forests |
| Higher-taxon accumulation | Descriptions of families and orders saturate before species do, so the curve for higher ranks predicts the total below them | All eukaryote kingdoms | Fails where higher-rank description has not saturated, which is the situation in prokaryotes |
| Taxonomic ratios | A stable ratio between two groups, such as fungi per vascular plant, transfers to unsampled regions | Fungi, parasites | The ratio is itself measured from well-worked temperate floras |
| Abundance scaling | A scaling law fitted across many orders of magnitude of community size extends to the global community | Microbial diversity | Extrapolation far beyond the fitted range is untestable |
| Sequence census | Pooling enough environmental sequence data eventually saturates the discovery curve | Bacteria and archaea | A sequence cluster is a proxy for a taxon, not one |

The single most cited figure comes from the second row. Analysis of how the description of higher ranks has slowed over time yielded an estimate of about 8.7 million eukaryotic species, with a standard error of 1.3 million, of which roughly 2.2 ± 0.18 million are marine. On that basis some 86 per cent of species on Earth and 91 per cent of species in the ocean still awaited description. The same work reported kingdom-level estimates — 7.77 million animals, 611,000 fungi, 298,000 plants — and was explicit that the approach could not be applied to prokaryotes at all, because their higher ranks are still being described at a steady pace with no asymptote to fit.

## The arthropod estimate that fell by a factor of four

Extrapolation from insect host specificity produced the most widely repeated figure of the late twentieth century: 30 million or more arthropod species. A review of the methods that succeeded it reached very different numbers. Averaging across newer estimates gives roughly 1.5 million beetles, 5.5 million insects and 7 million terrestrial arthropods, and treats totals of 30 million or more as extremely unlikely. With about a million insects named, that implies roughly 80 per cent still undescribed. The same review reported that unbiased studies of insect faunas already worked over thoroughly indicate only 1 to 2 per cent of species may be genuinely cryptic — molecular data revealed far fewer hidden species in well-studied groups than the enthusiasm for DNA-based delimitation had suggested.

The correction did not come from better counting. It came from noticing that host specificity had been measured on a handful of tree species at a handful of tropical sites and then multiplied across the whole tropics as though it were a constant.

## Fungi and microbes, where the choice of method decides the answer

The kingdom-level estimate of 611,000 fungi sits inside a literature that mostly disagrees with it. A re-examination drawing on plant-to-fungus ratios, environmental sequencing and the discovery of cryptic species inside morphological species complexes concluded that 2.2 to 3.8 million is the defensible range, and that with about 120,000 species accepted at the time only 3 to 8 per cent had been named. The catalogued fungal total has since risen to 157,044 — real progress, and still a small fraction of any of these estimates. Why the [fungal kingdom](/en/biology/microbiology/fungi-explained) is so poorly catalogued is partly that most fungi are only ever encountered as sequences in soil or as symbionts inside other organisms.

Prokaryotes make the problem sharper. Combining a dominance scaling law fitted across some 35,000 sampled communities with a lognormal abundance model produced a prediction that Earth holds as many as a trillion microbial species. A census-based analysis using 1.7 billion high-quality 16S rRNA reads from 34,368 samples across 492 studies reached 0.8 to 1.6 million bacterial and archaeal operational taxonomic units at 97 per cent similarity — about six orders of magnitude lower, with the authors arguing that phenomenological scaling relationships fitted to individual communities cannot be extrapolated to the whole planet. The evidence is genuinely mixed here, and the two results are not reconcilable by splitting the difference. They rest on different objects: one counts predicted lineages, the other counts clusters that current sequencing can distinguish. The dependence of microbial diversity figures on [how microbes are cultured and sequenced](/en/biology/microbiology/culturing-and-sequencing-microbes) is not a side issue; it is most of the disagreement.

## How fast the gap is closing

The arithmetic of description is discouraging. Over the two decades before 2011, new species were being named at about 6,200 per year with a standard deviation of 811, and the average taxonomist described 24.8 species across an entire career. At an estimated US$48,500 per description, completing the eukaryote catalogue on those figures would take roughly 1,200 years and 303,000 taxonomists at a cost near US$364 billion. Those are 2011 numbers and the rate has shifted since — description of fungi, for one, accelerated once molecular delimitation became routine — but no plausible increase in effort changes the order of magnitude of the shortfall.

## What a global total cannot settle

Three limitations travel with every figure on this page. The first is that the totals are only as coherent as the underlying [definition of a species](/en/biology/taxonomy/what-is-a-species), and that definition is applied differently across kingdoms; a bacterial sequence cluster at 97 per cent similarity and a described beetle are not the same kind of object, so summing them produces a number with no consistent unit.

The second is that the described total is not a random sample of the undescribed one. Catalogued diversity is concentrated where taxonomists have worked — large, conspicuous, temperate, terrestrial organisms — which is why the animal count is three-quarters arthropods and why nematodes, abundant almost everywhere, hold fewer than 20,000 accepted names. Extrapolating from a biased sample is the whole difficulty, not an incidental flaw.

The third is that a global total is nearly useless as a conservation input on its own, a point developed in the existing article on [why species counts mislead conservation](/en/ecology/biodiversity/why-species-counts-mislead-conservation) and in the note on [how extinction rates are estimated](/en/insight/extinction-rate-estimates-and-their-assumptions). Loss is measured against a described baseline, so an estimate that most of the world's organisms have never been named is simultaneously an admission that most losses will never be recorded. That is a stronger and more defensible claim than any single total, and it does not depend on which of these methods turns out to be right.
