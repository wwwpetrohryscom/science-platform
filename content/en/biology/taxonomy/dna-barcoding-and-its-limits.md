DNA barcoding identifies a specimen by comparing a short standard sequence against a reference library, applying rather than discovering the [classification](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained) the library encodes. It works, it has transformed the practical business of identification, and its failures cluster in a specific and predictable place.

## The number that matters

A 2012 study in *PLOS One* compared matching methods and reported the pattern directly: "success rates are significantly lower for recently diverged species (∼75%) than for older species (∼97%) (P<0.00001)."

Twenty-two percentage points is a large gap, and it falls exactly where the taxonomic questions are hardest. Distinguishing two long-separated genera is not usually the problem; distinguishing two recently separated species is, and that is where the method weakens.

## Why the failure is where it is

Barcoding works because the sequence has accumulated differences between species and few within them. That requires time. In a recent divergence, three things are true at once.

**Not enough substitutions have accumulated.** There has been insufficient time for fixed differences to build between the lineages.

**Ancestral variation is still segregating.** Both descendant species may carry haplotypes from their common ancestor, so the gene tree does not match the species tree. This is [incomplete lineage sorting](/en/biology/taxonomy/phylogenetics-explained), and it is expected rather than anomalous when speciation events are close together.

**Gene flow may be continuing.** Recently diverged species often still exchange genes occasionally, and a single mitochondrial marker is particularly exposed to that — one hybridisation event can move a whole mitochondrial genome between species permanently.

These are not defects in the assay. They are properties of the biology, and no improvement in sequencing chemistry addresses them.

## What the method actually assumes

Two assumptions are load-bearing and frequently unstated.

**The reference library contains the species.** A query sequence is assigned to the nearest reference, and a specimen whose species is absent from the library will be assigned to something else with apparent confidence. Coverage of described species is far from complete, and it is worst for the invertebrate groups where diversity is highest.

**The taxonomy the library encodes is right.** Barcoding does not discover species boundaries; it applies existing ones. Where the underlying taxonomy is outdated or where a named species is in fact several, the barcode faithfully reproduces the error.

## Where it is strongest

The method is at its best in exactly the situations that motivated it: identifying material that cannot be identified morphologically. Larvae, fragments, gut contents, processed products, and specimens outside anyone's expertise all become identifiable, and for well-covered groups the success rate is high.

It is also the foundation of [environmental DNA surveys](/en/biology/taxonomy/environmental-dna-and-what-it-detects), which inherit both its strengths and its two assumptions — with the added difficulty that an eDNA sample has no specimen to fall back on when the assignment is doubtful.

## The honest summary

Barcoding is a matching tool with a quantified accuracy that varies systematically with divergence age. Reporting an identification without saying which library was searched, and without acknowledging that recent divergences fail more often, presents a 75 per cent result with the authority of a 97 per cent one.
