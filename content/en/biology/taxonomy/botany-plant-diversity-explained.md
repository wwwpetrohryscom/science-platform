The green cover of the continents is dominated, numerically and almost everywhere ecologically, by one recent branch of a much older group. World Flora Online currently accepts 382,401 species among the 1,649,543 plant names it tracks. In the August 2026 release of the Catalogue of Life, flowering plants account for 352,619 of the 368,152 accepted vascular species — roughly 96 per cent — while all the world's conifers come to 835 and all its cycads to 376. Any account of botanical diversity has to explain that shape: a deep history of successive lineages, almost all of them now small, and one late radiation that swamped the count. The group labels used below sit at ranks — class, division, kingdom — that are conventions of the naming system rather than natural units, as the [overview of ranks and nomenclature](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained) sets out.

## One freshwater algal lineage made it onto land

The closest living relatives of land plants are not the seaweeds but the Zygnematophyceae, a group of freshwater green algae that includes the desmids and *Spirogyra*. A phylotranscriptomic analysis using up to 852 nuclear genes and 1,701,170 aligned sites across 92 streptophyte taxa recovered that sister relationship robustly across 69 separate analytical treatments, and later work using site-heterogeneous models reached the same placement. The ancestor of every moss, fern and oak was therefore something that lived in fresh water and already had a good deal of the necessary biochemistry.

How much of it was in place before the move is now traceable. The One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative sequenced 1,124 species spanning Archaeplastida and found that large expansions of gene families preceded — rather than followed — the origins of green plants, land plants and vascular plants in turn. The genetic groundwork for each transition was laid down in advance of the anatomical change it eventually supported. The same analysis found whole-genome duplications occurring repeatedly through the history of flowering plants and ferns, which is one reason plant genomes are such awkward objects to compare.

Dating the transition is harder than reading the oldest fossils, because early land plant megafossils are sparse and their stratigraphic distribution is patchy. A Bayesian relaxed-clock analysis that codified 37 fossil calibrations and integrated over competing hypotheses for bryophyte–tracheophyte relationships recovered a Cambrian origin for Embryophyta with highest probability, and put crown vascular plants between the Late Ordovician and the late Silurian. That is substantially earlier than the fossil record alone would suggest, and the mismatch is a standard feature of divergence-time work rather than a fault in either dataset — the reasoning behind [molecular clocks and their calibration](/en/biology/taxonomy/phylogenetics-explained) explains why the two sources of evidence usually disagree in this direction.

## Which generation you are actually looking at

Every land plant alternates between two multicellular bodies: a haploid **gametophyte** that makes gametes by mitosis, and a diploid **sporophyte** that makes spores by meiosis. The confusion this causes is worth clearing up, because it changes what the anatomy is for.

The green cushion of a moss is the gametophyte. Its sporophyte is the small brown stalk and capsule growing out of that cushion, nutritionally dependent on it and short-lived. In a fern, a pine or a rose, the relationship is inverted: the plant you see is the sporophyte, and the gametophyte has been reduced to a thumbnail-sized green flake on damp soil, or — in seed plants — to a handful of cells retained inside pollen grains and ovules. The trend across land plant history is not from simple to complex so much as from a life cycle whose independent phase is haploid to one whose independent phase is diploid, with the other generation reduced and enclosed. Nearly everything a reader recognises as botany, including the [light-harvesting machinery of photosynthesis](/en/biology/cells/photosynthesis-explained) operating in leaves, is sporophyte biology in the vascular lineages and gametophyte biology in the mosses.

## Four problems, and the traits that answered them

Terrestrial life poses a specific and finite set of physical difficulties: water loss to the air, mechanical support without buoyancy, gamete transfer without a liquid medium, and provisioning an embryo away from the parent's immediate surroundings. The major groups can be read as different distances travelled along those four problems.

| Group | Independent generation | Innovation that mattered | Accepted species, COL26.8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Liverworts | Gametophyte | A cuticle over a thalloid or leafy gametophyte; no stomata | 7,500 |
| Mosses | Gametophyte | Stomata on the sporophyte capsule in most lineages; conducting cells without lignin | 12,031 |
| Hornworts | Gametophyte | A sporophyte with a persistent basal meristem | 305 |
| Lycophytes | Sporophyte | Lignified water-conducting tissue; true roots | 1,490 |
| Ferns | Sporophyte | Large leaves with branched vasculature | 12,444 |
| Gymnosperms | Sporophyte | Pollen and seed — fertilisation and dispersal without free water | 1,212 |
| Flowering plants | Sporophyte | Enclosed ovules, double fertilisation, fruit | 352,619 |

Lignin is the pivot in that table. It made vertical growth possible and, with it, competition for light, which is the selective context for almost everything that follows. Pollen is the second: once the male gametophyte could be delivered as a dry, wind- or animal-borne package, reproduction stopped requiring a film of water and seed plants could occupy dry interiors.

## The sequence is not a ladder

The order of the rows above is a sequence of innovations, not a ranking of organisms, and the difference matters more than it sounds. A living liverwort is not a preserved stage of early terrestrialisation; it has the same several hundred million years of history behind it as an orchid, spent under different selection.

Recent phylogenomic work makes this concrete. Analyses accommodating compositional heterogeneity across sites support bryophyte monophyly — liverworts, mosses and hornworts forming a clade — and, in several treatments, place a mosses-plus-liverworts group called **Setaphyta** as sister to all other land plants. Of fifteen possible rooted arrangements for the major embryophyte lineages, that work rejected all but three, which differ only in where hornworts attach. The consequence is that the ancestral land plant was probably more complex than the older textbook topology implied, and that the simplicity of modern liverworts reflects loss rather than retention. Reading traits off a phylogeny requires knowing which way the arrows point, and that has changed direction here within the last decade.

## A count of species is not a measure of importance

The gymnosperm row is the clearest illustration. The Catalogue of Life lists 388 accepted species in Ginkgoales, of which 387 carry an extinct flag: the group is a fossil clade with one survivor, *Ginkgo biloba*. Conifers fare better at 835 accepted species, cycads at 376 — 1,212 living gymnosperms in total against more than a third of a million flowering plants. Yet that small group holds the biomass of the boreal zone and much of the temperate montane forest, as the treatment of [forest ecosystem structure](/en/ecology/forests/forest-ecosystems-explained) sets out. Richness and ecological weight are close to uncorrelated across this tree, which is the main reason a headline count of botanical diversity carries less information than it appears to.

## Darwin's abominable mystery, restated

The flowering plant radiation remains genuinely unresolved, though the question has been sharpened. Molecular clocks have long placed the origin of angiosperms well before their first unequivocal fossils, which appear in the Early Cretaceous. A Bayesian analysis of about 15,000 fossil occurrences across 198 families, correcting explicitly for incomplete sampling, found several families originating in the Jurassic and rejected a Cretaceous origin for the group — while also recovering a marked increase in lineage accumulation between 125 and 72 million years ago. So the rapid Cretaceous diversification that puzzled Darwin appears real, but it happened to a lineage that already existed and had left no fossils anyone has recognised.

What is missing is the physical evidence for that earlier interval. Until pre-Cretaceous angiosperm fossils are found and accepted, the case rests on inference from sampling models and clocks, and the discrepancy could still be resolved by revising either the clocks or the fossil identifications rather than by discovery. Two further limits are worth stating plainly: catalogue totals differ between products — World Flora Online accepts 382,401 species where the Catalogue of Life's plant kingdom holds 388,686, and the two are not curating an identical scope — and plant lineages hybridise and change ploidy often enough that the [boundary between one species and another](/en/biology/taxonomy/what-is-a-species) is harder to draw here than in most animal groups. Anyone comparing botanical diversity figures across sources is comparing curation decisions as much as biology, a problem examined more generally in the article on [how many species exist](/en/biology/taxonomy/how-many-species-are-there).
