Before 1977 the deepest division in biology was thought to be between cells with nuclei and cells without. That year a comparison of ribosomal RNA catalogues reported that living systems represent [one of three aboriginal lines of descent](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC432104/): the eubacteria, the archaebacteria — then known mainly through methanogens — and the urkaryotes, represented by the cytoplasmic component of eukaryotic cells. The prokaryotes, which had looked like one thing, were two, and the split was invisible under a microscope.

## Why a molecule, and why that one

Deep classification had been stuck because morphology runs out. A bacterium and an archaeon offer almost no comparable structures, so there is nothing to score. Sequence comparison removed the constraint: differences between the sequences of two organisms give a direct quantitative measure of evolutionary distance, and ribosomal RNA is present in every cell, performs the same job everywhere, and changes slowly enough to retain signal over billions of years. Comparing it is what [made the three-domain classification visible](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26866/) in the first place.

Thirteen years later the same reasoning was formalised. A 1990 proposal argued that life divides into three primary groupings and introduced [a new rank, the domain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC54159/), above kingdom, naming them Bacteria, Archaea and Eucarya. It is worth noticing what kind of act that was. The evidence was a phylogenetic result; the domain itself was a nomenclatural invention, a container created because the existing ranks had run out — the distinction developed in [how ranks and names are governed](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained).

## The third domain moves inside the second

The three-domain picture requires eukaryotes to branch off beside Archaea, not from within them. Phylogenomic analyses using larger gene sets increasingly failed to reproduce that, placing eukaryotic core genes inside the archaeal domain instead — which, if correct, means there are two primary domains and eukaryotes are a composite lineage arising from within one of them.

Metagenomics turned this into a positional question. Deep marine sediment from the Arctic Mid-Ocean Ridge, cored about 15 km from the Loki's Castle vent field at 3,283 m below sea level, yielded a candidate archaeal phylum named after that site, Lokiarchaeota, which [formed a monophyletic group with eukaryotes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4444528/) and carried an expanded repertoire of proteins previously thought eukaryote-specific, including machinery suggestive of membrane remodelling. Subsequent sampling expanded this into the [Asgard archaea](/en/glossary/asgard-archaea), and an analysis of an enlarged genomic sample placed eukaryotes [as a well-nested clade within Asgard archaea](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10307638/), sister to a newly proposed order, the Hodarchaeales, within Heimdallarchaeia. The archaeal side of this reorganisation is described further in the article on [bacteria and archaea](/en/biology/microbiology/bacteria-and-archaea-explained).

## The organism that took twelve years to grow

Genomes assembled from environmental DNA describe organisms nobody has seen. Getting one into culture took a Japanese group [a twelve-year study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7015854/), starting from a bioreactor enrichment of sediment collected at 2,533 metres depth in the Nankai Trough and ending with seven years of in-vitro enrichment. The organism, 'Candidatus Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum' strain MK-D1, is a coccus about 550 nm across that degrades amino acids in syntrophy with partner organisms, has a lag phase of 30 to 60 days and a doubling time estimated at 14 to 25 days.

Two findings from it matter. The cell has no visible internal complexes of the kind that had been proposed for Asgard archaea — the eukaryote-like proteins are present, the eukaryote-like organisation is not. And the authors are explicit that roughly two billion years separate this organism from the ancestor that gave rise to eukaryotes, so it constrains hypotheses rather than exhibiting an ancestor. A modern relative is not a fossil.

The name itself records a further complication. Even after the isolation, the organism was published under the provisional 'Candidatus' designation rather than as a validly named species, with the authors explaining separately why that status remained necessary. The Asgard lineages above it are in the same position: they are candidate phyla, proposed names rather than validly published ones. The nomenclature and the biology advance on separate schedules, and at this depth in the tree the nomenclature is lagging.

## Branches that are actually mergers

The other reason the deep diagram resists being a tree is that two of its major features are fusions. Mitochondria and plastids are [former bacteria](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26439354/): the evidence points clearly to an α-proteobacterial ancestry for mitochondria and a cyanobacterial progenitor for the plastid, whose host was already a fully-fledged eukaryote. How often plastids were subsequently transferred between eukaryotic lineages is vigorously debated, and the identity of the host cell that took up the mitochondrial ancestor remains open.

A branching diagram cannot express a merger. Where a eukaryotic genome carries genes of archaeal, α-proteobacterial and — in photosynthetic lineages — cyanobacterial origin, the honest depiction has lines converging as well as splitting. That is why the respiratory machinery has a different ancestry from the rest of the cell that houses it, a division treated in [mitochondria and cellular respiration](/en/biology/cells/mitochondria-and-cellular-respiration) and visible in the organisation described in [what a cell is](/en/biology/cells/what-is-a-cell).

## Where the tree becomes a network

Endosymbiosis is the dramatic case; horizontal gene transfer is the pervasive one. Sharing genetic material between organisms that are not in a parent–offspring relationship is [a recognised mechanism of adaptation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26184597/) in bacteria and archaea, and it also occurs between prokaryotes and eukaryotes and even between multicellular eukaryotes. Antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity are its familiar consequences, but its evolutionary reach is much wider than disease.

Transfer has an awkward consequence for deep phylogeny. If genes move between lineages, different genes in the same genome can have different histories, and the tree recovered depends partly on which genes were chosen. This is the same discordance problem discussed in [reconstructing phylogenies from present-day data](/en/biology/taxonomy/phylogenetics-explained), amplified: at the deepest levels, transfer can be ancient, saturated and hard to detect. It does not abolish the tree — vertical inheritance still dominates for the core translational machinery — but it does mean that the base of the diagram is better read as a tree drawn through a network than as a clean bifurcating history.

## What is genuinely unresolved

Three things remain open, and they are open for different reasons. The rooting of the universal tree is the hardest, because there is no outgroup to life; it must be inferred from ancient gene duplications that predate the last common ancestor, which are few and long. Whether eukaryotes arose from within Asgard archaea or beside them is now a question about model adequacy rather than about data volume — the deep signal is saturated, and the artefacts that afflict long branches bear directly on the answer. And the order of events in eukaryogenesis, particularly whether the mitochondrial endosymbiont arrived early or late relative to other eukaryotic features, is not settled by any current dataset.

Where the field has been able to move, it has done so by importing calibration from geology. A divergence-time analysis of pre-LUCA gene duplicates, calibrated with microbial fossils and isotope records, infers that the last universal common ancestor lived [around 4.2 billion years ago](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11383801/), within a range of 4.09 to 4.33 billion years, with a genome of at least 2.5 megabases encoding roughly 2,600 proteins — an organism of prokaryote grade already embedded in an ecological system rather than alone. That is an inference from present-day sequences plus dated rocks, not an observation, and it carries the uncertainties of both.
