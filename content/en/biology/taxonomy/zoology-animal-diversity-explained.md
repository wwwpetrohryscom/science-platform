Thirty-four animal phyla sit directly under Animalia in the August 2026 release of the Catalogue of Life, and they are wildly unequal in every respect that can be measured. Between them they carry 1,531,146 accepted species names, of which 128,616 are flagged extinct. Arthropods alone account for 1,153,259 of those names and insects for 941,223. Describing animal diversity is, numerically, describing arthropods with a long tail of everything else attached — which makes the tail the interesting part, because that is where the distinct ways of building an animal actually live.

## Where the described animals are, and what that reflects

A phylum is not a measured quantity. It is a rank applied to a lineage judged morphologically distinct at the time it was named, which is why Placozoa with four accepted species and Arthropoda with more than a million carry the same label — the reasoning behind ranks of this kind is set out in the article on [how taxonomic ranks are assigned](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained). With that caveat, the distribution of names across phyla tracks two things at once: how many kinds of organism a lineage really contains, and how much attention taxonomists have paid it.

Two of those columns need care. The extinct-flagged share is not consistent between rows: more than two-fifths of the molluscan and a third of the echinoderm entries are fossil taxa contributed by palaeontological checklists, while the flatworm and nematode rows are effectively all living, because nothing soft-bodied and microscopic fossilises reliably. The catalogue is a union of source datasets with different scopes, not a survey.

The nematode row is the clearest case of effort rather than biology setting the total. Roundworms are among the most abundant animals in almost every soil and sediment on Earth, yet fewer than 20,000 carry accepted names — a total that reflects how few specialists have worked on the group rather than how many kinds of roundworm exist. The general problem of reading a described total as though it estimated a real one is taken up in the companion piece on [the arithmetic of global species estimates](/en/biology/taxonomy/how-many-species-are-there).

## The scheme that molecular data replaced

Until the 1990s, the higher classification of bilaterian animals rested on embryology and body-cavity anatomy. Animals were sorted by whether the blastopore became the mouth or the anus, and then by whether the body cavity was a true coelom, a pseudocoelom, or absent. Segmentation carried heavy weight: annelids and arthropods were united as **Articulata**, on the reasoning that a segmented body was too elaborate to have arisen twice.

Two ribosomal RNA studies took that apart. A 1995 analysis of 18S ribosomal DNA from all three lophophorate phyla — brachiopods, bryozoans and phoronids — found them to be protostomes closely related to molluscs and annelids, overturning a long-standing assignment to the deuterostomes based on their mode of coelom formation. Two years later, an 18S analysis covering the moulting phyla recovered a clade uniting arthropods, tardigrades, onychophorans, nematodes, nematomorphs, kinorhynchs and priapulids, named **Ecdysozoa** on the inference that ecdysis arose once. The same analysis found no support for Articulata.

The consequence was a protostome split into Ecdysozoa and the spiralian assemblage often labelled Lophotrochozoa, with segmentation demoted from a deep phylogenetic signal to a feature that arose more than once. It also relocated the two most-used invertebrate model organisms: *Caenorhabditis elegans* and *Drosophila melanogaster*, long treated as distant points of comparison, turned out to be relatively close relatives, which changes what a shared developmental mechanism between them implies. Segmentation arising more than once is an instance of the general pattern surveyed in the article on [convergent evolution](/en/biology/evolution/convergent-evolution-explained).

## What the Cambrian record does and does not show

The fossil evidence for this reorganisation is concentrated in a narrow interval. Trace fossils attributable to total-group euarthropods appear at around 537 million years ago; trilobites, which are crown-group euarthropods, are in the record by 521 Ma; and the Burgess Shale-type deposits that preserve soft-bodied stem lineages begin around 518 Ma. Running the comparison the other way is what makes the interval informative: latest-Ediacaran deposits of the same preservational type, such as the 550 Ma Miaohe biota, are richly fossiliferous with algae and contain no animals at all, and the 560 Ma Doushantuo phosphates do not supply them either. On that evidence the euarthropod stem lineage is no older than about 550 Ma.

Molecular clocks tell a partly different story, and the honest reading is that they cannot yet be made to agree. A Bayesian analysis using four separate fossil-calibration strategies concluded that crown Metazoa originated in the Cryogenian, with most crown-bilaterian phyla diversifying through the Ediacaran, while also concluding that the ambiguity of ancient fossils and violations of the clock impose a hard limit on achievable precision — precise enough to place metazoan origins before the Cambrian, not precise enough to test whether particular geological events caused particular divergences. What the Cambrian record shows, then, is the appearance of skeletons, burrows and preservable hard parts, which is not the same event as the origin of the lineages that made them.

## The root of the animal tree is still moving

The deepest split in Metazoa remains contested, and it is a genuine dispute rather than a data shortage. The question is whether sponges or comb jellies branched off first, and it matters because comb jellies have nerves and muscles while sponges have neither: one topology implies a single origin of neural tissue, the other implies either an early loss or an independent origin.

Sequence-based analyses have gone both ways depending on model and taxon sampling. A 2023 study took a different kind of character to the problem, using chromosome-scale gene linkage — synteny — with new genomes for a ctenophore, two sponges and three unicellular relatives of animals. Ctenophores and the unicellular outgroups share ancestral chromosomal arrangements, while sponges, cnidarians, placozoans and bilaterians share derived fusion-and-mixing events; because such rearrangements are rare and effectively irreversible, they support placing ctenophores as sister to all other animals.

That is a qualitatively different kind of evidence from a sequence alignment, and it has still not closed the question. Genome-scale sampling of every phylum has shown that support for major spiralian groupings can be constructed in strongly conflicting ways depending on which taxa and which orthologue sets go into the matrix, and that recoding amino acids into six categories — a standard remedy for compositional bias — can itself flip support at the base of the tree. The methods issue here is not incidental; the behaviour of [substitution models and node support measures](/en/biology/taxonomy/phylogenetics-explained) is what the disagreement is actually about, in the same way that model choice governs the [deep divisions between the domains of life](/en/biology/taxonomy/the-tree-of-life-and-domains).

## What did not change

The revision rearranged relationships; it did not dissolve the body plans. An arthropod is still built on a segmented, moulted exoskeleton with jointed appendages, whatever its sister group turns out to be. What it removed was the assumption that shared anatomy is a reliable guide to shared ancestry at this depth. Segmentation, body-cavity type and mode of coelom formation are still real features of real animals; they simply turned out to be poor evidence about who is related to whom.

Two limits are worth carrying away. Sampling remains uneven in a way that maps onto habitat: the phyla with the fewest names are disproportionately small, soft-bodied and marine, and much of the relevant habitat is only now being surveyed systematically, as the article on [deep-sea ecosystems](/en/ecology/oceans/deep-sea-ecosystems) describes. And species-poor phyla are often the hardest to place, because a lineage with few surviving relatives offers few intermediate branches to break up the long ones that mislead phylogenetic inference. The parts of the animal tree we know least about tend, for that reason, to be the parts hardest to fix in position.
