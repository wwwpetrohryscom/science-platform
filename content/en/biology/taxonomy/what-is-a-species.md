Two researchers can examine the same populations, share every sequence and specimen, and still publish incompatible answers about how many kinds of organism they are looking at. The disagreement is almost never about the data. It is about which property counts as decisive, and biology supplies several candidates that do not pick out the same boundaries. Everything downstream — the naming machinery described in [the rules that govern scientific names](/en/biology/taxonomy/taxonomy-and-classification-explained), the counts, the legal protections — inherits that unresolved choice.

## Five criteria, five sets of boundaries

The concepts in circulation are not rival theories of one phenomenon so much as different answers to the question of what makes a lineage distinct.

| Criterion | Treated as decisive | Typically applied through | Characteristic failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reproductive isolation | inability to interbreed successfully | crossing data, hybrid zones | asexual lineages; freely hybridising groups |
| Morphological distinctness | diagnosable difference in form | specimens, measurements | cryptic lineages; strong within-group variation |
| Phylogenetic exclusivity | monophyly or a diagnostic character | gene and genome trees | where to cut the tree remains a choice |
| Ecological role | occupation of a distinct niche | habitat and resource data | niches are hard to measure and shift |
| Genotypic clustering | discontinuity where forms co-occur | population genomic data | needs sympatry; structure is not always a boundary |

Each column of failures is instructive. Reproductive isolation is the criterion most people are taught, and it is untestable for the majority of life: it cannot be applied to organisms that do not outcross, and for geographically separated populations it can only be guessed at. Morphology is cheap and can be applied to museum material and fossils, but it separates only what happens to differ visibly. Phylogenetic criteria are precise about ancestry yet silent about where along a branch the cut belongs, a point that connects directly to the reconstruction methods discussed in [inferring evolutionary trees](/en/biology/taxonomy/phylogenetics-explained).

## The organisms that break the reproductive test

Hybridisation is not the marginal phenomenon the textbook version implies. A survey of natural interspecific crossing found that [at least 25 per cent of plant species and 10 per cent of animal species](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16701374/) are involved in hybridisation and potential introgression with another, mostly among younger lineages, and that groups in nature are frequently incompletely isolated for millions of years after they begin diverging. Gene flow does not stop when a boundary is drawn.

Prokaryotes are the harder case, because they do not outcross at all in the sexual sense and exchange genes horizontally instead. That should predict a smooth genetic continuum, and it does not. Comparing whole-genome average nucleotide identity across roughly 90,000 genomes, [99.8 per cent of eight billion pairwise comparisons](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6269478/) fell either above 95 per cent identity, within a group, or below 83 per cent, between groups, with the intermediate zone conspicuously sparse. The discontinuity is real even though the mechanism that generates it in animals is absent, which is a useful corrective: reproductive isolation is one way to produce distinct clusters, not the definition of one. How microbial classification handles this is taken up in the article on [bacteria and archaea](/en/biology/microbiology/bacteria-and-archaea-explained).

## Divergence is a process, and it is usually caught mid-way

The clearest illustration comes from the case long taught as the textbook demonstration. In the greenish warbler complex, two reproductively isolated forms meet in central Siberia, connected by a chain of populations running south around the Tibetan Plateau — the classic ring. Genome-wide analysis found that although the present-day pattern mostly matches expectation, [historical breaks in gene flow](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24870239/) occurred at more than one point around the ring, the two Siberian forms have occasionally interbred, and an extensive hybrid zone sits in the western Himalayas. The authors read this as casting doubt on the complex as a clean example of divergence purely by distance, while confirming something more useful: a continuum running from barely distinguishable neighbouring populations to almost complete reproductive isolation.

If divergence is a process with duration, then asking whether two populations "are" separate is asking for a verdict the process has not yet delivered. The mechanisms that drive it are covered separately in [how new lineages form](/en/biology/evolution/speciation-mechanisms).

## Separating the definition from the evidence

The most useful move in the modern literature was to stop treating the criteria as competing definitions. The unified account defines a species as [a separately evolving metapopulation lineage](https://academic.oup.com/sysbio/article/56/6/879/1653163) and nothing more, and demotes reproductive isolation, diagnosability, monophyly and ecological distinctness to operational criteria — lines of evidence that a lineage has separated. On this reading, the historical disputes were not about the nature of species at all; they were arguments about which evidence is sufficient, conducted in the vocabulary of definition.

This reframing explains an otherwise odd feature of taxonomic practice: working taxonomists rarely invoke a concept by name. They assemble morphological, genetic, ecological and geographic evidence, ask whether it converges, and publish a decision that is explicitly a hypothesis. Integrative practice is not a compromise between concepts; it is what the unified account recommends.

## When the software answers a different question

Genomic delimitation methods promised to remove the judgement, and they moved it instead. Simulation work modelling divergence as an extended process rather than an instantaneous event showed that the multispecies coalescent, which underlies many delimitation programs, [diagnoses genetic structure rather than species boundaries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5320999/) and cannot statistically distinguish population isolation from a boundary between lineages. Because genomic data detect ever finer structure, the failure mode is systematic and directional: it inflates counts. The authors' recommendation is that a genome-based delimitation should be treated as a hypothesis requiring validation from phenotypic and ecological evidence, not as a result.

## What this does to a conservation count

The consequences are measurable. Mammals are the best-catalogued class, and the second release of the Mammal Diversity Database recognised [6,759 living and recently extinct species](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12526941/), 24.8 per cent more than the standard 2005 compendium and 68.8 per cent more than the totals of 1980 — a net gain of 2,754, from 3,149 splits and new descriptions against 395 lumps, at roughly 65 per year. The character of description has shifted too: 83.4 per cent of available names coined since 2000 were published as species rather than subspecies, against 36.1 per cent between 1950 and 1999. Most of that growth is reclassification, not discovery, which is the pattern named [taxonomic inflation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16701308/) two decades ago — with the warning that ecologists and conservation biologists treat lists as stable measures when they are nothing of the kind.

The mismatch is visible where taxonomy meets policy. Version 2024-1 of the IUCN Red List recognised 5,983 mammal species; 5,799 of those are shared with the database, while 184 are treated there as synonyms of something else. Neither list is wrong. They apply different thresholds to the same populations, and an organism's protected status can depend on which threshold a given instrument adopted. The database also records that 25.5 per cent of the mammals it accepts are either Data Deficient or never evaluated — the taxonomy has run ahead of the assessment capacity that is supposed to act on it.

Read this way, a species total is a summary of taxonomic practice as much as of biology, which is why the estimates in [how many species exist](/en/biology/taxonomy/how-many-species-are-there) vary by method, and why counts should not be compared across sources without checking whose criteria produced them. The consequences for prioritisation are examined in [why species counts mislead conservation](/en/ecology/biodiversity/why-species-counts-mislead-conservation).
